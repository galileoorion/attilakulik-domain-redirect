Chapter 11 · The Middle

6. Diagnostic Tests

Continue in Reference

The eight tests are for revision, and they need a summary of the middle to work on: one line per sequence, in order, with its position in the book as a percentage. Two of them are procedures rather than questions. The value-shift log writes, for each sequence of the middle, what is true at its start and no longer true at its close, in two parallel sentences as The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) does for a single scene, with the stakes in a word. The option list, defined in The Mechanism, records the protagonist’s open moves at three points. Tests 1 to 3 (Value-shift log, Option list and Reorder) read the sequences, tests 4 to 6 (Midpoint, Opposition and Clock) the forces acting on them, and tests 7 (Cut) and 8 (Question) what the middle does to the reader. Record a sentence, not a verdict.

Test 1 — Value-shift log

Do neighbouring sequences repeat both the shift and the stakes?

Failure. The plateau: The Purposes of a Scene’s redundancy test (Chapter 1) failed at the scale of the book.

Exception. The Old Man and the Sea (1952), whose middle repeats one struggle for days and raises its price only by exhaustion.

Test 2 — Option list

Does the list shrink between the end of the beginning, the midpoint and the start of the ending, and does each shrinkage have a cause on the page?

Failure. The middle has not narrowed, and the ending will read as one choice among several.

Exception. Mrs Dalloway (1925), a single day in which little closes and the approach of the party supplies the pressure.

Test 3 — Reorder

Swap two sequences in the summary: does anything break?

Failure. Episodic: the sequences are joined by a constant, not by cause.

Exception. Three Men in a Boat (1889), whose river episodes could change places and whose digressions are the point.

Test 4 — Midpoint

What is different near the centre in the protagonist’s goal, knowledge, mode or fortune?

Failure. No turn: the second half pursues the first half’s question by the first half’s means.

Exception. W. G. Sebald’s The Rings of Saturn (1995), translated by Michael Hulse (1998), a walk with no turn, held by association.

Test 5 — Opposition

In each quarter of the middle, does the opposing force act, on the page or through a consequence the protagonist feels?

Failure. The absent opposition.

Exception. Robinson Crusoe (1719), whose opposition for most of its length is weather, illness and solitude rather than an agent.

Test 6 — Clock

Is there a deadline or limit, does the protagonist know it, and does the time left shorten visibly?

Failure. A middle without felt time, in which any sequence could take a week or a year.

Exception. The Magic Mountain, which dissolves its clock on purpose and makes the loss of time its subject.

Test 7 — Cut

Delete the weakest sequence of the middle and reread the ending: what has the ending lost?

Failure. Nothing: the sequence was transit.

Exception. Groundhog Day (1993), whose repeated wake-ups would fail this test one by one and together carry the premise.

Test 8 — Question

What question is the reader carrying at 40 per cent and at 60 per cent?

Failure. The same question: the delays have repeated it instead of changing it.

Exception. The classic detective novel, The Moonstone (1868) among them, whose question stays who took it? while its answers narrow.

Reading the results together

Failures cluster, and the cluster says more than any single result. Tests 1 (Value-shift log) and 3 (Reorder) failing together mean that the sequences are joined neither by cause nor by change, and the remedy is to link them, so that each sequence’s outcome produces the next, before cutting any. Test 2 (Option list) failing while test 4 (Midpoint) passes means a turn that changes what the protagonist knows without closing any move, and the second half needs consequences of the turn, not more revelation. Tests 5 (Opposition) and 6 (Clock) failing together usually mean that the opposition has no schedule: give the opposing force a deadline of its own and let the protagonist learn of it, as the household revision does with Dov’s buyer. Test 7 (Cut) failing on several sequences means the middle is longer than its material, and the remedy is to cut rather than to intensify. Test 8 (Question) passing while test 2 (Option list) fails describes a middle of snares, in which the question keeps changing and nothing closes, which readers experience as being led about.

The hardest result is a clean pass on every test and a middle that still reads slowly. The fault is then probably not structural: sentence rhythm, the proportion of scene to summary, or a register held too long, which The Purposes of a Scene’s register test (Chapter 1) and its reverse outline are built to find.
