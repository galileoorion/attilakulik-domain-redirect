Chapter 11 · The Middle

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below fails through a mechanism rather than a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent. The full treatment has a longer catalogue, among them the training-montage middle, the spectacle that turns nothing, the reset at the end of every sequence, the research middle and the second sag after a strong midpoint.

The plateau

Symptom. Consecutive sequences end where they began. A summary of the middle repeats its verbs: asks, is refused, asks again; waits, refuses, waits. Readers report that nothing happens, though the writer can list a great deal that does.

Mechanism. The plateau repeats one value shift at constant stakes, so the option list never changes and the reader’s sense of where the book might end never narrows. It is the opposite twin of Scene and Sequel’s escalation treadmill (Chapter 2): the treadmill changes the size of what is at risk and nothing else, the plateau changes nothing. Writers produce it when a sequence is written to keep a situation present, to remind the reader of a question rather than to move it.

Remedy. Tests 1 (Value-shift log) and 2 (Option list). Keep the strongest instance of each repeated shift and cut the others, or make each repetition close something the last left open: the third refusal should cost the protagonist a move the first did not.

Exception. Samuel Beckett’s Waiting for Godot, first staged in French in 1953 and in Beckett’s own English in 1955, which Vivian Mercier described in The Irish Times in 1956 as a play in which “nothing happens, twice”. Its second act repeats the first: the same road, the same waiting, a boy with the same message. It works because the repetition is the argument and because it degrades: Pozzo returns blind and Lucky dumb, and the price rises behind a foreground that stands still. The fault is a repetition the book does not mean.

The episodic middle

Symptom. The middle’s sequences could change places. Each is a self-contained adventure, and the middle can be summarised only with and then.

Mechanism. The sequences are linked by a constant, a road or a list or a hero, and not by cause, so no sequence closes anything for the next. Aristotle’s objection is practical as well as formal: a reader forms expectations about what comes next from what has just happened, and in an episodic middle nothing has just happened that predicts anything.

Remedy. Test 3 (Reorder). Carry an outcome from one sequence into the next, a wound, a debt, an enemy, a piece of knowledge, or impose a rule or a limit that removes a possibility at each station.

Exception. Cervantes’s Don Quixote (1605 and 1615), translated by Edith Grossman (2003). Many of the first part’s adventures, the windmills, the flocks of sheep, the galley slaves, could be reordered with little loss. It works because the constant is itself an engine, the knight’s reading of the world through his romances tested afresh at each station, and because the second part supplies the cause the first lacked: the people Don Quixote meets have read the first part, and a duke and duchess stage adventures for him, so that the world narrows around his delusion.

The absent opposition

Symptom. The opposing force, a person or an institution or a natural power, disappears for long stretches while the protagonist prepares, trains, researches or travels. The middle feels safe.

Mechanism. It is usually the opposition that closes doors. Without it acting, the protagonist’s moves have no price and the option list cannot shrink, so the middle becomes the protagonist’s private project, observed. The absent opposition is common in middles built as preparation, where the writer is saving the antagonist for the end.

Remedy. Test 5 (Opposition). Give the opposition a chain of its own, running off the page, and let it surface in each quarter of the middle as a consequence the protagonist feels, which is the pinch of the screenwriting manuals.

Exception. Herman Melville’s Moby-Dick (1851). The whale is not seen until chapter 133 of 135. It works because the opposition acts through consequence throughout: Ahab’s leg, and the nine gams, the Pequod’s meetings with other ships, several of which bring news of the whale. The Samuel Enderby’s captain has lost an arm to it; the Rachel is searching for a boat lost in chasing it, and her captain’s plea for help, which Ahab refuses, closes the last move by which he might have turned back. The gams are pinches in the later manuals’ sense, reminders of the opposing force, placed from chapter 52 onwards, and at each one Ahab asks the same question of a stranger.

The holding pattern

Symptom. The middle waits for an event scheduled at its end, a trial, a wedding, a departure, a contest, and its sequences fill the time. The protagonist’s situation is the same at each.

Mechanism. In a holding pattern the scheduled event carries all the pressure, and nothing in the waiting can change it, so nothing in the waiting can narrow anything. The reader skims towards the date.

Remedy. Tests 6 (Clock) and 7 (Cut). Make the waiting change the event: what the protagonist does while waiting should alter how they will meet it, or bring it forward. Otherwise cut the wait.

Exception. Donna Tartt’s The Goldfinch (2013). After his mother’s death Theo Decker is taken to Las Vegas by his father, and spends a long stretch in a half-built suburb with his friend Boris, drinking and taking drugs, while the painting he carried out of the museum stays hidden. The book’s length drew what a Vanity Fair article in 2014 called some of the severest pans in memory, and Meg Wolitzer, reviewing it for NPR, praised its “rich, dense, leisurely middle”. The holding pattern works because it is where the adult of the book’s later parts is formed, and because the waiting conceals the book’s central plant: Boris exchanged the painting in Las Vegas, and the package Theo has guarded for years turns out to hold a school textbook.

The broken back

Symptom. The midpoint is so large that the second half reads as a different book, with a new protagonist, a new genre or a new question, and readers who were held by the first half feel abandoned.

Mechanism. The turn changes goal, knowledge and protagonist together and leaves the first half’s question open, so the reader carries two questions and the book answers one. The broken back is the turned middle’s characteristic failure.

Remedy. Tests 4 (Midpoint) and 8 (Question). Close the first half’s question visibly at the turn, or carry something across it, a character, an object, the old question in a new form, so that the second half reads as the first half’s consequence.

Exception. Alfred Hitchcock’s Psycho (1960). Marion Crane, the film’s star and its protagonist, has stolen $40,000, and she is killed about 47 minutes into a film of 109, 47 ÷ 109 or about 43 per cent of the way through. The second half belongs to Norman Bates and to the people looking for her. It works because the film closes the first half’s question on screen: cleaning up, Norman puts the newspaper with the money in it into the boot of her car without knowing what it holds, and sinks the car in a swamp, so that the viewer watches the theft go down with it. Hitchcock protected the break by refusing late admission to screenings.
