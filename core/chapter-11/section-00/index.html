Chapter 11 · The Middle

The Middle

Continue in Reference

Where a book’s possibilities are narrowed

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 11. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

In brief. The middle is the long stretch between the commitment that ends a novel’s beginning and the movement that begins its ending, usually half the book or more, and it is where drafts most often lose their readers. A middle seldom sags because too little happens. It sags because what happens leaves the protagonist’s options, and the price of each, where they were, so that the same kind of setback arrives at the same stakes again. The chapter’s tools are the option list, which records what the protagonist could still do at the end of the beginning, at the midpoint and at the start of the ending, and the value-shift log, which finds the sequences that repeat each other. Use the chapter when readers say a book drags in the middle, when its middle sequences could be shuffled without loss, or when its ending feels like one choice among several.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Say where a middle begins and ends, and find the turns inside it.
  2. Distinguish a complication from an escalation, and a delay that changes the reader’s question from one that repeats it.
  3. Name the shape of a middle and the engine that carries its pressure.
  4. Use the option list to see whether the middle narrows the book towards its ending.
  5. Diagnose a sagging middle and choose a remedy that changes the terms rather than adding incident.

Key terms

Middle; Act Two; complication and escalation; narrowing and price; the option list; the value-shift log; the midpoint; false victory and false defeat; the mirror moment; pinch; the hermeneutic code and its delays; the dilatory space; short circuit; the clock; the six shapes of a middle (escalating chain, turned middle, stations middle, braided middle, accumulating middle, contained middle); the plateau; the episodic middle; the absent opposition; the holding pattern; the broken back. Each is defined at first use and collected in the consolidated glossary.

Contents

The first files say what the middle is and why it is hard to write, the central files how a middle generates pressure and how it fails to, and the last are for use at the desk.

  1. The Problem
    A household middle in which a great deal happens and nothing moves, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The middle, its boundaries and turns, and the terms around it
  3. How It Works
    Why a middle exists at all, why readers tolerate delay, where the conventions put the turns, and what the evidence says
  4. The Taxonomy
    Six shapes of middle, each named for the engine that carries its pressure
  5. The Mechanism
    Narrowing and price, the kinds of midpoint, and the proportions of a middle
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Eight tests on one page, and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    Pride and Prejudice, A Gentleman in Moscow, The Midnight Library and Groundhog Day, and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the model assumes, and why the middle is where a book is decided

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests; a writer planning a book should read the mechanism first, because the option list is easier to build before the middle is drafted than to impose on it afterwards.

The full treatment

This is a new chapter, so the Reference layer holds a single full treatment rather than a corrected original: the theory at greater length, with Barthes’s delays set out one by one and the screenwriting conventions compared in a table, an extended catalogue of shapes and failure patterns, further analyses (Catching Fire, The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo, the second stage of Great Expectations and Squid Game), the household middle in full and the sources.

See also: The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1), whose value shift and redundancy test this chapter applies at the scale of the book; Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), whose escalation treadmill is the plateau’s opposite failure; The Subplot (Chapter 14), for the braided middle; The Ending (Chapter 16), which inherits what the middle has or has not narrowed; Arc and Theme (Chapter 12), for the change in the protagonist that a middle usually carries.
