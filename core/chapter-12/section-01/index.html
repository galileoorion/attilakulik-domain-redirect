Chapter 12 · Arc and Theme

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

A draft of the household novel ends with two paragraphs that its writer added late, after an early reader said that Elena seemed to finish the book as she began it. By this point the reader knows the situation. Four months after their father’s death, three adult children have spent the book reconstructing what he left: Elena, who learned in Dov’s doorway that their mother had changed the shop’s locks in March and has wanted ever since to know why; Nadia, who counts things and has learned not to touch the coat on its hook in the flat above the shop; and Dov, who gave Elena the new key and kept the reason, and who has been keeping the ledger in a looser hand since the evening of 9 March. The three have just read the ledger together, and found in their father’s careful hand the dates of an illness he told nobody about. The draft then continues:

Elena sat at the register with her hands flat on the counter and understood, all at once, what the four months had been for. She had tried to find everything out alone, the way their father had kept everything alone, and it had nearly cost her the only people left who could tell her anything. Some things, she saw now, a family could only know together.

“He was protecting us,” Nadia said. “That’s what this family does. We protect each other by not saying.”

The early reader’s second note was shorter than the first: Now I’m being told what to think. Both notes are accurate, and they describe one fault. The first paragraph gives Elena an arc, a change in a character across the book, by reporting it: she understood, she saw now, and the narration supplies the content of the understanding. The second gives the book a theme, the question its events are about, by having a character state an answer to it. Neither is a decision. Elena does nothing in the scene that she would not have done in the first chapter, and the reader has no way to test her new understanding except by taking the narration’s word for it. Nadia’s line closes a question the book has spent its whole length keeping open, and closes it with a proposition the reader may reject, since the mother’s silence, which is the household’s largest withholding, has not been shown to protect anyone.

The writer’s instinct, faced with the first note, was to explain the change more fully, and the instinct is common enough to be the subject of a failure pattern in this chapter, the announced arc. More explanation leaves the cause untouched. Elena has made the same kind of decision throughout the book: in the doorway she asks once and is refused; in her truck afterwards she decides not to telephone her mother; at the register drawer she reads the ledger alone, from the beginning; at the empty drawer, about 38 per cent of the way through, she decides not to ask Dov who took it, because she had asked him once. The rule behind every one of these choices is the same, find out for yourself, and do not ask people who will not tell you, and nothing in the book shows her choosing by any other rule. A paragraph of understanding at the end cannot make the reader believe in a change that no choice has shown.

The chapter’s claim is that arc and theme are one structure, and that they fail together.

An arc is a change in the rule by which a character chooses, and it exists only at a decision that costs something the old rule valued; a theme is the question such decisions answer, argued by setting one character’s choice beside the others’ rather than by saying what it means. A change that is announced, or a meaning that is spoken, is an arc or a theme in description only.

The claim has a consequence for revision that runs against instinct. The remedy for an unconvincing change is not a better account of it but an earlier price and a later decision: a scene in which the old rule costs the character something she can see, and a moment, under pressure, at which she chooses by a new one. The remedy for a stated theme is to delete the statement and make sure the question is answered differently by different people, so that the ending’s arrangement does the arguing. The revision in Worked Analyses deletes both of the draft’s paragraphs and adds two short passages of decision, about two hundred words in all, one in the middle of the book and one at the convergence before the reading.

The claim also explains why craft teaching often separates the two subjects and why the separation causes trouble in revision. A writer who treats arc as a matter of character tends to repair it with backstory, a wound that explains the old rule; a writer who treats theme as a matter of meaning tends to repair it with images and statements. Neither repair touches the decisions, which is where both are made. This is a judgement from editorial practice rather than a measured finding, but the household draft illustrates it: its writer had already given Elena a reason to distrust people who withhold, and had already given the book a recurring object, the ledger, as a carrier of its meaning, and still the ending told rather than showed.

See also: the full treatment, on the relation between this chapter and the character and theme domains of the ending; The Ending (Chapter 16), whose revision case records that Elena “completes by presence rather than act”.
