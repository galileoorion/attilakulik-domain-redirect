Introduction

1. The Book's Argument

Continue in Reference

Units and their work

A finished novel is read as a continuous experience, and most of the advice a writer receives treats it that way, as a matter of pace, voice or tension felt across the whole. Revision cannot work on a whole. It works on parts: a scene cut, a chapter moved, a viewpoint withdrawn, a reveal brought forward. This book starts from that fact. It divides the novel into the units a writer can act on, and asks of each one the same question, which the first chapter, The Purposes of a Scene, puts to the scene: what is it for, and how can a writer tell whether it is doing it?

The answer the book gives for every unit has the same shape. A unit is a stretch of text, or a decision about the arrangement of text, that a writer can add, cut, move or change without rewriting everything else: a scene, a passage of dialogue, a detail, a transition, a chapter break, a cutaway to the past, a strand in another time, an opening, a prologue, a middle, an arc, a viewpoint, a subplot, a reveal, an ending, a genre’s promise. Each unit costs the reader something, in attention, orientation or patience, and each must repay the cost by doing work the reader would miss if the unit were gone. The work can be done in several registers at once, which the chapters call domains, and a unit that does two or three jobs with one piece of material earns its place more cheaply than one that does a single job alone. The chapters call that economy multiplicity, and it recurs more often than any other principle in the book: the first chapter, The Purposes of a Scene, introduces it for the scene, and it appears in thirteen of the eighteen chapters.

A unit of a novel earns its place by changing something the reader would miss, and it is diagnosed by asking what it costs, what it does, and whether another unit could do the same work more cheaply.

That claim is the book’s governing argument. Each chapter restates it for its own unit, and each one gives it a practical form: a test a writer can run on a draft, and a table or chart on which the test is run.

The four parts

The parts follow the scale of the units, from the smallest to the largest and then outward.

Part I, The Scene and Its Materials, treats the scene and what it is made of. The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) defines the scene by the change it makes (the value shift, a charged value moving from one state towards its opposite) and introduces the domains and multiplicity. Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2) divides the unit in two, the scene and the reflective sequel in which a character absorbs a setback and decides, and shows how a decision becomes the next scene’s goal, so that events read as having had to happen next. The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) treats the passage of talk, whose work happens largely in what speakers imply, and The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4) the detail, whose work begins with the choice of one true thing out of many.

Part II, The Joins, treats what lies between units. The Transition (Chapter 5) treats the writing on either side of a gap in time, place or viewpoint, and prices it by how many things the gap changes. The Chapter Break (Chapter 6) treats the point at which a reader may stop, and the price the last sentence before it sets on stopping. Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7) treats the passage that leaves the present for the past, and Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) the book that is arranged in more than one time, so that the order of telling becomes its main instrument.

Part III, The Architecture of the Whole, treats the decisions that shape a book entire. It begins at the book’s first boundary, with The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) and The Prologue (Chapter 10), passes through The Middle (Chapter 11), Arc and Theme (Chapter 12), The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), The Subplot (Chapter 14) and The Reveal (Chapter 15), and ends at the second boundary, The Ending (Chapter 16). The opening and the ending are inverses. The opening works with no accumulated context and must win trust from nothing; the ending works with nothing but accumulated context, and may spend only what the book has deposited.

Part IV, Beyond the Book and Into Practice, steps outside the single book in two directions. Series and Genre (Chapter 17) treats the promises a reader brings to a book from its genre and carries out of it into a series. The Structural Edit (Chapter 18) treats the revision of a whole draft, and puts every earlier chapter’s instruments in the order in which a revision should use them: first what the book promises, then whose book it is and how its times and threads are arranged, then its shape, then its joins, and its scenes and their materials last.

Several chapters also divide one problem between them, and a writer who knows the division can tell which chapter a problem belongs to. Three chapters divide the gap: The Transition (Chapter 5) owns how a gap is crossed, The Chapter Break (Chapter 6) where it is placed, and The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13) who stands on the other side of it. Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7) and Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) divide the past: a passage of past material that recurs without a question of its own is a cutaway, judged by the first, and one that recurs in a declared pattern and runs on its own question is a strand, judged by the second. The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13) and The Subplot (Chapter 14) divide the reader’s attention, the first by minds, the second by threads, and both treat attention as a budget that every addition draws on. The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) and The Ending (Chapter 16) divide trust, minted at one boundary and spent at the other, and The Reveal (Chapter 15) and The Ending divide what the reader learns late, the reveal changing what the earlier pages meant, the ending changing what the whole book meant.

The models are heuristics

Every model in the book is a heuristic, a rule of thumb for noticing, and none is a law. The evidence is in the chapters themselves, each of which pairs every failure it describes with a published work that commits the failure and succeeds. Pride and Prejudice opens on a general statement rather than a character; Anna Karenina runs for the length of a long novel’s part after its heroine’s death; Middlemarch has no subplot in the sense The Subplot (Chapter 14) defines, because it has four plots. A model that ruled these books out would be reporting its own limits, and the chapters say so where it happens.

What the instruments do reliably is turn a vague dissatisfaction into a question that can be answered. This isn’t working becomes the voice contract is undischarged, or this thread’s turning points are satellites, not kernels, or the middle’s setbacks leave the protagonist’s options where they were. A writer who can name the question can decide whether the book should answer it or whether the book has a reason to break the rule, and the second decision is the one the counterexamples are there to teach.
