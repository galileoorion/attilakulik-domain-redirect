Chapter 3 · The Dialogue Exchange

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings show different parts of the instrument: the participation framework in a Victorian novel, the two channels held apart for a whole book and closed by one party, and a post-2000 exchange that does without quotation marks and tags altogether. A short screen reading and the household revision case follow.

Wuthering Heights: who is in the room

Emily Brontë’s Wuthering Heights (1847) often reaches the reader at two removes. Much of it is Nelly Dean’s account to Lockwood, a tenant who knows little of the household, so many exchanges are reported by a narrator with her own loyalties. A central exchange is Catherine’s confession to Nelly in the kitchen that she has accepted Edgar Linton. Heathcliff is in the room, out of Catherine’s sight. He listens until she says that it would degrade her to marry him, and then leaves, so he does not hear her say that she is Heathcliff. Nelly notices his departure and tells Catherine to hush, but does not plainly tell her what he heard.

Read with the taxonomy, the exchange’s plot work is done almost entirely in the silence domain. Two silences, a departure and a withholding, set the course of the rest of the book, and neither is speech. The participation framework explains why the scene cannot be rewritten as a two-hander: Catherine’s speech is designed for one hearer and received by two, and the overhearer takes away the half that wounds. Nelly’s silence is a narrator’s silence as well as a character’s, since the woman who chose not to tell Catherine is the woman now choosing what to tell Lockwood, and the reader is left to weigh how far her account is shaped by the same reticence. The exchange fails test 2 (Want) on a strict reading, because Catherine wants nothing from Nelly but a hearing, and the failure is the point: a confession is a speech looking for a witness, and the witness the speech finds is the wrong one.

The Remains of the Day: the gap kept open

Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day (1989) is narrated by Stevens, a butler whose professional register is a status instrument: its formality lets him decline every intimacy Miss Kenton offers without refusing one. The relational value that moves across the novel is not power, since his position is secure, but permission. What Miss Kenton may say to him narrows from scene to scene, through evasions of impeccable length and no content, ritual formulas where answers were owed, and his habit of dating and placing his recollections with a precision that tells the reader what he has dwelt on. Her announcement of her engagement is made in order to be answered, and his failure to answer is the event.

The novel’s end turns the two channels into its structure. Twenty years later, waiting with him at a bus stop in the rain, Miss Kenton, now Mrs Benn, closes her gap: on desolate occasions, she says, she thinks of a life she might have had with him. The narration then closes Stevens’s gap for the reader alone, in the sentence in which he admits that at that moment his heart was breaking, while what he says aloud is a courtesy that returns her to her marriage. The configuration is the reader-only one, held for two hundred pages and confirmed at the last moment, and the gap-closing move is made by one party while the other keeps his open. A writer borrowing the register should note its cost: a character who evades at this volume risks becoming unreadable rather than opaque, and Ishiguro avoids it by varying the kind of evasion from scene to scene.

The Road: an exchange without apparatus

Cormac McCarthy’s The Road (2006) follows an unnamed man and his son south through a burned America. Its dialogue has no quotation marks, almost no tags and few apostrophes, and is set out one turn to a line. In one exchange the boy asks whether they would ever eat anybody, and the father says no. Then:

Even if we were starving?
We’re starving now.
You said we werent.
I said we werent dying. I didnt say we werent starving.

The boy then asks the father to confirm the formula they live by, that they are the good guys and are carrying the fire, the father repeats it after him, and the boy ends the exchange with Okay.

The transcript test is built into the page, since there is nothing to strip, and it passes on structure alone: the speakers differ by their relation to questions, the boy asking and the father answering. The status work is done by repair. You said we werent is an other-correction, a rare and pointed move in talk because speakers prefer to correct themselves, made by a child against the father who is his only authority, and the father’s too-precise distinction between dying and starving is the answer of a man caught out. The formula that follows is a ritual one, and its meaning depends on who demands it. Here the boy holds the right to ask, makes the father recite, and decides when the exchange is over. The plot does not move. What moves is the relation: the child has become the keeper of his father’s ethics, which is the novel’s subject stated in nothing but turns.

A screen reading: Succession, “Hunting”

In “Hunting”, the third episode of the second series of Succession (HBO, first broadcast 25 August 2019; written by Tony Roche, directed by Andrij Parekh), Logan Roy, at a hunting lodge in Hungary and convinced that someone close to him has been talking to an outsider, interrupts dinner and makes Tom, Greg and Karl get down on the floor as boars and fight for sausages while the table watches and chants. The scene is a participation framework turned into an instrument. Logan holds the floor and the sole right to ask; the three men on the floor are compelled to perform; and the rest of the table, his children among them, are overhearers who must laugh to show they are not suspects. Nobody can refuse, because refusal is what the game exists to detect. Logan says little, and his short commands against his children’s jokes show the repertoire principle at work: the joke is a way of keeping face, and in this room only he may do without one. Before the night is out, Kendall has given Roman up, and the humiliation moves to his brother. On screen the narrator’s resources are gone, so the camera and the actors’ faces carry the subtext a novel would give to interiority or a contradicting action beat.

The household revision case: the doorway

Elena has driven across town to Dov’s front door. She does not yet know that their mother changed the shop’s locks in March. The first draft reads as follows.

Dov stood in the doorway. “I know you want the shop key,” he said. “I’ve been dreading this conversation.”

“I’ve had a key to this shop since I was twelve,” Elena said. “It’s humiliating to be offered one by you.”

“It won’t fit any more. Ma changed the locks in March, right after the funeral. I’ve felt caught in the middle ever since — I don’t want to betray her, but I don’t want to lie to you either.”

“Why would she do that? Doesn’t she trust me?”

“She said you’d ask that. She knew you’d take it personally, and I do think she’s right that you take things personally. But she asked me not to explain, and I feel I have to respect that, even though I know it’s hurting you.” He stepped back from the door. “I’m sorry. I really am.”

The tests find the faults quickly. Test 1 (Removal) fails, since Dov gave her the key and would not say why the locks had been changed carries everything the exchange carries. Test 2 (Want) passes only on the surface, because both wants are stated, which removes the reader’s work. Test 3 (Status) fails: Dov concedes the key, the fact, his own divided position and an apology, and nothing is contested because nothing is withheld. Tests 4 (Cost), 5 (Silence) and 6 (Implicature) fail together, since every disclosure is volunteered, neither speaker keeps anything back, and the exchange supplies its own interpretation. Test 7 (Transcript) fails, because without tags the two are one person, fluent in the same emotionally literate register. The patterns present are on the nose, with its therapeutic variant (caught in the middle, take things personally), a touch of as you know (right after the funeral, which Elena knows) and the ventriloquist exchange. The draft holds all the necessary information and is not badly written; it runs on one channel, and it is more emotionally explicit than the revision while conveying less emotion.

“You’ll want the shop key,” Dov said, not moving from the doorway.

Elena held out her hand. “I’ve had a key since I was twelve.”

“Not this key.” He turned it over once between two fingers before he set it in her palm, face down, like he was folding a hand of cards. “Ma changed the locks in March.”

“Did she.” Elena closed her fist around it. “Did she say why.”

“She said you’d ask that.” Dov stepped back into the hall. “She didn’t say I had to answer.”

The revision converts every statement of feeling into an act: I’ve been dreading this conversation becomes not moving from the doorway, and Elena’s humiliation becomes a hand held out while she asserts her old right. The fact arrives inside a correction, Not this key, so that Elena is contradicted before she is informed, which makes the information cost something. The turned key is a contradicting action beat: a man simply conceding does not handle the concession so carefully. Elena’s Did she is a significant repetition, and the missing question marks mark questions that are not requests for information, because she has already drawn a conclusion. Dov’s last line is a third-person deflection, the same refusal as the draft’s with responsibility shifted to the absent mother; he is speaking someone else’s instruction, which shields him and also discloses a man who needs the instruction to be someone else’s. The registers are differentiated by two variables only, Dov’s complete declaratives against Elena’s two- and three-word turns. The relational shift is real and partial. Dov opens holding the doorway and the key; he gives up the key, keeps the reason, and ends the conversation by withdrawing into his own hall. The plot is served by a fact with a consequence Elena has not yet worked out, that she has been shut out for four months without knowing it, and the silence domain by Dov’s refusal and by the absence of the mother from a conversation entirely about her.

Three things remain unsolved. The revision is conspicuously written: the card simile and the missing question marks are visible technique, and it passes the transcript test against its own draft more easily than against the shelf it will sit on. It leans on two mechanisms, deflection and repetition, in three of its five turns, and Dov is at risk of becoming a man who only ever declines. And its theme, what family members owe each other in the way of truth, is available to a reader who has the rest of the book and thin in the scene itself. Against that, it does one thing the draft could not. Dov’s reason is now a withheld object with a place in the book, which a later exchange can close, and the draft spent it.
