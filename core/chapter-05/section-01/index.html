Chapter 5 · The Transition

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

The household novel that runs through this book has reached a join. Elena has come away from her brother’s front door with a new key to the family shop and without the reason their mother changed its locks; she has sat in her truck, thought of telephoning their mother, and decided against it. Her sequel ends on a sentence that names where she is going and what she will do there: She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K. Then comes white space, and the draft’s next paragraph begins:

Three days later, Elena drove to the shop. It was a Thursday, and the traffic on the ring road was worse than she had expected, so it was nearly four by the time she parked outside.

The paragraph goes on for 187 words. The town has not changed; the greengrocer two doors down has put out its trestle table; the shop’s window is still full of stock their father never marked down; Elena sits with her hands on the wheel and thinks about the doorway and the key; she crosses the pavement, lets herself in, smells dust, lets her eyes adjust, and at word 178 reaches the register drawer. Every sentence would survive a line edit, and the unmarked-down stock characterises the father in a clause; readers of the finished book would find it slow without being able to say why.

What has gone wrong

Nothing in the paragraph is false, and nothing in it is needed. The sentence before the white space had already told the reader where Elena was going, how she would get in and what she would reach for; the paragraph after it tells her again, at the length of a small scene. The only new information is three days later, and that is the most damaging phrase in the passage. Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2) built Elena’s decision as the end of a sequel: a reaction, a dilemma, a decision made in anger and executed at once. Three days inserted between the choice and the act turn a woman who drove to the shop because she could not bear not to into a woman who thought about it over a long weekend. The time-stamp, which looks like the safest sentence in the paragraph, has quietly rewritten the character.

The writer cannot see any of this, for a structural reason. For the writer the gap is not a gap. She has imagined the drive, the parking, the weather and the key in the lock, and all of it is present to her at once as a continuous world; the white space is punctuation between two things she already knows. For the reader the white space is a real discontinuity in the only world she has, which is the text. At the moment her eye crosses it, everything she was holding — a doorway, two people, a withheld reason, a key changing hands — becomes provisional, and she does not yet know whether five minutes have passed or five months, whether she is in the same town, or whose eyes she is behind. This is the join problem: the writer is the one reader who is never on the far side of her own gaps, so everything a reader needs at a join is information the writer already has and therefore does not miss. Transitions are therefore found in revision, by reading each join cold.

The claim

The chapter offers an orientation budget as a revision tool. A gap can make the reader update time, place, person or purpose, and the writer must decide what help this particular crossing needs. That is a question of style as well as quantity.

A transition is not the white space. It manages what changes across the gap and helps re-anchor the reader. When story time is omitted, it also manages an ellipsis. The amount to write depends on this particular crossing, and may be nothing.

The claim does not say that shorter transitions are better: the hard cut is one technique among six, and a manuscript that uses it at every gap can have the same fault as one that time-stamps every gap. It does not say that disorientation is always a fault, since several of the novels this chapter reads withhold orientation on purpose and are built on the withholding. Nor does it say that transitions are mere plumbing. A transition can do a second job, though some need only to orient. The household paragraph spends heavily on a gap that needs little, orients the reader three times, and delays the new scene. The revision, in the worked analyses, replaces it with two sentences.

See also: Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), for the sequel this join follows; the Reference introduction, with the claim’s two halves set out at length.
