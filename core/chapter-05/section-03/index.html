Chapter 5 · The Transition

3. How It Works

Continue in Reference

Craft advice says that a transition must orient the reader quickly, and does not say why, how quickly, or what orienting consists of. Five bodies of work answer, each opening here with the question it answers at the desk.

How large is this gap, and does the reader know?

The frame is Gérard Genette’s treatment of duration in Narrative Discourse (1972; translated by Jane E. Lewin, 1980): the relation between the time an event takes in the story and the space it takes in the telling. The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) sets out his four movements — scene, summary, pause and ellipsis — as a decision about the speed of a unit. This chapter works at the seam between speeds. An ellipsis is story time that passes while the telling spends nothing on it. When a transition skips story time, it can be understood as an ellipsis whose edges have been finished. A simultaneous shift of place or viewpoint still calls for orientation, but is not an ellipsis in Genette’s duration scheme.

Genette distinguishes an explicit ellipsis, which declares the lapse (three days later), from an implicit ellipsis, which the reader infers from evidence (new light, new clothes, a healed cut), and from a hypothetical ellipsis, which cannot be located and is discovered only later, when something the narrative skipped comes to light. The explicit kind can be economical, though repetition risks monotony. The implicit kind leaves the new unit’s first sentences to supply evidence of elapsed time as well as place and person. The hypothetical kind is useful for thinking about the uncertain chronology of Ford Madox Ford’s The Good Soldier. An ellipsis is definite when its duration is stated and indefinite when it is not. Genette calls it characterizing when the temporal indication also gives information about the interval (three days later, after the funeral).

Genette’s category of frequency adds a bridging tool. The singulative narrates once what happened once; the iterative narrates once what happened many times (Every Thursday he counted the till twice). An iterative sentence crosses a stretch of time while characterising, because a habit is a fact about a person as well as a calendar. One more term is held in reserve: paralipsis, Genette’s word for withholding information the established focalisation would ordinarily supply. A gap that hides a decisive event may be what this chapter calls a false ellipsis; it is paralipsis only if that focalisation condition is met. The failure patterns make the distinction.

What is the reader doing in the first words after the white space?

Catherine Emmott’s Narrative Comprehension (1997) proposes that readers keep contextual frames, mental representations of the current episode holding who is present, where and when. Characters are bound into a frame, and the frame in use is primed. Three of her operations describe a transition. A frame switch abandons the primed frame and builds another; frame recall retrieves a frame built earlier, as when a novel returns to a thread; frame repair corrects a frame built on a wrong assumption.

The consequences are practical. White space need not clear a contextual frame; without a cue, a reader may initially read new sentences into the old scene and later have to revise that assumption. A familiar name can recall an established thread, while a new setting may take more work to build. This suggests testing whether a paragraph of reminder is needed. A place with a person bound into it can anchor more specifically, so Elena let herself into the shop may do more work than The shop was cold.

Why does one gap feel heavier than another?

Discourse psychology offers evidence about the load, though not a fixed price for a literary join. The event-indexing model of Rolf Zwaan, Mark Langston and Arthur Graesser (1995) proposes that readers track five dimensions of a narrative — time, space, protagonist, causality and intentionality (whose goal is in play). That study used a memory task, not reading-time measurements. Zwaan’s time-shift experiments (1996) found longer reading times after an hour later than after a moment later in the tested stories. In a separate 1998 reading-time study by Zwaan, Gabriel Radvansky, Amy Hilliard and Jacqueline Curiel, time, causality, goals and protagonist showed effects; a spatial shift slowed reading when participants had first learned a map, but not in the other conditions. Multiple discontinuities could increase reading time, but the study did not establish an additive cost per dimension. The dimension count is therefore a diagnostic for asking what changed, not a measured bill or a better predictor than duration in every case. Causality and intentionality can help explain why a join that settles when and where still reads as a non sequitur.

The experiments use short constructed texts in laboratories and describe tendencies in processing, not literary value. Work on event segmentation by Jeffrey Zacks and Barbara Tversky (2001), and by Nicole Speer, Zacks and Jeremy Reynolds (2007), supports the narrower claim that people segment narrated activity and show transient neural responses at boundaries they later identify. It does not establish that every literary join consolidates memory.

Where can I put exposition without stalling the story?

Meir Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978) treats exposition as a problem of distribution: a story conveys antecedents in a chosen order and at chosen times. A break can give a writer a place to summarise skipped events, if the reader needs them before the next scene begins. This is one possible home for distributed exposition, not a guarantee that a reader will welcome summary there. Sternberg’s distinction between suspense (uncertainty about what will happen) and curiosity (uncertainty about what has happened) suggests a question for intervals: is uncertainty about the gap something the book means to develop later? If not, a simple indication of elapsed time may serve better.

Is “cut to” a good way to think?

Film vocabulary can help a writer think about transitions. David Bordwell and Kristin Thompson’s Film Art describes continuity editing, including establishing shots, matches on action and cross-cutting, which have prose analogues. Sergei Eisenstein’s montage emphasises meaning created by juxtaposition; the Kuleshov effect is another example of context shaping a viewer’s reading of a shot. Neither makes the match-cut a direct descendant of that experiment. Film can present many visual cues together, while prose supplies its cues in sequence and can choose which details to name. A description of rain also directs a reader’s attention to rain, so an establishing paragraph may create expectations beyond orientation. Viewers can revisit a shot in some circumstances, just as readers can backtrack, though the usual viewing and reading conditions differ. Cut to is useful for imagining the sequence; in prose the writer must still decide what the arrival needs to make clear.

The dates of the convention

Narrative conventions have varied. Fielding opens the books of Tom Jones with introductory chapters, and Dickens’s narrator in Bleak House turns from Chancery, in its second chapter, to the world of fashion on the same miry afternoon. More recent fiction also uses unmarked cuts, dated headings and direct narratorial handovers. No single period has an exclusive claim on any of them. The orientation budget is therefore offered as a revision instrument: a gap may call for cues, and the writer decides how much to give. It assumes that orientation can be treated as an expenditure; the coda names novels whose effects exceed that metaphor.
