Chapter 5 · The Transition

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

The famous joins in fiction are mostly violations: unanchored, overlong, unmarked or simultaneous. The well-made ones are unmemorable by design. Each pattern below is therefore described by its mechanism and ends with a published work that commits it and succeeds. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue adds seven more: the precious match-cut, the whiplash handoff, the double anchor, the travel montage, the unmarked simultaneity, the undeclared backward cut and the frictionless manuscript.

The orphaned cut

Symptom. A hard cut into a unit that supplies no anchor for a paragraph or more: an unnamed pronoun, an unstated place and time, an action whose relation to the last scene is unclear.

Mechanism. A frame switch is needed and not signalled, so the reader builds the opening sentences into the old scene and then has to dismantle them. The fault is worse when the prose is fluent, because confident sentences are assimilated more thoroughly, and it persists because the writer is the one reader who arrives oriented.

Remedy. Test 1 (Anchor), run cold, then a resolving cue in the first clause. One word usually does it, and the word is usually a name.

Exception. The first section of William Faulkner’s The Sound and the Fury (1929) moves between 1898 and 1928 without warning, inside Benjy’s mind, and its italics mark the shifts inconsistently; Faulkner proposed printing the time levels in coloured inks. It succeeds because the disorientation is the represented consciousness, and because it is anchored by unconventional cues — honeysuckle, a fence, water — that the novel teaches within a few pages. A draft’s orphaned cut is anchored by nothing.

The throat-clearing bridge

Symptom. A paragraph of approach before the scene: the drive, the parking, the walk to the door, the weather, a recollection of what was said earlier.

Mechanism. Over-payment at the point of greatest impatience, and a broken promise, since material at the head of a unit is read as significant and the drive turns out not to matter. It survives revision because nothing in it is wrong, and because the writer needed the run-up to re-enter the book; it is drafting apparatus left in the finished text. Its relative, the travel montage, narrates the journey between two scenes as though it were story.

Remedy. Delete downward from the gap one sentence at a time until deletion causes loss. The point at which the loss appears tells you which earlier sentences were serving the reader and which were only a drafting run-up.

Exception. Each of the eighteen books of Henry Fielding’s Tom Jones (1749) opens with an introductory chapter of commentary — on critics, love, the qualifications of a historian of this kind and other subjects — before the narrative resumes. For readers who value the narrator’s company, these chapters are part of what the book offers. A bridge is throat-clearing only when the reader would rather be somewhere else.

The metronome

Symptom. Every gap crossed with one instrument, almost always the numerical stamp: Three days later … A week after that … The following Tuesday.

Mechanism. Locally a defaulted technique may over-explain some gaps and under-explain others; cumulatively identical joins can stop carrying information about relative importance. A time-stamp can prevent chronological confusion while still dulling the book’s rhythm, so the problem may not generate obvious feedback.

Remedy. The transition inventory, then re-cut the six joins that matter most, each with a technique it does not use.

Exception. David Nicholls’s One Day (2009) returns to Emma and Dexter on 15 July across nearly two decades, beginning in 1988. The recurring date is the form, but the joins are not all one-year forward leaps: the novel returns to 1988 near its end. The repeated date asks what has changed in their lives; the return to an earlier year becomes conspicuous because the forward pattern has been established.

The recap bridge

Symptom. A transition that re-establishes what the reader knows: who this character is, what she decided last time, why she is angry with her brother.

Mechanism. Frame recall mistaken for frame construction. A returning reader may retrieve a thread from a single cue, so a long reminder can feel unnecessary or condescending. Recap repeats information and may slow an opening when the reader still remembers it; its effect depends on the reading interval and what needs refreshing.

Remedy. Delete the recap and substitute one cue: a name, a resumed object, a phrase from the last scene. Where something is lost, supply it as present consequence, a character behaving as the previous scene explains.

Exception. Alexandre Dumas’s The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46) and other serial novels were published in instalments. A gap between readings can make a recap useful, including a reminder of a character’s role. Whether it helps depends on the reader’s distance from the previous instalment and what the prose has made memorable; it is not a feature of every serial or every series volume.

The false ellipsis

Symptom. A gap that turns out to have contained the important event, presented at the time as an ordinary lapse.

Mechanism. The narration withholds a consequential event inside an ordinary-looking interval, so the reader may build a mistaken account. This can be paralipsis in Genette’s sense when the established focalisation would have supplied the information; other cases are simply undisclosed omissions. The unintentional form is a drafting accident — a confrontation skipped before the writer decided what happened — whose consequence may arrive much later without a visible cause.

Remedy. Test 6 (Gap content): write out what happens in every significant gap, and where the reader will need it, choose between dramatising it, summarising it and declaring the omission.

Exception. In Emily Brontë’s Wuthering Heights (1847) Heathcliff leaves, three years pass, and he returns educated, moneyed and dangerous; the novel never says where he went or how. The gap holds the transformation on which the second half depends, and its permanent unavailability is part of his power. The deliberate false ellipsis differs from the accidental one in that the reader is made aware, at some point, that the gap was loaded. Woolf’s “Time Passes”, read in the worked analyses, tells the reader what was in its gap and refuses to dramatise it; Brontë tells the reader the gap exists and refuses to fill it.
