Chapter 5 · The Transition

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The taxonomy names six ways of managing a crossing, so that write a transition becomes a choice among options with different demands, and the repeated use of one instrument becomes visible as a choice. The categories describe strategies, not sentences, and a single join often braids two. They are not ranked: the hard cut is not more sophisticated than the time-stamp; it leaves more of the orientation work to the arrival. They answer which without supplying a formula for how many words.

The hard cut

The hard cut crosses a gap with no bridging language: the unit ends, white space or a chapter number intervenes, and the next unit begins inside its own material and orients the reader in its first sentences while getting on with its business. It leaves the orientation work to the new unit’s opening, and succeeds when that opening can carry it.

Two conditions can help. The first is that the launching line, this chapter’s term for the last sentence before the gap, has done preparatory work: a departure that names a place, an object or a person prepares the reader for the arrival. Scene and Sequel’s decision beat (Chapter 2) is one version. The second is that the reader can infer or recall enough of the new frame. Counting changed dimensions can expose a difficult handoff, but it cannot set a universal limit for the hard cut. Tolstoy’s chapter-level alternation in Anna Karenina (1878) often uses names and familiar settings to help the reader cross. Under-delivered, the hard cut is the orphaned cut; overused, it can leave a manuscript feeling fragmented.

The time-stamp

The time-stamp states elapsed time or temporal position at the head of the new unit: Three days later. In April. By the time the shop reopened. It is a concise way to orient the reader in time, though it cannot by itself settle place, person or purpose. Its bad name comes from defaulting to a numerical stamp whether or not the interval matters.

The remedy is to use its varieties. Besides the relative interval there are the absolute date, the event-relative stamp (after the funeral), clock time, the seasonal or liturgical stamp (by Michaelmas), the stamp measured on a body (when the bruise had gone yellow), the iterative (all that autumn), and the dated heading set above the prose. The event-relative form shows how a stamp can do more by saying more. Three days later, she went back to the house is nine words and delivers time. The day after the funeral, she went back to the house is eleven, and the extra words also invoke a death and a reason for returning. Some weeks later can indicate that the exact interval does not matter. A long interval may need more explicit orientation when context cannot supply it; a short interval can matter just as much when causality depends on it. Gabriel García Márquez turns the instrument against itself in One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967; translated by Gregory Rabassa), where it rains for four years, eleven months and two days: a hyperbolic event given a chronicle’s exact duration.

The match-cut

The match-cut, or echo transition, crosses a gap on a repetition: a word, image, action, sound or sentence shape at the end of one unit recurs, changed, at the start of the next, so that the join carries meaning. It is the only technique whose purpose is not orientation; it orients as a by-product and buys an argument, on Eisenstein’s principle that two things placed side by side produce a third that is in neither. The screen’s best-known case is the cut in Stanley Kubrick’s 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) from a bone thrown into the air by a hominid to an orbiting satellite, which the film leaves unidentified and Arthur C. Clarke’s novel makes an orbital weapon: one cut joins the first tool to the latest and says they are the same thing.

Its failure is invisibility: a reader who misses the echo has lost nothing she knows of, and the writer cannot detect the loss. Hence a floor: an echo must be detectable on a first reading at ordinary speed by a reader not looking for it, and an echo found only on rereading is a reward for rereading, not a transition. Three things raise detectability without ostentation: proximity (the echo at the very end of one unit and the very start of the next), distinctiveness (an unusual word, or an ordinary one used unusually), and difference (the same object changed, which reads as an argument, where pure repetition reads as a stutter). The most reliable form is ironic inversion, the same element with the opposite value. Dickens shows how high the ceiling of ostentation goes when his narrator in Bleak House (1853) asks outright what connexion there can be between the place in Lincolnshire, the house in town and Jo, the outlaw with the broom.

The summary bridge

The summary bridge narrates the interval itself in compressed form: For the next three weeks nothing was decided. Dov came in on Saturdays; the stock did not move. It can put selected content inside an interval without giving it a full scene. A break can be a useful place for that content when the reader needs it before the next scene. Summary is one way to make duration felt, and its negative form (nothing was decided; nobody rang) gives an interval a shape. Its difficulty is the hand-over from summary into scene; one possible repair is to end the bridge on a specific occasion. Austen’s bridges, in which a season passes or a family moves, suggest a working question: what can the reader infer, and what needs to be dramatized? John Steinbeck raises collective summary to a whole chapter in The Grapes of Wrath (1939), whose twelfth chapter renders Highway 66, the mother road, as the migration in aggregate. Under-delivered, the bridge leaves an interval that mattered crossed with nothing; over-delivered, it becomes throat-clearing or recap.

The handoff

The handoff changes whose consciousness the reader occupies, or where she is, or both, and it is common in novels with more than one thread. It can change several dimensions at once, though the processing cost is not a fixed sum. Three revision choices follow. Where the story allows, make time, place or an object shared across the handoff. Give a name or another clear cue early enough for this reader and this passage. And make a recurring pattern legible: an established thread can be recalled rather than rebuilt, though later handoffs are not automatically free. Paula Hawkins’s The Girl on the Train (2015) uses section headings with narrator names and dates, sometimes followed by Morning or Evening: Rachel’s first section is dated Friday, 5 July 2013, and Megan’s first Wednesday, 16 May 2012, so the headings show that Megan’s strand begins over a year earlier. A reader pulled away from a thread at its height, which is The Chapter Break’s subject (Chapter 6), may need an arrival that quickly establishes what has changed.

A proposed sixth: the bridging object

One recurring practice is useful to name separately from the five. The bridging object is a physical thing that appears on both sides of a gap and can help anchor the new unit: the coat on the hook, the ledger, the key, the car in the street. A match-cut joins by resemblance; a bridging object invites recognition of an apparently identical thing. That may help recall a known frame, though recognition is itself an inference and its speed is not a measured advantage. Its carriers include the definite article, limited re-description, a changed condition and a transfer of custody. A distinctive object planted earlier can gain meaning through repetition, giving the argument of The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4) a structural payoff. In Mrs Dalloway (1925) the motor car with drawn blinds moving up Bond Street draws attention from several characters, linking their responses to the same public event.

Three objections stand. It may be a carrier, not a technique: every join needs an anchor, the resumed object is one of the anchor types in The Mechanism, and any of the five techniques can land on one. The distinction between identity and resemblance may not survive scrutiny, since the reader meets only a second occurrence of a noun and infers that the referent is the same by the route a match-cut uses. And a category for recurring objects invites one for recurring sounds, phrases and weather patterns without limit. This chapter adopts the bridging object for revision, because the advice it yields — plant it, particularise it, use the definite article, do not re-describe it — differs enough from the match-cut’s to be worth separating, and it concedes the second objection as unanswered. Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8), which joins whole timelines rather than scenes, adopts it at the scale of a book as a means of convergence by identity, on condition that the object’s condition or custody changes between appearances, which answers the second objection at that scale.

Must a join use only one?

No. Compounding is normal; the fault is compounding a technique with its own redundancy, such as a stamp followed by a summary of the same interval, and the test is whether the second element resolves a dimension the first did not. Some gaps need no technique at all, such as a paragraph break of ninety seconds between two moments of one scene. The question is never which technique is best, but what this gap costs.
