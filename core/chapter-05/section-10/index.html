Chapter 5 · The Transition

10. Coda

Continue in Reference

The orientation budget is a metaphor, and it carries an assumption that should be stated: that orientation is a cost to be kept down, that a reader’s attention is scarce, and that the well-made transition is the one nobody notices. Those are conventions available to realist and commercial fiction, not properties of narrative as such. The method in this chapter can be useful in revision when a draft seeks that kind of clarity.

The metaphor cannot fully describe several of the books this chapter has used. “Time Passes” compresses about ten years into a substantial middle part, narrating weather, decay and human visits while omitting many individual scenes. Pedro Páramo often withholds clear signals between speakers, times and the living and the dead; one reading is that this uncertainty helps make Comala’s temporal order unstable. Fielding’s introductory chapters spend lavishly and buy a narrator’s company, which is much of what Tom Jones offers. These are transitions whose value exceeds what an orientation budget can measure.

The budget therefore survives as an instrument for making an expenditure visible, so that a writer can confirm it as a choice. A join that spends two hundred words, or withholds its anchor for a page, may be right; what the method asks is that the writer know what the spending buys and be able to say so. The tests stop being useful at the point where they begin to decide for her.
