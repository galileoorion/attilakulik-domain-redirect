Chapter 5 · The Transition

The Transition

Continue in Reference

How to carry a reader across a gap, and how much to spend doing it

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 5. Part II: The Joins.

In brief. A gap in a novel — a skipped afternoon, a change of town, a move into someone else’s head — may ask the reader to update several things at once. The transition is the writing on either side that helps her across. The effort can depend on which dimensions change (time, place, protagonist, causality and goal), how much time passes, and what the reader already knows. The right amount of orientation may be a paragraph, a clause, or nothing at all. The tools are a count of what changes, six techniques with different demands, the last sentence before the gap, twelve anchor types to choose from, a test for deliberate disorientation, and ten diagnostic questions. Use them on a draft whose readers stall after scene breaks, or whose scene openings feel slow without being wrong.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Count the dimensions a gap changes, then judge which changes the reader needs help with.
  2. Choose among six transition techniques instead of defaulting to one.
  3. Rewrite the last sentence before a gap so that the first sentence after it has less to do.
  4. Locate the anchor in any join, measure its delay, and substitute a stronger one.
  5. Audit the joins in a manuscript and read the resulting pattern.

Contents

The first files say why a gap costs the reader something and what the cost depends on; the middle files set out the techniques and the anchor; the last are for use at the desk.

  1. The Problem
    A well-written paragraph at the wrong place, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The transition against the scene break, the scales of a gap, adjacent terms
  3. How It Works
    Ellipsis, contextual frames, evidence on processing shifts, exposition at the gap, the film analogy, and the history of the convention
  4. The Taxonomy
    Five techniques, and a proposed sixth: the bridging object
  5. The Mechanism
    The anchor, its types and timing, drift, and where a transition does more than orient
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Ten questions on one page, and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a published work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    To the Lighthouse, Station Eleven, A Visit from the Goon Squad, Up, and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the budget cannot price

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests; the short coda is best read early.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds the complete chapter, The Transition: the theory at length, each technique’s textual carriers, the anchor types with exemplars, the typography of the gap, twelve failure patterns, close readings of Mrs Dalloway, “Wandering Rocks”, Anna Karenina, The Sound and the Fury, Pedro Páramo, One Hundred Years of Solitude and Bleak House, transitions in the short story, the series, screen and translation, seminar questions and an assessment rubric, and the sources.
