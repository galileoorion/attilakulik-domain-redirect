Chapter 5 · The Transition

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a screen reading and the household case, each asking which technique, which anchor at what word, how many dimensions change, and what the join does besides orient.

“Time Passes”: the interval inhabited

The middle part of Virginia Woolf’s To the Lighthouse (1927) occupies about twenty-eight pages in the first American edition, roughly a tenth of the novel. It spans about ten years between the first and last parts. Its technique is compressed narration raised to a part of the novel, and its most persistent anchor is the house itself. The Ramsay family leaves; the narration stays with the rooms, the airs that creep round corners, the shawl loosening on the boar’s skull, and the damp in the books. Mrs McNab and Mrs Bast later enter the house and the narration briefly enters their perspectives. The familiar place supplies continuity across years of change, though Woolf also narrates parts of the interval rather than simply omitting it.

What the interval contains is central to the novel. Three deaths, among them Mrs Ramsay’s, are reported in square brackets as interruptions to a narrative about weather and dust. Her death comes inside a sentence about her husband stumbling along a passage one dark morning with his arms stretched out, which remain empty because she died rather suddenly the night before. This contrasts with the false ellipsis: Woolf discloses these events while declining to dramatise them. One reading is that the brackets make important deaths appear formally subordinate. The budget can describe this crossing’s demand for orientation but cannot account for its value, which is the coda’s argument.

Station Eleven: an object across twenty years

Emily St. John Mandel’s Station Eleven (2014) opens with the actor Arthur Leander dying of a heart attack on stage during King Lear at Toronto’s Elgin Theatre, as the Georgia Flu reaches the city. Reports of the illness in Georgia had appeared the previous day. The novel moves between the decades before the collapse and a travelling company twenty years after it, in which Kirsten, a child actor in that production, is grown. Its handoffs can change several dimensions at once, and familiar names help readers recall earlier threads.

Its subtler connective device is a glass paperweight. Arthur’s friend Clark buys it in a museum gift shop and brings it to a dinner party for Arthur and his first wife Miranda; Miranda keeps it for years and sends it back to Arthur shortly before the collapse; Arthur gives it to Tanya, who gives it to the eight-year-old Kirsten on the night he dies; and Kirsten carries it into the years after. Each appearance invites the reader to recognise the same object and recall its earlier owners. Kirsten and Clark later reach the same airport settlement, but the paperweight does not cause that meeting, and the novel does not stage its return to Clark. Whether such an object is a technique or an anchor type is the question Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) inherits.

A Visit from the Goon Squad: the gap crossed inside a sentence

Jennifer Egan’s A Visit from the Goon Squad (2010) is thirteen chapters that move back and forth between the 1970s and the near future, centred on a succession of characters, often one who was peripheral in another chapter. Its chapter joins are hard cuts across several dimensions, anchored by a name and by the reader’s slowly accumulating knowledge of the cast, so that a minor figure met in one decade becomes the frame recalled in the next.

Its distinctive movement happens without a section break. “Safari”, set in 1973 on a family’s African trip, repeatedly looks decades ahead to say what will become of the people there: Rolph, Lou’s young son, will kill himself at twenty-eight; Lou and Mindy will marry, have two children and divorce; Mindy will later resume her doctoral work. These are prolepses, or flash-forwards, that narrate selected future events before returning to the safari. They do not merely omit the intervening years, and the future scenes need not preserve the safari’s place or vantage. Their weight falls on the present scene, which the reader now watches knowing some of its sequel. The novel’s best-known chapter, “Great Rock and Roll Pauses”, is a slide presentation by a girl, Alison Blake, about her family and her brother Lincoln’s preoccupation with the silences inside rock songs. It is a chapter about the join, and it makes the case this chapter makes: a gap can be part of the structure it interrupts.

A screen reading: Up

The opening of Pixar’s Up (2009) contains a summary bridge of a few minutes, almost silent and scored by Michael Giacchino’s “Married Life”, that crosses Carl and Ellie’s whole marriage between their wedding and her death. It is built from the forms the taxonomy lists for prose: enumeration (the house renovated, cloud-watching, work at the zoo), a negative event (the discovery that they cannot have children), and an iterative object, the jar of savings for a trip to Paradise Falls, filled and emptied again for a flat tyre, a broken leg and storm damage. The jar is a bridging object inside a bridge, giving the interval its shape and carrying its meaning. What does not transfer is the score, which supplies continuity across every cut for nothing; prose has no equivalent, and a writer borrowing the montage must make the recurring object, or the iterative sentence, do the music’s work. The bridge ends on a specific occasion, which is the hand-over into scene: Carl alone in the house.

The household revision case: the hard cut into the register drawer

The join follows Elena’s sequel in the truck (Scene and Sequel, Chapter 2), after the doorway in which Dov gave her the new key and withheld the reason for the changed locks (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3). It plants the smell of ink, which later chapters treat as the disturbance that starts the investigation. The outgoing unit ends: She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K.

Version A, the draft.

⁂ Three days later, Elena drove to the shop. It was a Thursday, and the traffic on the ring road was worse than she had expected, so it was nearly four by the time she parked outside. The town had not changed. The greengrocer two doors down had put out the same trestle table it had put out for as long as she could remember, and the window of their own shop was still full of the things their father had never got round to marking down. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, thinking about the conversation in the doorway and about the new key in her pocket, which Dov had handed over as though it were a favour, beside the old one she had carried since she was twelve. Then she got out, crossed the pavement, unlocked the door and went in. The shop smelled of dust and cold. She stood in the doorway for a moment and let her eyes adjust. Then she went behind the counter and tried the register drawer, the one that had always stuck on the K.

Diagnosis. The draft supplies three early cues: three days later begins at word 1, Elena is word 4, and the shop begins at word 7. The launching line has already taken Elena into the shop and named the drawer, so the intended continuation needs little new orientation. The draft’s three-day stamp instead introduces a time change and breaks the stated immediacy of her decision. It then repeats the journey and the arrival. The new unit’s business, the drawer, begins at word 178. The problem is chiefly the invented interval and the delayed continuation, not a measurable four-dimension bill.

Version B, the revision.

⁂ The drawer came free on the third try, same as always, and the smell that met her wasn’t dust. It was ink, fresh ink, the sweetish smell of a biro, wrong for a till nobody had opened since March.

The technique is a hard cut, because the launching line has already taken Elena to the drawer. The first strong cue is word 2, the drawer, a resumed object that asks the reader to recall what was just named. The revised join introduces no necessary change of time, place or protagonist; its continuity is the point. The elapsed time is unstated, inviting an inference of immediacy that suits the causal chain. The smell is a cue and a disturbance at once: someone has used this drawer lately, a detail Dov’s chapter will explain. Since March dates Elena’s belief about the drawer rather than stamping the join. The revision trusted the launching line, deleted the invented interval and the repeated journey, and made the sensory detail carry the plot.

What the revision has not solved. It is anchored only for a reader who has just read the previous scene, since the drawer presupposes the plant, so it cannot be assessed or set as an exercise in isolation. It suppresses the interval entirely, which this book can afford and a book keeping a tighter clock could not. It is not repeatable at the next join, into the ledger and the changed hand (The Chapter Break, Chapter 6), because Elena’s next move is not announced in advance; a transition solution solves one gap. And a till nobody had opened since March is Elena’s inference, reported in her idiom, and it is wrong: Dov has opened the drawer and written in the ledger since the evening of 9 March, with a ballpoint from the same drawer, as his own chapter shows. The sentence plants a misreading the book corrects much later, and whether the reader holds that against the narration depends on the book’s retrospective distance from its characters, which the opening and viewpoint chapters set.
