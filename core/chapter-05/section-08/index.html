Chapter 5 · The Transition

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a screen reading and the household case, each asking which technique, which anchor at what word, how many dimensions change, and what the join does besides orient.

“Time Passes”: the ellipsis inhabited

The middle part of Virginia Woolf’s To the Lighthouse (1927) is a transition about twenty pages long between two days ten years apart, and by the logic of the orientation budget it is an absurdity: a crossing that costs more than either of the units it joins. Its technique is the summary bridge raised to a part of the novel, and its anchor is a bridging object, the house itself. Every person leaves; the narration stays with the rooms, the airs that creep round corners, the shawl loosening on the boar’s skull, the damp in the books, and the charwoman Mrs McNab, who comes to clean. The reader crosses ten years without difficulty because she arrives at the far side in a house she knows, which is Emmott’s point that the size of an ellipsis and the difficulty of crossing it are loosely related.

What the gap contains is the novel’s centre. Three deaths, among them the central consciousness’s, are reported in square brackets as interruptions to a narrative about weather and dust. Mrs Ramsay’s comes inside a sentence about her husband stumbling along a passage one dark morning with his arms stretched out, which remain empty because she died rather suddenly the night before. In the terms of the failure patterns this is a false ellipsis made honest: Woolf tells the reader what was in the gap and refuses to dramatise it, and the typographic demotion of the deaths is what makes them unbearable. The budget can describe the mechanics of this crossing and cannot account for its value, which is the coda’s argument.

Station Eleven: an object across twenty years

Emily St. John Mandel’s Station Eleven (2014) opens with the actor Arthur Leander dying of a heart attack on stage during King Lear at Toronto’s Elgin Theatre, on the first night of the Georgia Flu, and moves between the decades before the collapse and a travelling company twenty years after it, in which Kirsten, a child actor in that production, is grown. Its handoffs often change four dimensions at once, and it pays for them chiefly with names, in a cast small enough that a name recalls a frame.

Its subtler payment is a glass paperweight, which carries the book across its threads by identity. Arthur’s friend Clark buys it in a museum gift shop and gives it to Arthur and his first wife Miranda at a dinner party; Miranda keeps it for years and returns it to Arthur before the collapse; Arthur gives it to Tanya, who gives it to the eight-year-old Kirsten on the night he dies; and Kirsten carries it through the twenty years after. Each time it surfaces in a new pair of hands, the definite article does the work the bridging object needs: the reader recognises the thing, recalls the frames it has passed through, and is handed from one life to another by an object that is in both. The novel lets the paperweight bring Kirsten to the place where Clark has spent the intervening twenty years and does not stage a scene in which the object comes home, which several readers have recorded as a loose end. As transition craft the restraint is consistent: a recognition scene would have turned a carrier into a symbol announcing itself. Whether a carrier of this kind is a technique or only an anchor type is the question Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) inherits, and Station Eleven is the case on which it decides it at the scale of a book, for an object that changes hands or condition between times.

A Visit from the Goon Squad: the gap crossed inside a sentence

Jennifer Egan’s A Visit from the Goon Squad (2010) is thirteen chapters that move back and forth between the 1970s and the near future, centred on a succession of characters, often one who was peripheral in another chapter. Its chapter joins are hard cuts across several dimensions, anchored by a name and by the reader’s slowly accumulating knowledge of the cast, so that a minor figure met in one decade becomes the frame recalled in the next.

Its distinctive transition happens without any break. “Safari”, set in 1973 on a family’s African trip, repeatedly leaps decades forward inside a sentence to say what will become of the people on the trip: that Rolph, Lou’s young son, will kill himself at twenty-eight in his father’s house, that Lou and Mindy will marry, have two children and divorce, that Mindy will go back to finish her doctorate. Each leap is an explicit, definite and qualified ellipsis turned forward, and each returns the reader to the safari in the next clause; because it changes only time and keeps place, people and vantage, a gap of decades costs a clause, and its weight falls on the present scene, which the reader now watches knowing its sequel. The novel’s best-known chapter, “Great Rock and Roll Pauses”, is a slide presentation by a girl, Alison Blake, about her family and her brother Lincoln’s preoccupation with the silences inside rock songs, the moment a song stops so that the listener expects it to end. It is a chapter about the join, and it makes the case this chapter makes: a gap is part of the structure it interrupts.

A screen reading: Up

The opening of Pixar’s Up (2009) contains a summary bridge of a few minutes, almost silent and scored by Michael Giacchino’s “Married Life”, that crosses Carl and Ellie’s whole marriage between their wedding and her death. It is built from the forms the taxonomy lists for prose: enumeration (the house renovated, cloud-watching, work at the zoo), a negative event (the discovery that they cannot have children), and an iterative object, the jar of savings for a trip to Paradise Falls, filled and emptied again for a flat tyre, a broken leg and storm damage. The jar is a bridging object inside a bridge, giving the interval its shape and carrying its meaning. What does not transfer is the score, which supplies continuity across every cut for nothing; prose has no equivalent, and a writer borrowing the montage must make the recurring object, or the iterative sentence, do the music’s work. The bridge ends on a specific occasion, which is the hand-over into scene: Carl alone in the house.

The household revision case: the hard cut into the register drawer

The join follows Elena’s sequel in the truck (Scene and Sequel, Chapter 2), after the doorway in which Dov gave her the new key and withheld the reason for the changed locks (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3). It plants the smell of ink, which later chapters treat as the disturbance that starts the investigation. The outgoing unit ends: She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K.

Version A, the draft.

⁂ Three days later, Elena drove to the shop. It was a Thursday, and the traffic on the ring road was worse than she had expected, so it was nearly four by the time she parked outside. The town had not changed. The greengrocer two doors down had put out the same trestle table it had put out for as long as she could remember, and the window of their own shop was still full of the things their father had never got round to marking down. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, thinking about the conversation in the doorway and about the new key in her pocket, which Dov had handed over as though it were a favour, beside the old one she had carried since she was twelve. Then she got out, crossed the pavement, unlocked the door and went in. The shop smelled of dust and cold. She stood in the doorway for a moment and let her eyes adjust. Then she went behind the counter and tried the register drawer, the one that had always stuck on the K.

Diagnosis. The anchor test finds the join anchored three times, at word 1 (three days later), word 4 (Elena) and word 7 (the shop): over-anchored, not under. The dimension count is one, space, and even that was announced; a one-dimension shift is being paid for as though it changed four. The launching line named the destination, the means of entry and the object, and the paragraph re-supplies all three (launch, redundancy). The technique is a time-stamp, a travel montage and a recap at a gap that needed none of them. The new unit’s business, the drawer, begins at word 178 (momentum). The three days contain nothing the reader needs (gap content), and the stamp is the root fault, since it cuts the causal link between an angry decision and its execution. The join does no second job (echo).

Version B, the revision.

⁂ The drawer came free on the third try, same as always, and the smell that met her wasn’t dust. It was ink, fresh ink, the sweetish smell of a biro, wrong for a till nobody had opened since March.

The technique is a hard cut, because the launching line had done the orienting. The anchor is word 2, the drawer, a resumed object whose definite article tells the reader she knows it, so she recalls a frame instead of building one. One dimension changes. The elapsed time is unstated, and the reader will assume immediacy, which is what the causal chain needs. The smell is an anchor and a disturbance at once: someone has used this drawer lately, and with a cheap pen, a detail Dov’s chapter will explain. Since March dates the situation in two words, as an inference from the object rather than a stamp. The revision trusted the launching line, deleted the interval and the journey, anchored on an object rather than a clock, and made the sensory detail carry the plot.

What the revision has not solved. It is anchored only for a reader who has just read the previous scene, since the drawer presupposes the plant, so it cannot be assessed or set as an exercise in isolation. It suppresses the interval entirely, which this book can afford and a book keeping a tighter clock could not. It is not repeatable at the next join, into the ledger and the changed hand (The Chapter Break, Chapter 6), because Elena’s next move is not announced in advance; a transition solution solves one gap. And a till nobody had opened since March is Elena’s inference, reported in her idiom, and it is wrong: Dov has opened the drawer and written in the ledger since the evening of 9 March, with a ballpoint from the same drawer, as his own chapter shows. The sentence plants a misreading the book corrects much later, and whether the reader holds that against the narration depends on the book’s retrospective distance from its characters, which the opening and viewpoint chapters set.
