Chapter 5 · The Transition

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

An anchor is the first concrete cue after a gap that answers when, where or whose vantage. Drift is the deliberate delay of the anchor, holding the reader in uncertainty for a beat before orienting her, and it differs from an oversight in one respect: the anchor still arrives.

What the anchor does

Emmott’s contextual-frame account suggests that a cue can help a reader identify which episode is now in view. Without it, the reader may initially assimilate new sentences to the previous frame and later revise that reading. An anchor can be a referent, a description or a heading; a familiar name may work faster than a paragraph of scene-setting. One symptom to hunt in revision is the backtrack. A cue is useful for the questions it settles: a name may identify a familiar protagonist and imply a place, while weather alone may leave both uncertain.

Position matters because a delayed cue can give the reader more to reinterpret. For revision, mark where the first reliable cue appears and ask a reader to do the same. A cue in the first clause may suit a rapid handoff; a later one may serve deliberate drift. The numerical ranges used in this chapter are prompts for inspection, not measured thresholds, and the right delay depends on the prose and the frame already in memory.

Twelve anchors to choose from

The twelve anchor types offer alternatives for revision. Their order is a working list, not a measured ranking of how many dimensions each settles.

# Anchor Instance
1 The proper name in the first clause Tolstoy’s Levin chapters
2 The place-noun, particularised Bleak House opens on “London.”
3 The time-marker The dated entries of Dracula
4 The resumed object, with the definite article and no re-description The coat; the drawer
5 The continued action, already announced before the gap A hand already on a drawer
6 The sensory cue Benjy’s honeysuckle; fresh ink
7 The somatic cue: cold, pain, a healing bruise The heat of Comala in Pedro Páramo
8 The dialogue tag in the first line The opening of “Time Passes”
9 Weather or light used as a clock The rain in One Hundred Years of Solitude
10 The social cue: a title, a form of address Tolstoy’s formal and familiar names
11 The register shift: the voice itself changes Esther against Bleak House’s panoramic narrator
12 The paratextual anchor: a heading or date-line The Girl on the Train’s section headings

Four are especially worth testing. A resumed object can recall an earlier scene once planted. Continued action can make a seam feel slight when the departure has prepared it. A register shift can distinguish a new voice without naming its speaker, though the reader may need time to recognise it. A paratextual anchor uses no narrative words but still shapes the reading. To substitute, take a join that is not working, record its anchor and its word position, choose two other types, and rewrite the first two sentences once for each. If every version needs the time-stamp back, the interval may matter; if every version reads flat, examine the new scene’s purpose; and if nothing improves, look at the launching line.

Drift

Drift is a real technique and the commonest alibi for an orphaned cut, so the criterion must be stated plainly: in drift the anchor arrives late and on purpose; in an orphaned cut it arrives where the writer’s failure to notice its absence put it, or not at all. Three conditions mark drift. The uncertainty is the character’s own, as when she wakes in a strange room or is held by grief that has detached her from place and time. The uncertainty is bounded, so the reader knows what she does not yet know. And something else is supplied meanwhile, a voice, a body, a sensation, since delay plus thin prose is only thin prose. When a writer says I meant it to be disorienting, ask for the schedule: when does the anchor arrive, and what is the reader doing until then?

Where a transition does more than orient

A novel of ninety thousand words has, on this chapter’s estimate (a working guess, not a count), between eighty and two hundred joins. If each spends forty words on orientation alone, the book spends between 3,200 and 8,000 words on connective tissue that does nothing else (80 × 40 and 200 × 40), placed where the reader is most impatient. The remedy is selection rather than compression: the one detail that already carries orientation and a second job. It lives in four humble places. The launching line can name the destination: after She left, the arrival must say where, when and why; after She drove to the shop instead, and went straight for the register drawer, it need say nothing. The first noun after the gap is the reader’s first chance to build or recall a frame, and the drawer anchors on an object with history where the room anchors on nothing. Tense and aspect orient for free: a pluperfect after a gap (the shop had been shut for a fortnight) delivers an interval and its contents. And what is not re-described orients by its absence, since naming the coat without describing it tells the reader she knows it.

After two chapters of a present-tense narrator ranging over London, the third chapter of Bleak House opens as Esther Summerson begins: I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages, for I know I am not clever. In twenty-three words the pronoun settles the vantage by total contrast with what came before; have sets a present of composition against the panoramic narrator’s present of scene; beginning to write declares a retrospective document; my portion concedes that the book has other portions; and I know I am not clever gives a character and a narrator the reader must read past. Nothing bridges, and not one word is about the transition.

There is a ceiling. Significance fatigue is what a reader feels when every seam rhymes and every arrival lands on a loaded object: no join is plain, so none can be felt as emphatic. A novel needs joins that only orient, as rests. As a working figure for a first revision, not a rule, fewer than one join in four should be doing conspicuous double duty.

The mark on the page

Typography can cue a reader to expect a change in time, place, vantage or subject, but no mark has a fixed meaning or a fixed gap size. A manuscript broken at every small shift may feel choppy; an unmarked large shift may be harder to follow. A blank line at the foot of a printed page can be missed, and some digital layouts handle blank space differently. Where a section break must remain visible, a mark such as spaced asterisks may help, while the prose should still supply any needed orientation.
