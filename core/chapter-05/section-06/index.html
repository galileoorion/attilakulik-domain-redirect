Chapter 5 · The Transition

6. Diagnostic Tests

Continue in Reference

The ten tests are for revision, not composition. They rest on one procedure: read the join cold, covering the preceding unit and reading the transition and the next two hundred words as though the book began there, since a writer reading in sequence arrives already oriented and will never find the fault. Run tests 1 to 4 (Anchor, Dimension count, Launch and Technique fit) first, because there is no point tuning an echo at a join whose anchor never arrives. Record a sentence, not a verdict: “the anchor is a place-noun at word thirty-four” is useful, and “fails test 1” is not. On a full manuscript, run the transition inventory (in the checklist) first, then the tests on the dozen joins it flags. Each test is failed on purpose by a book worth reading.

Test 1 — Anchor

Read cold: at what word can you say when, where and whose vantage? Is any delay doing something?

Failure. An orphaned cut, unless the delay meets the three conditions of drift.

Exception. Benjy’s section of The Sound and the Fury.

Test 2 — Dimension count

How many of these change: time, space, protagonist, causality and intentionality?

Failure. The reader needs a cue the join has not supplied, or receives more explanation than this crossing needs. The count alone cannot decide which.

Exception. A disorientation the book means.

Test 3 — Launch

What does the last sentence before the gap do for the far side?

Failure. The departure simply stops, so the arrival carries the whole bill.

Exception. A cut meant to surprise.

Test 4 — Technique fit

Which technique is this, and was it chosen or defaulted to?

Failure. A habit: usually the numerical time-stamp.

Exception. A book whose repeated document headings are part of its form, as in Dracula.

Test 5 — Momentum

At what word does the new unit’s own business begin?

Failure. Throat-clearing: words telling the reader what she would have assumed.

Exception. A narrator whose company is the point (Tom Jones).

Test 6 — Gap content

Write in one sentence what happens inside the gap. Will the reader need it?

Failure. A false ellipsis: a consequence will later arrive without its cause.

Contrast. “Time Passes” reports consequential events in compressed form rather than hiding them altogether.

Test 7 — Redundancy

Is the reader oriented more than once?

Failure. A double anchor or a recap.

Exception. A reader returning after a long absence (serial publication).

Test 8 — Simultaneity

Does the reader know whether this unit comes after the last one or alongside it?

Failure. Unmarked simultaneity: she may assume “after”.

Exception. A chronology meant to be unrecoverable (Pedro Páramo).

Test 9 — Echo

Could the join do a second job, and if it does one, will a first-time reader notice?

Failure. Inert connective tissue, or an echo too faint to land.

Exception. A book written for rereading (Ulysses).

Test 10 — Consistency

Across the manuscript, does the technique vary with the gap?

Prompt for inspection. One technique in more than two joins in three; the fraction is a workshop heuristic, not a measured threshold.

Exception. The same as test 4 (Technique fit).

Four refinements sharpen the tests. In test 1 (Anchor), give the join to a reader who has not read the previous scene and ask her to mark where she knew where she was; compare her mark with yours. Test 2 (Dimension count) catches joins that change several things at once, but its count should prompt a question rather than govern a formula: which changes can the reader infer, and which need a cue? Test 3 (Launch) can solve a join that has resisted rewriting at the arrival. In test 8 (Simultaneity), write “after” or “during” in the margin at every handoff, then check whether the text makes the relationship legible; where it does not, a phrase such as that same afternoon may suffice.

Reading the results together

Failures can cluster, and a cluster suggests a first move rather than a diagnosis by formula. Test 1 (Anchor) alone suggests trying a clearer cue. Tests 1 (Anchor) and 2 (Dimension count) together suggest checking which changed dimensions remain unclear; a compound cue or a different gap position may help. Tests 1 (Anchor) and 3 (Launch) invite a revision of the departure. Tests 4 (Technique fit) and 10 (Consistency) invite a comparison of repeated joins. Test 5 (Momentum) suggests deleting downward until something necessary is lost. Test 7 (Redundancy) suggests testing whether the second cue is needed. Tests 2 (Dimension count), 5 (Momentum) and 10 (Consistency) together may point beyond sentence-level repair to the placement of breaks, which is The Chapter Break’s business (Chapter 6).

Test 9 (Echo) is the one most likely to make a manuscript worse when over-applied, because it rewards the writer’s ingenuity, not the reader’s experience. Run tests 1 to 8 (Anchor, Dimension count, Launch, Technique fit, Momentum, Gap content, Redundancy and Simultaneity) on every flagged join, and test 9 (Echo) on the ten joins that matter most and on no others.
