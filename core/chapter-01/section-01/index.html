Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

1. The Problem: Scenery

Continue in Reference

Two drafts of one passage

The household novel, whose drafts and revisions run through every chapter of this book, is a quiet contemporary family novel set in a British town. A father who kept a small shop died in March; his three children, Elena, Nadia and Dov, are left with the shop, the flat above it, his coat on its hook, and the leather ledger in which he recorded eleven years of the shop’s ordinary arithmetic. A draft chapter of that novel contains a passage of about nine hundred words in which Nadia climbs the stairs to the flat. She lets herself in, fills the kettle, and looks for a while at her father’s coat, army green gone the colour of dishwater, with the left cuff frayed through where he used to hook his thumb before he spoke. She remembers the habit. She washes the two cups in the sink, counts the cups in the cupboard, and goes back down to the shop. The prose is careful and the observation is good, and a reader finishes the passage having enjoyed it in the mild way one enjoys competent description. A day later the reader remembers nothing of it.

Now suppose a second draft of the same passage, identical except for three sentences. Nadia looks at the coat and sees that its shoulders have been brushed: the dust that lies on the rest of the coat and everything else in the flat has not settled there. She has not brushed them. She washes the cups, counts the cupboard, and goes back down, and she does not ring anyone to ask who has been coming up the stairs. Nothing in the prose has improved. Yet the reader now holds a fact that Nadia has chosen not to pursue, a question the book has not answered, and a reading of Nadia herself, who would rather count cups than know. The next forty pages will be read differently.

What the difference shows

Two things follow from the comparison, and the rest of the chapter is built on them.

The first is that the difference between the drafts is almost invisible while writing, since both feel like productive work and contain the same quantity of careful observation. It appears only when the passage is read as part of a finished draft and the writer asks what it did, which is why the diagnosis this chapter teaches belongs to revision and is harmful during composition.

The second is that the quality of the prose is not evidence about the scene. A well-written passage that does nothing is harder to cut than a badly written one, and is cut less often, which is how manuscripts come to accumulate them.

The claim

The principle the chapter examines can be put in one sentence:

If nothing changes, the scene is not a scene: it is scenery.

The sentence is deliberately harsh. It claims that a stretch of dramatised narrative must answer for the reader’s attention and the book’s length, and that it answers in one currency, a change of state. It does not claim that the change must be large, external or visible to the characters. In chapter 42 of Henry James’s The Portrait of a Lady a woman spends much of the night by the fire and comes to understand her marriage, though little happens outwardly; the chapter is examined among the worked analyses. Nor does it claim that summary is a lesser mode: much of what a draft calls a flat scene is a paragraph of summary inflated into dialogue and stage business because the writer believed scenes are always better, and the chapter argues that they are not. Finally, it does not describe narrative in general: the preference for the dramatised scene over the narrated report is prominent in much twentieth-century Anglo-American craft advice, and its limits are set out in How It Works.

Where this chapter sits

Later chapters depend on this chapter’s vocabulary. The value-shift test, the question what changed?, returns in Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3), The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4), The Transition (Chapter 5) and The Ending (Chapter 16). The five domains of purpose are adopted by The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3), The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4) and Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7), each of which proposes a further domain, and The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) also adopts the relational domain proposed here. The cost of a scene in the reader’s attention becomes, in The Subplot (Chapter 14), the cost of a second line of story, and multiplicity recurs in every chapter.

Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2) owns the rhythm of the dramatic unit, Dwight Swain’s division of it into a scene proper (goal, conflict, disaster) and a sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision). This chapter uses “scene” in the ordinary sense of any continuous stretch of dramatised action.
