Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

5. The Mechanism: Engine, Economy and Speed

Continue in Reference

A scene produces change by a mechanism with three parts: an engine makes something happen, multiplicity determines how much the happening is worth, and the choice of speed decides whether the material should be a scene at all.

The engine

A scene’s engine is a want that operates now, a resistance to it, and a cost. The third converts activity into consequence: a want that overcomes resistance at no cost has the shape of conflict and none of its effects, so the useful question after any scene is what did getting it cost?

The engine has one variant that looks like a failure. A want may be granted at once, and the granting may itself be the disaster. In chapter 18 of Dickens’s Great Expectations (1861), the lawyer Jaggers finds Pip and Joe at the Three Jolly Bargemen, then tells them in the parlour that Pip has great expectations and is to be brought up as a gentleman. Pip wants what he is given, but the transaction has conditions, and Joe resists Jaggers’s manner and refuses payment for releasing Pip from his apprenticeship. The cost lies in Pip’s response to the news and in the estrangement from Joe that follows.

The engine also tells a writer where a scene stops: when the value stops moving, and anything after that point slackens it.

Multiplicity

Multiplicity is the practice of making one unit of text discharge several narrative functions at once. At the scale of the scene it is best stated as an economy. A novel has a finite number of scenes, each of which costs the reader time and takes up space in the book; a scene that serves one domain has spent a scene’s worth of attention to buy one function, and a scene that serves three has bought three at the same price. The practical instruction is not to add domains but to notice which are already latent: almost every draft scene contains an unexploited second function in material already present.

The opening of Darcy’s first proposal, in chapter 34 of Austen’s Pride and Prejudice (1813), shows how much work a few phrases can do:

In vain I have struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.

The speech opens on the vocabulary of defeat, so the reader learns first that this man experiences love as a failure of self-government; that is character, and because the first words are about him rather than her, it is also relational. It will not do is the idiom one uses of an unsatisfactory arrangement, applied to a passion, which makes it texture and character at once. The passive my feelings will not be repressed removes him as agent. You first appears in an instruction, you must allow me, which shapes the refusal that follows. And ardently, the one word in which feeling escapes control, collides with it will not do to open the novel’s question about feeling and judgement. Elizabeth’s answer does the same work in reverse. She tells him he could not have made her the offer of his hand in any possible way that would have tempted her to accept it, and in any possible way refuses not this proposal but every proposal from this man. The clause also closes her own options, and the rest of the book is needed to reopen them.

Revision often looks for multiplicity in metaphor. At the scale of the scene the work is usually done in four humbler places. The first is the physical business, what hands do while mouths talk: a man who goes on wrapping shop stock in newspaper through an accusation supplies texture (the chore), character (the refusal to stop), relation (he will not give her his attention) and plot (a man packing a shop has already decided something). The second is the object that changes hands. A key, a ledger or a plate passed or withheld registers power between two people without a word spoken, which is how the doorway exchange in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) works, when Dov gives Elena the new key and keeps the reason. The third is what a character declines to say or ask. An unasked question costs no words, and when one sibling does not ask another where he was in March the omission does character, relational and plot work while the reader performs the inference. The fourth is placement against the reader’s knowledge: a scene the reader enters knowing more than the characters means two things in every line, so dramatic irony multiplies functions without adding text.

Multiplicity has a ceiling, and the failure at the ceiling is the overloaded scene. Its symptoms are that every line is significant, so that emphasis becomes impossible; that several characters each pursue a different want in one room, so that the reader cannot tell whose scene it is; that the reader can say what is being established but not what is happening; and that every object means something, so that the reader stops believing in the room. The remedy is to let some scenes do only one thing, because a reader who is never allowed to rest cannot register emphasis.

Speed: the scene–summary continuum

A prior question is at what speed the material should be narrated. The scene–summary continuum is the range of modes between full scene and ellipsis; the Reference chapter sets out fourteen, and the eight below cover most revision decisions.

Mode What it is It buys It costs
Full scene The occasion rendered moment by moment Presence; the clearest value shift Length; flattened proportion if used everywhere
Compressed scene Scene texture with hours removed at the joins Scenic force at a fraction of the length Clumsy joins read as lapses
Scene within summary A summary that opens for a paragraph into one rendered moment Proof that the summary is true Repeated moments can turn a summary into a sequence of scenes
Singulative summary Plain report of a bounded stretch of time Speed; the power to say this mattered less May offer less immediate experience; overuse reads as synopsis
Iterative summary One narration of something that happened many times A life rather than an episode; the normal Individual occasions and choices may be less distinct
Interiorised scene An occasion rendered as thought, paced by thought Deep change with no external event Needs a mind worth following for pages
Ellipsis The event is not narrated; the reader infers it The reader supplies what the text withholds An ellipsis over an unguessable event is a gap
Documentary rendering The event delivered as a letter, entry or report A change of state through a document Access to perception is limited by what the document maker records

The mode most often missing from contemporary drafts is iterative narration, which tells once what happened many times: every week someone brushed the shoulders of the coat. One dramatised occasion can illustrate a habit, but it cannot by itself establish its duration or repetition: a writer who tries to convey eleven years of a man’s ledger-keeping by dramatising one Tuesday has shown the reader only one Tuesday.

The continuum is used by substitution testing: take a scene that is not working, rewrite it in two modes as far from its own as possible (a full scene as summary and as ellipsis, say), and judge only when all three versions exist. If the summary loses nothing, use the summary. If it loses something the writer cannot name, the scene has a function nobody has identified, and the next step is to name it and strengthen it. If it loses something the writer can name at once, the scene is doing its job, and the revision is to cut whatever in it is not that job.

The domain audit

The mechanism can be checked across a chapter with a table. For each scene, write its length and the letters of every domain it actively serves (P, C, T, X for texture, R), and beside each letter write the specific change in three or four words: not character but she stops defending him. A letter that cannot be annotated marks a domain the scene occupies rather than serves, and is struck out. If most scenes carry a single letter, the draft is spending scenes one function at a time; if most carry four or five, the reader has no rests. A long scene carrying only X is either a runaway set piece or a scene establishing the normal, and the question that separates them is which later scene it serves.
