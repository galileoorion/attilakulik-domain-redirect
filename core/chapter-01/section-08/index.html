Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings show different parts of the instrument at work: multiplicity in a commercial novel, a relational change made offstage in a literary one, and a chapter with no external event. A short screen reading and the household revision case follow.

The Hunger Games: the reaping

Suzanne Collins’s The Hunger Games (2008) is a young-adult dystopian novel whose premise is launched in a single scene. The novel’s first chapter opens on the morning of the reaping, the annual lottery in which District 12 must surrender one girl and one boy to the televised Games. Katniss Everdeen wakes and goes hunting with her friend Gale, and the unhurried chapter builds the normal (scarcity, illegal hunting, the arithmetic by which Katniss’s name is in the lottery twenty times and her twelve-year-old sister Prim’s once) that the draw will break. At the end of the chapter the name drawn is Prim’s, and at the start of chapter 2 Katniss volunteers to take her place.

The engine changes gear in a sentence. Katniss’s want in chapter 1 is to get through the day; once the name is read, it is to save Prim, and the resistance is the rule itself, which allows a substitute only if one volunteers, together with Prim clinging to her until Gale carries the child away. The cost is Katniss’s probable death, accepted in two words: I volunteer! Stated in two parallel sentences, the shift is this. At the start, Katniss believes that survival means keeping her family fed and her head down. At the close, she has publicly traded her life for her sister’s.

Each domain is served by the same material. The plot turns. Character is revealed at the highest available cost, which is how character becomes evidence. The relation between the district and the Capitol shifts too, because when the crowd is invited to applaud, it does not; people touch three fingers of the left hand to their lips and hold them out, a gesture the novel will later use as a sign of dissent. That refusal is also where the theme enters, the book’s question about spectacle and consent, staged without comment. The second draw names Peeta Mellark, prompting Katniss to recall how he once gave her bread from his family’s bakery when her family was starving. The memory introduces a debt the Games will make her confront, and the whole sequence is built from a few objects and gestures.

Normal People: the car

Sally Rooney’s Normal People (2018) is built in dated sections, each headed by an interval and a month (Three weeks later (February 2011)), and the intervals carry much of its structure. Connell and Marianne’s relationship at school is secret, because he fears what his friends would think. In the section headed Two days later (April 2011), Connell, having asked a popular classmate, Rachel Moran, to the Debs (the school-leaving dance), tells his mother Lorraine in the car. When she learns that he has not told Marianne, she asks to be let out.

A draft would probably stage the confrontation between Connell and Marianne. Rooney places the scene’s weight on a third party. Connell’s want is small and evasive, to have his decision accepted as a practical matter; the resistance is his mother’s judgement, and it is total, since she refuses even to stay in the car with him. The value shift is relational twice over. Between mother and son, approval has been withdrawn, and his mother’s moral authority, the one he cannot argue with, is now against him. Between Connell and Marianne, a change has happened that neither of them has yet spoken about. The character domain is served by the gap between Connell’s account of what he has done (a social convenience) and what the reader and his mother see (a betrayal), which is the gap on which the whole novel turns. The theme, whether people can be themselves under the gaze of others, is staged without being stated.

The next section heading jumps four months, to August, by which time Marianne has stopped attending school except to sit her exams. The rupture arrives across an ellipsis as a state already changed. Rooney’s dated headings let her move a relationship by omission: the reader measures the injury by the distance revealed when the next section opens.

The Portrait of a Lady: the vigil

Chapter 42 of Henry James’s The Portrait of a Lady (1881) gives most of its space to Isabel Archer’s thought rather than dialogue or outward event. After her husband leaves the room, a servant briefly attends to the fire; Isabel asks for fresh candles and continues her meditation. She has also come upon her husband seated with Madame Merle standing nearby, a configuration whose familiarity troubles her. When the clock strikes four, she rises to go to bed but pauses over that remembered image. In the preface to the New York Edition James described the chapter as a representation of her “motionlessly seeing”, and claimed it threw the action further forward than twenty incidents would have done.

James’s chapter passes all ten tests. Its engine is Isabel’s want to understand what she saw, and the resistance is her own: the understanding is unbearable, and the chapter proceeds through her approaches to and retreats from it. At the start, she believes her marriage is a private failure of feeling and that her choices were her own. At the close, she knows her husband hates her, that the hatred is settled, and suspects that her freest act was arranged for her. In terms of speed, it is the interiorised scene: story time is several hours and text time is a chapter, with the pace held chiefly by thought rather than by talk. The chapter has stages, each with its own recognition, so that the reader experiences progress rather than accumulation, which is where attempts at this mode usually fail, since the thought circles instead of advancing. The chapter shows the difference between a function and its carrier. Everything the model requires is present; the usual outward carriers occupy little space.

A screen reading: Fleabag, series 2, episode 1

The first episode of the second series of Fleabag (BBC, first broadcast 4 March 2019) takes place almost entirely at one restaurant dinner, held to mark the engagement of Fleabag’s father to her godmother. Several wants run at once around a single table, which is one of the symptoms of the overloaded scene described in The Mechanism. The episode stays legible because the heroine’s asides to the camera make her the focaliser throughout, so that the question whose scene is this? never arises. Its decisive change is relational. When her sister Claire miscarries in the restaurant’s toilets and the truth threatens to come out, Fleabag tells the table that the miscarriage is hers. The lie exposes her to the table’s judgement and spares her sister, and the relation between the sisters changes without either of them saying so. The episode’s opening shot, of Fleabag wiping blood from her nose, places the end of the evening first; the viewer then watches the dinner knowing that it ends in violence, which turns the episode into a sustained piece of dramatic irony.

The household revision case: the ledger confrontation

This case is an exercise rather than a part of the household novel’s sequence. In the novel Elena finds the ledger in the register drawer, about a quarter of the way through the book, and sees that the hand changes after the father’s last entry on 9 March. The exercise imagines a later scene in which Nadia, told of the discovery by Elena, confronts Dov at the shop. Nothing in it binds the novel.

Version A (draft)

Nadia had been at the shop since nine. She had counted the receipts twice, which took a while because the register drawer stuck on the K and only came free on the third try. Dov arrived at eleven with a roll of bin bags and started on the shelves.

Elena had found the ledger the week before and rung her about it. Their father had kept it for eleven years, in his careful slanting hand with the crossed sevens. But after the ninth of March the writing changed. It was looser, and it was not his.

Since their father’s death, things had been difficult between all three of them, and she found it hard to trust Dov the way she once had.

“You’ve been writing in it,” she said at last. “Haven’t you.”

Dov admitted that he had. He explained that he had not wanted to worry her or Elena, and that he had only been keeping up the accounts because somebody had to. Nadia said she understood. They finished the shelves and went out for lunch.

Diagnosis

When run through the diagnostic tests, the draft fails almost all of them. Nadia wants nothing until eleven o’clock; Dov concedes at once and explains fully; the value shift, from suspicion to confirmation, is the weakest available; and the one fact delivered is one the reader already has from Elena’s discovery. Only texture is served, thinly, and the relational shift is stated (she found it hard to trust Dov) rather than enacted. The scene enters two hours early, leaves after lunch, and delivers every fact as briefing. The prose is not bad, which is why the faults are hard to see.

Version B (revision)

The ledger was in the register drawer, open at the ninth of March. Nadia took it out and held it up. “You’ve been writing in it.” Dov went on wrapping the shelf stock in newspaper, not looking up. “Somebody had to. I knew something was wrong.” He held out his hand for it. She watched his hands, steady, too steady, and put the ledger back in the drawer and shut it, and kept her palm flat on the wood. He didn’t argue. That was the part that frightened her.

At the start, Nadia expects Dov to deny it or explain it away. At the close, she knows he will do neither; she has kept the ledger from him and found that his not arguing frightens her more than a lie would have. The plot is served because a question has been opened (what did Dov see on the ninth of March that told him something was wrong?) which the novel answers only in Dov’s own chapter. Character is shown through a physical tell and through what Nadia does with the book rather than what she says. The theme, what the living owe the dead’s silences, is staged as two people negotiating over a document neither will read aloud. The wrapping of stock sets a register of ordinary business interrupted. And the relational shift, the scene’s principal transaction, has become an event with a place in the room: an outstretched hand, refused.

The revision entered with the ledger already open; gave Dov one sentence that admits the small fact and withholds the large one, so that what he says increases the reader’s uncertainty; replaced a stated relation with a transacted one; turned exposition into physical business; and exited on the last moving beat.

What Version B has not solved

Version B is too tidy. Eighty-nine words carrying five domains, a tell and a closing line of interpretation sit close to the ceiling of multiplicity, and That was the part that frightened her tells the reader how to read the gesture instead of letting the gesture work; a more confident version would end on He didn’t argue. The redundancy is also unresolved. The reader learned of the changed hand at Elena’s discovery, so the scene’s real shift is narrower than its staging suggests: not that Dov wrote in the ledger, but that he will not defend himself. A better version would make the accusation about something else. Finally, if the scene were ever moved into the sequence, Dov’s line would have to stay vague enough not to spend his own chapter, and Nadia could not read the ledger’s later pages, which the novel keeps for its ending.
