Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

2. Definitions

Continue in Reference

The working definition

A scene is a unit of story in which a character pursues a concrete, immediate objective against resistance, rendered at something close to the pace at which it happens, so that the state of the story is measurably different at its end from its state at its start. Three parts of the definition carry most of its weight.

The first is the concrete, immediate objective. This is not the story goal, the want that drives the whole book, but the scene goal, the want that drives the next ten minutes. Elena’s story goal is to find out why the shop’s locks were changed in March. Her goal in the doorway of her brother’s house, when he hands her the new key, is smaller and more urgent: to get the key without having to hear something she is not ready for, or to get the reason without having to ask for it twice. A reader who does not yet care about the story goal can still be carried by a scene goal, which is why the distinction matters most on the opening pages (The Opening Pages, Chapter 9).

The second is resistance. It is the component most often missing from draft scenes. A want that meets no obstacle produces activity rather than a scene, and the obstacle need not be a person: it can be the character’s own reluctance, the shape of the situation, or time running out.

The third is pace. Dramatisation is not a matter of dialogue or action, which are the scene’s usual materials, but of the rate at which the telling moves relative to the events told, which How It Works explains.

Where a scene ends

There is no principled word count for a scene. Three units of different size are in common use, and each answers a different question.

Unit Typical extent The question it answers
Beat 20–300 words What is the smallest exchange in which the balance between people shifts?
Scene 400–3,000 words What is the smallest unit that could be lifted out and still read as a story?
Sequence 3,000–12,000 words What is the smallest unit that completes a movement of the plot?

The figures are illustrative working ranges for this chapter, given only to show scale; they are not measured norms. A beat is the smallest unit of change, one exchange or action that alters the balance; the word comes from acting and screenwriting practice. A sequence is a run of scenes that together complete one movement of the plot. The working rule for the boundary of the middle unit is functional: a scene ends when the value it was moving stops moving, or when there is a break in time, place or viewpoint.

Summary is not a failed scene

The commonest confusion in workshop discussion is to treat summary as a scene that has not been written yet. Summary is narrated report, in which a stretch of story time is compressed into a shorter stretch of text and told rather than enacted: For the next three weeks she did not go near the shop covers three weeks in twelve words. It is a different speed with different uses, and the modes between it and the full scene are set out in The Mechanism.

Adjacent terms

Four neighbouring terms recur in this chapter, in the senses the table gives.

Term Sense in this book Relation to the scene
Sequel In Swain’s model, the reflective unit after a scene: reaction, dilemma, decision Treated in full in Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2)
Set piece An extended scene of spectacle: a battle, a ball, a storm, a party A scene marked by scale and by the reader’s expectation of it; its failure mode is among the failure patterns
Scene (screen) A dramatic unit in more or less continuous time and space; it may contain many shots and cuts Screen scenes often mark a change of time or place, but a cut alone need not end one
Kernel and satellite Events that branch the story, and events that amplify a branch already taken The distinction the extraction test measures; explained in How It Works

The prose sense of a scene is the broadest of these, and the other terms either subdivide it or describe what surrounds it.

What this chapter leaves to other chapters

The chapter concerns the single scene and what it is for. The rhythm of scene and sequel belongs to Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), talk to The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3), and the descriptive detail to The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4). Movement between scenes belongs to The Transition (Chapter 5), The Chapter Break (Chapter 6) and The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), and scenes set in the past to Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7). The allocation of scenes between plot lines is the subject of The Subplot (Chapter 14), redundancy in the long middle of a book that of The Middle (Chapter 11), and theme at the scale of the book that of Arc and Theme (Chapter 12). Opening, prefatory and final scenes belong to The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), The Prologue (Chapter 10) and The Ending (Chapter 16).
