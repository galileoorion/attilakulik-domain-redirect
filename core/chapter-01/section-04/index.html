Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

4. The Taxonomy: Five Domains of Purpose

Continue in Reference

The value-shift test asks whether something changed, not in what domain, and a writer revising a flat scene needs the second answer because each domain has its own remedies. This chapter uses five domains of purpose: plot (the action changes), character (a person changes, or is revealed), theme (the meaning changes), texture (the reader’s experience changes), and the relational domain (what lies between two people changes). The fifth is this chapter’s proposal, and The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) adopts it.

The domains are lenses rather than boxes: a betrayal is at once a turn in the plot, a revelation of character and a statement of theme, and the model supplies five questions to ask of one scene. They are also an instrument for revision, not a target for drafting, since aiming at all five produces the overloaded scene described in The Mechanism.

Plot: the action changes

A scene serves the plot when it alters the causal chain: what has happened, what can now happen, what must now happen. It may advance the chain a step, complicate it with an obstacle or a cost, escalate the stakes of a conflict already running, turn it by a reversal, resolve a question the book has held open, or plant an object or a fact that a later scene will pay off. Because of planting, a scene that seems to do little may be indispensable, and the extraction test has to be run forwards as well as backwards: not only what would the reader fail to know? but what later scene would become unearned?

Plot is easy to fake, because activity resembles consequence. Its characteristic under-delivery is the errand, a scene that moves a body from one place to another and calls the movement plot. Its over-delivery is the pile-up, three reversals in nine hundred words, each devaluing the last, since a reversal is legible only against a stable expectation. In Part One, chapter 7, of Dostoevsky’s Crime and Punishment (1866; in Constance Garnett’s translation), the murder of the pawnbroker is the planned turn, but the structurally decisive event is the unplanned one: her sister Lizaveta comes home and is killed too. A crime Raskolnikov had theorised becomes one he cannot explain to himself, and the rest of the novel runs on the difference. A scene’s plot value often lies in its departure from what a character planned.

Character: a person changes, or is revealed

A scene serves character when it alters what the reader knows about a person, or alters the person, under conditions that make the alteration evidence rather than assertion. It may reveal an existing trait through action under pressure, test a stated belief to see whether it holds, move a character a measurable step along an arc, complicate the reader’s sympathy, or show the gap between what a character says of herself and what she does. The carriers are behavioural: a choice made quickly, what someone notices, whom they lie to. The commonest error is the comfortable occasion, in which a character is given a confidant or a well-placed question and is simply herself; nothing is learned because nothing is risked. The domain’s under-delivery is the trait exhibition, a scene demonstrating a quality the reader has already accepted. Its over-delivery is the epiphany, in which a character understands herself completely, in words she would not have, at the convenient end of a chapter. In chapter 7 of Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart (1958), Okonkwo cuts down Ikemefuna, the boy who has lived in his household for three years, and Achebe gives the motive in one short sentence after the act: he was afraid of being thought weak. Nothing about Okonkwo changes in that scene; the reader’s understanding of him does.

Theme: the meaning changes

A scene serves theme when it stages the book’s governing question in a form that produces meaning the scene does not state. Theme works better as the book’s repeated question than as a message: what the living owe the dead’s silences, in the household novel; what a marriage is for; what an institution does to the people inside it. A scene serves the domain when it puts that question to a character in circumstances where any answer costs something. Its instruments are a choice whose terms are the book’s terms, a mirror or a foil (two characters or events set side by side so that their difference means something), dramatic irony, and the recurrence of a motif under changed conditions.

Its under-delivery is the name-check, one line that sounds like the book’s title; its over-delivery is the mouthpiece, a character who exists to state the theme. Counterpoint, two strands running at once without comment, does the domain’s work without either. In Part Two, chapter 8, of Flaubert’s Madame Bovary (1857; in Geoffrey Wall’s translation, 1992), Rodolphe’s seduction speech is intercut with the announcement of prizes at an agricultural show, for manure and livestock and fifty-four years of a servant’s labour, and the novel’s argument about the language of romance is produced entirely by adjacency.

Texture: the experience changes

A scene serves texture when it alters the reader’s sensory, tonal or temporal experience of the book: where they feel they are, what register they are in, how fast the book is moving. Its functions are to ground the reader in a place, set a tone, control pace, deliver a set piece the book has promised, and establish the normal, the baseline against which a later departure will register. That last function is the domain’s best defence. A scene that establishes what is ordinary in a world is load-bearing for a later scene, and the extraction test has to be run on the pair: remove the ordinary scene and it is the later one that loses its force.

Under-delivery here is the generic room with a table in it, and over-delivery is the runaway set piece, spectacle at great length with every other value where it started. Richard Osman’s The Thursday Murder Club (2020), a comic crime novel set in a retirement village, alternates its third-person investigation with chapters from Joyce’s diary, written in the first person. By a strict plot accounting the diary chapters are the slackest material in the book: domestic news, opinions, the small politics of the village. They establish the normal life of the place against which the murders register, set a comic register that lets the book be about death without solemnity, and pace the investigation by supplying rests between its chapters.

Relational: what lies between them changes

Ask what changed in a quarrel between siblings over a key, or in the Hunsford proposal in Austen’s Pride and Prejudice, and the honest answer is rarely the plot and rarely either person. It is what lies between them. The chapter therefore proposes a fifth domain. A scene serves the relational domain when it alters the state of a relationship (the distribution of trust, power, obligation, intimacy, debt or hostility between two or more people) so that their later dealings must proceed on different terms.

Two arguments support separating it from character. Relations change while both parties stay the same: after Hunsford neither Darcy nor Elizabeth is a different person, but both hold a radically revised view of each other, and the character domain, which records what a scene shows about one person, has no vocabulary for a change located in neither party and real in both. The carriers also differ. A relation is carried by forms of address and changes in them, by who begins and who yields in conversation, by physical distance, by who may say nothing without being asked what is wrong, by shared jokes that still work or no longer land, by who knows what about whom, and by the questions each may still put to the other.

There are fair objections: a relation may be only a pair of mental states, one in each person, and so belong to character; admitting every category with distinct carriers would multiply domains until the model lost its use; and most relational shifts can be redescribed as plot. The chapter adopts the fifth domain for diagnosis on an asymmetry of error: writers who audit for four domains routinely fail to notice that no relational shift has happened, whereas writers who audit for five rarely mistake a plot shift for a relational one. The blind spot it removes is the family novel in which nothing between anybody ever changes. Sally Rooney’s Normal People (2018) is read in the worked analyses as a novel whose principal events are relational.

How many domains at once

A scene need not serve every domain, and the model is misused when it is read that way. The table summarises how each domain is usually established and how it usually goes wrong.

Domain Established chiefly by Under-delivery Over-delivery
Plot A choice that closes options; a plant; an irreversible act The errand; recapitulation The reversal pile-up
Character Behaviour at a cost; the gap between account and act The trait exhibition The epiphany
Theme Juxtaposition; recurrence; dramatic irony The name-check The mouthpiece
Texture Exact nouns; rhythm; ritual; the established normal The generic room The runaway set piece
Relational Address, turn-taking, permitted silence, the right to ask The relation stated rather than enacted Every scene a renegotiation

The realistic target is two domains, occasionally three, and the allocation depends on the book and on the scene’s position. A thriller weights plot and may serve character mainly through action; a novel of consciousness may run for pages with the plot idle; a final scene usually has to close in several domains at once. The domains stay constant, while the allocation is chosen for each genre and position.
