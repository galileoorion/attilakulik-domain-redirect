Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

2. Definitions

Continue in Reference

The working definition

In this chapter “scene” has a narrow sense, taken from Dwight V. Swain’s Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965). A scene is a unit of dramatised action in which a viewpoint character pursues a concrete, immediate goal, meets conflict, and ends in disaster: worse off than at the start, or successful at a cost that exceeds the gain. A sequel is the unit that follows it, in which the same character registers the disaster (reaction), weighs options none of which is good (dilemma), and commits to a new concrete objective (decision), which becomes the following scene’s goal. Swain calls the six functions beats. The word keeps that sense here; in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1), it also denotes the smallest unit of change.

Four clauses of the definition carry its weight. Viewpoint character: the model follows one consciousness at a time, which is why a novel with several viewpoints runs several chains side by side. Concrete, immediate: “get Dov to say why the locks were changed” is a scene goal, because it has an addressee, an occasion and a test of success, while “understand my father” is a theme, which no one can pursue in a room. Worse off: a disaster is defined by its job, which is to generate a sequel, and not by its size; a raised eyebrow will do. Becomes the following scene’s goal: this is the clause the model exists for. Without it the six beats describe one unit; with it they explain how units connect.

No characteristic length

Neither half has a typical length. A scene may be a sentence or a hundred pages. A sequel may be a single clause, she did not wait to see whether he would follow, or five of the six parts of Crime and Punishment. The beats are functions rather than paragraphs: a beat is a job done, and a subordinate clause can do it. Nor does the model predict chapters. A chapter may hold three complete pairs, half a pair, or a sequel alone, and where the beats fall and where the breaks fall are separate decisions that drafts often run together.

Which “scene”

Craft conversation uses the word “scene” in at least four senses, and the table separates them.

Sense Meaning Where it is used
Loose Any continuous stretch of dramatised action The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1), and most workshop talk
Narrow The goal–conflict–disaster half of a pair This chapter
Duration Narrative time roughly equal to story time, as against summary and ellipsis (Gérard Genette’s sense) the continuum of speeds in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1); The Transition (Chapter 5)
Dramaturgical A division of an act, marked by entrances and exits Stage and screen practice

This chapter uses the narrow sense throughout and marks the loose one where it needs it. The duration sense is the one to keep apart with most care, because it measures speed rather than function: a scene in the narrow sense may contain summary, and a passage told at full duration may contain no goal at all.

“Sequel”, likewise, never means a follow-up volume in this chapter; this book calls a later volume a series instalment. Swain’s word is kept because the model travels under it and because the alternatives mislead. Some later manuals say “aftermath”, which implies passivity, when a sequel’s job is to produce a decision.

Adjacent terms

Swain also describes a unit at the scale of the sentence, the motivation-reaction unit: an outside stimulus, presented objectively, followed by the character’s response in a prescribed order of feeling, reflex, action and speech. The fixed order reads today as a period style. The principle beneath it is the chain at the scale of the paragraph: nothing should arrive without a visible cause just before it.

Two terms from Aristotle’s Poetics are the classical ancestors of the disaster. Peripeteia is a reversal of fortune arising out of the action itself; anagnorisis is recognition, a passage from ignorance to knowledge. Seymour Chatman’s distinction, used in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1), between a kernel (an event that opens alternatives the story must choose between) and a satellite (an event that elaborates without branching) also maps onto the model: a disaster is nearly always a kernel, and much of a reaction is satellite material. An obligatory scene is a confrontation the chain has promised far in advance and must deliver, which is the ending’s business. A deferred decision, treated under the mechanism, is a sequel whose decision is withheld across a break and paid later.

What this chapter leaves to other chapters

What a scene is for (the value shift, the five domains, the economy of doing several jobs at once) belongs to The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1). How the gap between two units is crossed belongs to The Transition (Chapter 5), and where the gaps fall to The Chapter Break (Chapter 6). Whose chain the reader follows after a change of viewpoint is the question of The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), chains that run in the past belong to Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7), and how a second, parallel chain earns its pages belongs to The Subplot (Chapter 14). Change in a character across a whole book is the subject of Arc and Theme (Chapter 12), which takes up this chapter’s hinge at that scale.
