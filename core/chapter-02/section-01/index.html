Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

1. The Problem: Busy and Static

Continue in Reference

One complaint, two opposite drafts

A reader’s report that “nothing is happening” is one of the least useful things a writer can be told, because two opposite conditions produce it, and the remedy for each makes the other worse.

The first draft is full of incident. In eighty pages there is an arrest, a flood, a confession and a fire. Each is competently written, each is over within four pages, and then the next chapter opens somewhere else with someone doing something new. The reader says the book is busy and that nothing is happening. What they mean is that nothing lands: events arrive faster than the reader can convert them into consequence, and consequence is the form in which a reader stores an event.

The second draft is full of interiority. Its protagonist suffered a real catastrophe on page nine and has been considering it ever since, in sentences that are the best the writer has produced. The reader says the book is beautiful and that nothing is happening. What they mean is that nothing is due: the character weighs options and never commits, and endless weighing is not deliberation but scenery with a mind in it.

The first draft needs its catastrophes absorbed; the second needs its absorption to end in a decision. A vocabulary that calls both of them pacing problems cannot tell them apart, and the craft language in common use is lopsided in a way that makes the second harder to see. For the active half of narrative there are many words: conflict, obstacle, stakes, reversal, climax. For the reactive half there are few, and they are faintly dismissive: reflection, downtime, the quiet bit. The imbalance encourages a writer to treat aftermath as the gap between the interesting parts, when the aftermath is where a novel converts what happened into what a person will do next.

The same fault in one paragraph

The household novel used throughout this book shows the fault at the scale of a paragraph. Elena has driven across town to her brother Dov’s front door and learned there that their mother changed the shop’s locks in March. Dov gives her the new key and withholds the reason, an exchange The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) works in detail. The first draft of what follows reads, in part: She was angry with Dov, but there was no point arguing with him when he got like that, and anyway she had what she’d come for. She then drives to the shop, lets herself in and starts on the register drawer.

Every event is in place, and the paragraph is inert. The reader cannot feel why Elena goes to the shop rather than anywhere else, because the paragraph has priced the doorway as a success (she had what she came for), registered its cost with a single named emotion, and dismissed the alternative of asking their mother in six words. The doorway scene is sound and so is the scene at the drawer. The fault lies in the join between them, and the join is this chapter’s subject. The worked analyses diagnose the paragraph and revise it.

The claim

The chapter’s governing claim has two halves.

A story is not a sequence of things that happened. It is a chain of things that had to happen next — and “had to” is a relation the reader constructs, not a property the text possesses.

The first sentence is the model’s promise. A reader experiences two units as connected when the second unit’s goal descends from a decision made at the end of the first, and when each unit ends in a setback that the next must absorb. The second sentence is its qualification. Readers supply causes readily, often on evidence a logician would refuse, so the feeling of necessity can be produced by other means than the six beats this chapter describes: by pattern, by a recurring image, by plain adjacency. It can also be refused on purpose, as the picaresque and much modernist fiction refuse it. The six beats are the most teachable route to that feeling, which is a reason to learn them, and not the only route, which is a reason to use them as a diagnosis rather than a rule.

Where this chapter sits

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) asked whether a unit does a job, and answered in terms of the value shift (what is true at the end of a scene that was not true at its start) and five domains in which the shift can happen. This chapter asks a different question: does the unit’s job arise from the last one’s, and does it create the next one’s? The two tests are independent. A scene can pass the first and fail the second, as a well-made episode that could be moved fifty pages in either direction without damage. The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) would keep such a scene; this chapter asks what holds it in place.

Three later chapters build on the answer. The Transition (Chapter 5) handles how the gap between two units is crossed, and this chapter handles what must be true on either side of it for the crossing to feel necessary. The Chapter Break (Chapter 6) handles where the gap is placed, which matters most when a break falls on a decision not yet made. And The Ending (Chapter 16) inherits the chain whole, since an ending feels earned largely to the degree that a reader can trace it backwards.

See also: the Reference introduction; The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), which works with no accumulated context, as the ending works with nothing else.
