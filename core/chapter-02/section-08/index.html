Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a screen reading and the household revision case. The method is the same throughout: find the beats, name the chain structure, and judge the compression.

Crime and Punishment: the sequel that fills a novel

Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novel (1866; read here in the translation by Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky, 1992) either breaks the model or shows its widest application. Raskolnikov’s goal is formed and rehearsed through Part One. The conflict is his own body, the staircase, the porter, and the unexpected return of Lizaveta, the pawnbroker’s sister. The disaster, at the end of Part One, is a compounded failure of the most complete kind: he kills two women instead of one, takes almost nothing, and finds that the theory the act was meant to prove has not been proved.

Five parts and an epilogue remain, and nothing in them is a scene that closes the chain. Raskolnikov forms goals (evade the investigator Porfiry, find out what his friend Razumikhin knows, discover what Svidrigailov overheard), but every one is generated by his reaction to the murder, not by a decision that ended a completed sequel. The book is one sequel with scenes inside it, the structure the Reference calls the recursive sequel.

It does not stall because each part adds a new consideration to a dilemma (confess or persist) that stays fixed. Part Two brings the body’s refusal to obey the theory; Part Three, Porfiry, who has read Raskolnikov’s article and states its theory aloud; Part Four, Sonya, and the possibility that suffering is not defeat; Part Five, Katerina Ivanovna’s death; Part Six, Svidrigailov, the theory lived to its end, and his suicide. Five parts, five new terms, and the dilemma is never reweighed in the same words, because each part changes what confession would mean. Where the hinge falls is a real critical question: Sonya’s reading of the raising of Lazarus, the confession to Sonya, and Svidrigailov’s suicide are all candidates, and the epilogue insists that the deeper change comes after the novel ends.

The verdict is double. The six beats are all present and in order across six hundred pages, which shows that the model says nothing about duration. And the model cannot say why five hundred pages of a fixed dilemma grip rather than bore; for that a reader needs Brooks on desire and delay, or simply the observation that the prose is doing what no diagram records.

Small Things Like These: the hinge

Claire Keegan’s novella (2021) is set in New Ross, County Wexford, during the months leading up to Christmas 1985, and it is almost entirely sequel. Bill Furlong, a coal and timber merchant with five daughters, makes an early-morning delivery to the convent and finds a girl shut in the coal shed, shivering. That is the disaster: knowledge he cannot unknow. It is compounded when the Mother Superior gives him tea and a Christmas card with money in it, and the girl is made to say she was hiding as a game.

The reaction is carried by routine and memory rather than named feeling: the deliveries continue, the town’s preparations for Christmas go on around him, and his own history keeps returning. His mother was an unmarried girl working for a Protestant widow, Mrs Wilson, who kept her on and raised the boy in her house. The dilemma is priced by the people around him rather than argued by him. His wife, Eileen, says it is not their business and that they have their own daughters to think of; Mrs Kehoe warns him to keep quiet about the convent, reminding him how far the nuns’ influence reaches. Every cost of acting is stated by someone else, and stated reasonably, which is what makes the dilemma real.

On Christmas Eve he goes back, finds the girl, Sarah, in the coal shed again, and walks her through the town towards his home. The hinge is the change of criterion beneath that act. Until then he has chosen by what keeps his household safe; at the end he chooses by what Mrs Wilson did for his mother, and the old criterion is outranked rather than refuted, since none of its warnings has been shown to be wrong. Keegan ends the book before the costs arrive. The decision is concrete and irreversible, and the reader is left holding the dilemma’s prices, unpaid. A sequel-dominant book of about a hundred pages keeps its momentum because every memory adds a consideration: this is the yield that separates a sustained sequel from a stalled one.

The Martian: the clean chain made literal

Andy Weir’s novel was first published by its author in 2011 and by Crown in 2014. Its structure is the clean chain in log form. Mark Watney, left for dead on Mars when his crew evacuates in a storm, records each day’s goal, works against physics, meets a disaster, and logs a compressed sequel before the next goal. The largest disaster is characteristic: the habitat’s canvas tears at an airlock, the Hab depressurises, and the potato crop on which his survival depends is killed. The sequel is a joke, a calculation and a plan, and the plan is the next goal.

The comedy is structural. Watney’s wisecrack is the reaction beat compressed to a line, which keeps the sequels short enough for a thriller’s register while still registering the cost. The dilemma becomes arithmetic (calories against days), and the decision is an engineering procedure the reader can follow. A second chain, on Earth, is braided with the first, and its decisions are generated by Watney’s disasters. What the book lacks is a hinge: Watney’s criterion, to stay alive by solving the next problem, never changes. The book does not need one, because its pleasure is competence under pressure, and a reader who finds its people flat is naming the cost of that choice rather than a fault.

A screen reading: Mad Max: Fury Road

George Miller’s film (2015) is nearly all scene in the narrow sense, a pursuit across the desert, and screen has no cheap interiority, since a reaction that prose gives in a sentence costs a held shot and a performance. The film spends that cost once. Furiosa learns from the Vuvalini, the women of her own clan, that the Green Place she has been driving towards is the poisoned swamp the convoy crossed the night before. She walks alone into the dunes, falls to her knees and screams, in a long shot with no dialogue: the reaction beat, priced by the only stillness in the film. The dilemma follows, with the group resolving to ride out across the salt flats on the chance of something beyond them. The decision is made by someone else. Max, who has parted from them, rides after them and proposes the opposite course: to go back to the Citadel, now undefended, which has water and crops. The decision reverses the goal the whole film has pursued, from flight to return, and it is a hinge for the group, whose criterion changes from escape to taking back. It is also a decision displaced onto a character other than the one whose disaster it answers.

The household revision case: Elena after the doorway

The pair follows the doorway exchange worked in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3). Elena learned there that their mother had changed the shop’s locks, and left wanting to know why; Dov gave her the new key and withheld the reason. The draft of the sequel reads as follows.

Version A (draft)

Elena left with the key. She was angry with Dov, but there was no point arguing with him when he got like that, and anyway she had what she’d come for. She got into the truck and drove across town to the shop, thinking about what her mother might say if she asked her directly. She decided it wasn’t worth it. Better to look for herself. She parked outside, let herself in, and started with the register drawer.

Diagnosis

The tests locate the faults in the join rather than in either scene. Test 3 (Disaster) finds the disaster weakened: “she had what she’d come for” prices the doorway as a win, and the cost (she trusts her brother less) is asserted by “angry” and never spent. Test 4 (Cost) fails, since no currency is named. Test 5 (Chain) finds the chain intact only in its connectives: but, anyway, better to. Test 7 (Dilemma) finds a false dilemma, the call to their mother dismissed in six words on grounds of convenience. Test 10 (Beat weight) finds five words of reaction, all of them a named emotion, twenty-three of dilemma, and a decision that is logistics rather than choice. The patterns present are the skipped sequel, the false dilemma and the named emotion. Every necessary item is in Version A; its fault is arrangement, since the sequel has been summarised rather than performed.

Version B (revision)

She didn’t cry in the truck, is what she’d tell people later, though she also couldn’t say what she’d done instead: some effortful blank thing with her hands on the wheel, the key already leaving a mark in her palm because she hadn’t unclenched.

She could call their mother and ask outright, and get the same answer Dov had given her, or worse, the silence that meant she’d asked something true. Or she could go through the ledger herself, quietly, and risk finding out Dov’s silence in the doorway hadn’t been protecting their mother. It might have been protecting her.

She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K.

The revision restores the reaction and renders it in the body: the unclenched hand and the key’s mark in the palm replace “angry”. Is what she’d tell people later installs the narration’s small retrospective gap, which lets it see past Elena’s account of herself without leaving her head; the book keeps that gap throughout. Both options are priced: calling risks the answer, and looking risks learning that Dov’s silence was protective, and a sympathetic reader could prefer either. The real disaster moves from the withheld reason to its interpretation, since a brother who is protecting her is worse than a brother who is obstructing her; the costed success has become a poisoned one. The decision is executed as it is made, she drove to the shop, and the drawer that sticks on the K, planted in the prologue drafted in The Prologue (Chapter 10), gives the next scene its first move. The beat weights are set deliberately low (reaction 44 words, dilemma 56, decision 27) against a moderate disaster, leaving room above for the heavier sequels Dov’s sale of the shop will need.

What Version B has not solved

Three things remain, and the six beats cannot see any of them. The decision is reversible: nothing forecloses calling their mother later, so the passage runs on momentum rather than on a closed door. There is no hinge: Elena’s criterion (find out for yourself, do not ask people who will not tell you) is the one she held in the doorway, which is right for an early chapter, but a book made of such pairs would earn the treadmill diagnosis. And the dilemma’s second option is more interesting than its first, which risks the lopsidedness the false-dilemma test warns against; whether It might have been protecting her is a turn or a thumb on the scale is an editorial disagreement worth having.
