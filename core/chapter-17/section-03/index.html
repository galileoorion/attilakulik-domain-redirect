Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

3. How It Works

Continue in Reference

The theory of genre and series answers four practical questions. What does a reader expect before she has read a word? Why can a familiar pattern please rather than bore her? How far can a book depart from that pattern and stay in its genre? What brings her from one volume to the next? Each account begins with the question it answers and then names its source.

What a reader brings: the horizon of expectations

The question. Two readers finish the same last chapter, and one is satisfied and the other cheated. What did they bring to the book that made the difference?

Hans Robert Jauss gave the answer a name in “Literary History as a Challenge to Literary Theory” (1967; translated by Timothy Bahti in Toward an Aesthetic of Reception, 1982). A reader meets a new work with a horizon of expectations formed partly by similar works she has read. She measures the new work against that horizon: it confirms, varies or breaks what she expected. Genre helps shape the horizon, as do the title, cover, shelf and reader’s own history before she opens the book. The two readers brought different horizons to the same last chapter, and the writer controlled neither.

Tzvetan Todorov’s “The Typology of Detective Fiction” (1966; translated by Richard Howard in The Poetics of Prose, 1977) shows how specific a horizon can be. The classic detective novel, he argued, contains two stories: the story of the crime, which is over before the book begins, and the story of the investigation, whose whole task is to recover the first. A reader who recognises the form expects the second story to end when the first is fully told. That expectation is the mystery’s core promise, and it is a promise about structure, not about corpses.

Why a formula pleases: repetition and its variations

The question. A reader who has read forty novels with the same hero picks up the forty-first. What is she paying for?

In Adventure, Mystery, and Romance (1976), John Cawelti called the familiar pattern a formula: a conventional story structure that a culture’s readers know. He argued that it gives two pleasures at once, the security of a known shape and the interest of a new handling. Umberto Eco made the same point about series. In “The Myth of Superman” (1962; translated by Natalie Chilton, 1972), he described the comic-book hero’s iterative scheme. Each adventure begins again as if nothing had happened, so the hero cannot be used up. In “Innovation and Repetition” (1985), Eco argued that a serial’s reader enjoys recognising the scheme and, if she knows the form, the variations within it. A writer can therefore use a formula without failing to invent: the book gives the reader the expected pleasure, while its distinction lies in what it varies.

How far a book can depart: semantics, syntax and mode

The question. A novel has a dragon, knights and a quest, and its author does not think it fantasy. Who is right, and does it matter?

In “A Semantic/Syntactic Approach to Film Genre” (1984), Altman separated a genre’s building blocks from the relations among them. He argued that genres change when familiar syntax takes new semantics, or familiar semantics take a new syntax. Alastair Fowler’s Kinds of Literature (1982) likewise distinguished a kind, a genre with its full set of features, from a mode, a selection of those features carried into other works. Together, the distinctions answer the question about the dragon. A novel with fantasy’s semantic elements and another genre’s syntax uses fantasy as a mode. Readers will judge the novel by the promises its syntax makes, whether or not the author calls it fantasy. The Mechanism explains how the book can make those promises clear early.

What holds a reader between volumes

The question. A reader finishes a volume and waits a year for the next. What has she been promised, and what does she remember?

In “Innovation and Repetition”, Eco distinguished the series, in which a fixed situation recurs and each work is complete, from the saga, in which characters age and the story accumulates. Jason Mittell gives the television version in Complex TV (2015): an episodic series resets its world each episode, while a serial carries consequences forward. Much contemporary television combines the forms, closing a case each week while a story runs across the season. Each form promises something different about what one volume will close or leave open. This chapter has not measured what readers remember between volumes. Like The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) and The Subplot (Chapter 14), it assumes that readers keep a story’s emotional shape better than its mechanics.

The evidence, and the convention

Rolf Zwaan’s “Effect of Genre Expectations on Text Comprehension” (1994) gave readers the same passages, telling some they were reading literature and others that they were reading news. According to the summaries the chapter has used, readers told they were reading literature read more slowly, remembered more of the wording and built a weaker model of the situation described: an expectation of kind changed how the same words were read. The study used short texts and two broad categories, and the chapter draws a modest conclusion from it: what a reader believes she is reading changes what she attends to, before any convention is kept or broken.

Genres are historical. The detective novel’s rules of fair play were codified in the late 1920s, and the romance’s requirement of an optimistic ending is a definition its own writers’ association adopted. Claims about which genres sell most, or about how readers of one genre regard another, the chapter treats as impressions, and it treats no genre as more serious than another: literary fiction has conventions and a core promise of its own, set out in The Taxonomy.
