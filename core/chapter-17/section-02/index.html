Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

2. Definitions

Continue in Reference

The working definition

The word genre has two senses. It can mean a group of works that readers, writers and booksellers treat as alike. This chapter uses its other sense: the expectations a reader brings to a book she identifies with such a group. Readers recognise a genre by its conventions, the features they expect. The film scholar Rick Altman divides those conventions into two kinds. Semantic elements are the genre’s building blocks: places, objects, figures and kinds of event, such as the body in the library, the spaceship and the ballroom. Syntactic elements are the relations among them: who wants what of whom, and the order in which things happen. An investigator sets out to identify a killer; a barrier keeps two people apart until it falls. A book can use a genre’s semantic elements without its syntax, or its syntax with other semantic elements. Much of what this chapter diagnoses lies in the gap between the two.

One convention is decisive. The core promise is the convention whose breach readers of the genre treat as a broken contract rather than a choice: the mystery’s answer to its question of fact, or the romance’s union of the lovers. A book can vary every other convention and still belong to its genre if it keeps the core promise. If it breaks that promise, many readers will feel that the book changed genre without telling them.

A series is a sequence of separately published works that share characters, a world or a continuing story. Readers are invited to read the works as related, and each work is a volume. Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2) reserves sequel for the reaction, dilemma and decision that follow a scene. This book therefore calls a later volume a series instalment. Each volume has a volume question, the question it must close. A series may also have a series question that runs across volumes and may close only in the last volume.

Adjacent terms

Several neighbouring terms belong to other chapters and are used here in the senses those chapters give them. The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) sets out five contracts that an opening makes, and one of them is the genre contract: what kind of book this is, and therefore what to expect, hope for and fear. This chapter follows the contract past the opening to the whole book and its ending. The series hook belongs to The Ending (Chapter 16), which defines it as a major question deliberately left open to create an appetite for a further volume. The declined reveal belongs to The Reveal (Chapter 15), where it is a question of fact that a book holds visibly open to its end. Arc and Theme (Chapter 12) names the steadfast arc, in which a character’s criterion holds and the change happens to others, and calls it the usual arc of a series hero.

Alastair Fowler distinguished two ways in which a book can take up a genre. A kind is a genre in full: a mystery belongs to the mystery kind, with its conventions and its core promise, an answer to the question of fact it poses. A mode is a selection of a kind’s conventions carried into a book of another kind. Fowler’s name for it comes from grammar, because the genre’s name stops being a noun and becomes an adjective: the book is not a mystery but a family novel in the mystery mode. The household novel is such a book. It borrows the mystery’s investigator, its changed lock and its document in a changed hand, but it remains a family novel, and it never undertakes to say why the mother changed the locks. The risk is that a mode carries a genre’s conventions without, by default, its core promise, while readers recognise the conventions and may assume the promise came with them. A reader who takes the household novel for a mystery waits for an answer the book never meant to give, and reads its silence as the writer’s failure.

What the chapter leaves to other chapters

The genre signals of the first pages belong to The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), and the genre test for a prologue to The Prologue (Chapter 10); this chapter asks what the whole book then owes. When one novel was published in instalments, as Dickens’s were, each instalment ended at a chapter break within one story, and those breaks belong to The Chapter Break (Chapter 6). The Ending (Chapter 16) treats the last line of a series and the retroactive lens through which that line recasts every volume; this chapter discusses only what the final volume must carry. Arrangements of time within a volume belong to Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8); this chapter takes the order of volumes, and the genres that expect a dual timeline. This book leaves out market categories, publishing economics and the length a genre’s readers expect, except where a length changes a structure.
