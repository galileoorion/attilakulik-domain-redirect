Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three readings show how a book can change a reader’s expectations: with an early comic signal, a partial warning, or a question of fact left open across four volumes. A screen reading shows how a second-season title sequence made a series’ refusal especially clear. The household case follows.

Jane Austen, Northanger Abbey (published 1817): a comic signal on its first page

Austen drafted Northanger Abbey in the late 1790s, when Gothic fiction was popular, and it was published in December 1817. Its first sentence invokes heroic conventions while signalling comic distance from them: No one who had ever seen Catherine Morland in her infancy would have supposed her born to be an heroine. The Gothic parody becomes explicit as Catherine reads Gothic novels and projects their plots onto the abbey. Her reading shapes her expectations, giving the reader a comedy while the romance’s promise of union runs beneath it.

That early comic framing lets Austen invoke the conventions at full strength. Catherine reads Ann Radcliffe’s The Mysteries of Udolpho at Bath and arrives at the Tilneys’ abbey expecting the gothic’s syntax: a locked cabinet, a hidden manuscript, a tyrannical father, a dead wife. Every sign is honoured and every payment refused. The manuscript in the cabinet is an inventory of linen; the late Mrs Tilney died of an illness, and Catherine’s suspicion that the General murdered or imprisoned her earns a rebuke from Henry Tilney, who asks her to remember the country and the age in which they live.

The variation comes afterwards. The General turns Catherine out of the house and sends her home alone, because he has learned she is not the heiress he had supposed, and Catherine concludes that in suspecting him she had scarcely sinned against his character, or magnified his cruelty. The gothic’s exposure, the tyrant found out, is kept in a domestic key: the villain is real and his crime is money. The romance’s promise is kept too, and Henry marries her. The first sentence prepares the reader for a comedy of literary expectations, though the specific Gothic parody is established later; the book’s distinction lies in the conventions it varies.

Tana French, In the Woods (2007): a promise kept and a promise declined

French’s first novel, which won the Edgar Award for best first novel, is a police procedural with two questions. In 1984, three children entered the woods at Knocknaree outside Dublin. One came out and was found clinging to a tree, with blood in his shoes and no memory; the other two were never found. Twenty years later, the survivor, Rob Ryan, has become a detective. With his partner Cassie Maddox, he investigates the murder of twelve-year-old Katy Devlin, whose body was found on an archaeological dig beside the same woods.

The volume question, who killed Katy, is answered by the genre’s terms: a man working on the dig confesses, and the book shows that Katy’s elder sister manipulated him. The second question, what happened in 1984, is the one the book has made personal, since Rob narrates it as the wound of his life, and it is never answered. Readers divided over the ending, and many said they felt cheated.

The promise inventory shows why readers divide. The procedural makes its core promise twice, once for each case, but answers only one question. It half declares the other decline. At the start, Rob warns the reader, I crave truth. And I lie. His warning invites doubt about the narrator but does not say that the past will remain closed. Place and evidence found near Katy’s body also link the cases, inviting the reader to expect one solution. Readers who approach the book as a novel of trauma may accept its replacement promise: Rob cannot recover the past. Readers who approach it as a procedural may stake the book on the link between the cases. The series form confirms the decline afterwards. French’s second novel, The Likeness (2008), is narrated by Cassie, and the Dublin Murder Squad became a relay series. No later volume carries the 1984 question, so the book did not defer its answer.

Elena Ferrante, the Neapolitan novels (2011–14; translated by Ann Goldstein, 2012–15): a serial with a declined frame

The four novels, My Brilliant Friend, The Story of a New Name, Those Who Leave and Those Who Stay and The Story of the Lost Child, form a serial. Elena Greco tells one story across sixty years of her friendship with Lila Cerullo. The first volume’s prologue sets the frame: Lila’s son calls Elena, now in her sixties like Lila, to say his mother has gone. Angry, Elena decides to write down everything she can remember. The opening declares the series question and declines its plainest form. The quartet never answers where Lila has gone; it pursues what Lila was.

Each volume closes a stage of the story. My Brilliant Friend ends at Lila’s wedding feast. Marcello Solara, the man she refused, arrives wearing the shoes she designed. His appearance shows that her husband has made his peace, and his business, with the Solaras. The volume answers its question there: Lila’s marriage will not free her from the neighbourhood’s terms. Its last page also opens the next stage, as a serial’s volume ending must. A reader who stops after the first volume has been given a whole book.

The final volume shows what a serial owes at its end. Lila’s daughter Tina vanishes in 1984 and is never found, leaving a second question of fact unanswered. The quartet ends when Elena receives a package holding the two dolls the girls lost as children in the first volume. Across four books, the dolls serve as a bridging object in the sense Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) gives the term. The book does not say whether the dolls are an insult or a reassurance. The serial keeps its promise of a whole account of a friendship while declining to answer the two questions of fact it never promised to settle.

A screen reading: The Leftovers (HBO, 2014–17)

Adapted from Tom Perrotta’s 2011 novel, Damon Lindelof and Perrotta’s series begins three years after two per cent of the world’s population vanished at once on 14 October. The premise invites a mystery reading, but the series focuses from the start on those left behind and never explains the departure. In its second season, the title sequence changed to Iris DeMent’s song “Let the Mystery Be”. That signal appears at the head of the second season’s episodes as part of the series’ own paratext. In the finale, one character offers an account of where the departed went that nobody can check. The man she tells says he believes her. Each season advances the series question, how people live beside an unexplained event, while keeping the declined question in view through the last scene.

The household revision case: a hook for a second book

The situation. The household novel’s present runs from about four months after the father’s death in March to the end of July. At the first chapter break, Elena learns in Dov’s doorway that their mother changed the locks. Dov will not say why. Just before the first quarter of the book, Elena considers ringing their mother from the truck but foresees the silence that meant she’d asked something true. She drives to the shop instead. At about 38 per cent of the book, she finds the register drawer empty and decides not to ask Dov who took the ledger, because she had asked him once. At the convergence, she rings Nadia rather than their mother. The three read the ledger in the shop, and Nadia goes up and puts on the coat. The locks are never explained.

Version A, the draft. The agent’s two additions from The Problem: a last page on which the mother rings and Elena answers, and jacket copy promising that one of the children will find out why the locks were changed.

Diagnosis. Test 1 (Shelf) fails at both ends: the jacket presents the book as a mystery, and the added last page ends as one, while the novel between them is a family story in the mystery mode. Test 3 (Declared decline) finds the decisive, unusual failure. The book had declared that it would leave the question of the locks open, but the added last page takes that declaration back. Readers who understood the doorway, truck and empty drawer as signs of a decline are told they were wrong. Test 5 (Volume question) fails because the hook spends the last page on a question and uses the ending’s privileged position to propose a further book. Test 6 (Series step) finds no series question to advance. A second volume could answer the question of the locks only through the mother’s own account, which the book’s meaning and canon forbid.

Version B, the revision. Cut the last page, so that the book ends where it did, with Nadia and the coat, and the mother’s silence stays in view. Replace the jacket’s question with a promise the book keeps: Four months after their father’s death, Elena finds that her key no longer fits the shop, and that someone else has been writing in his ledger. The promise inventory shows that the text needs nothing more:

Convention Genre First invoked Kept, varied or declined Where shown
A question of fact posed early: why the locks Mystery The doorway, at the first chapter break Declined The truck; the empty drawer at 38% of the book; the call to Nadia; the ending
The investigator Mystery The doorway; the truck Varied: she investigates by reading and decides not to ask The empty drawer
The document in a changed hand Mystery The drawer, about 25% of the book Kept: whose hand Dov’s chapter, about 78% of the book; the convergence
The suspect: who took the ledger Mystery The empty drawer, 38% of the book Kept The convergence
The secret of the dead: what the father knew Mystery, family novel The prologue’s In case Kept The reading
The gathering for the solution Mystery — Varied: three read one record, and nobody explains The ending

The declined row has a sign at intervals from the first chapter break to the last page, and the replacement promise, what these three do with what they can reconstruct, is kept by the gathering in the last row. The book’s genre was declared by three decisions not to ask, and the revision removes the two additions that contradicted them.

What the revision has not solved. The signs of decline work cumulatively. Only in retrospect does the first sign, in the truck, clearly read as a refusal to answer. A reader expecting a mystery may therefore still hold the question of the locks to the end and feel owed an answer. The writer does not control the jacket, so the book must declare its genre for itself. A mystery’s horizon also turns details into apparent clues: the boy at the glass, the sign left at OPEN and the coat brushed each week by someone the book never names. The brushing poses a second question of fact that the book declines without a sign. The writer can only decline the agent’s request for another volume: this book has no series question that would not spend its own.
