Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

The household novel has found an agent, and the agent has two requests. The book is a quiet family novel set in a small British town: four months after their father’s death in March, his three adult children reconstruct what he left. Elena learns in Dov’s doorway that their mother changed the shop’s locks in March, and Dov gives her the new key and keeps the reason; Nadia counts things and will not touch the coat that still hangs in the flat above the shop; the father’s ledger, kept for eleven years in a careful slanting hand, stops on 9 March, and a looser hand continues it. At the end the three read the ledger together in the shop, learn that their father was ill and wrote it down and told nobody, and Nadia goes up to the flat and puts on the coat. Their mother’s reason for changing the locks is never given.

The agent likes the book and wants a second one. Her first request is a last page that opens the door to it. The draft now ends, after the coat, with a short scene in the shop:

Elena was locking up when the phone lit on the counter. MA, it said. She let it ring twice, and answered.

Her second request is jacket copy that tells a browser what kind of book this is. The draft copy reads: In March their father died, and their mother changed the locks. Four months later, one of his children is going to find out why.

Neither request is foolish. Series sell, a family with a living mother who has never spoken has room for another book, and a jacket that asks a question is doing what jackets do. The trouble is that both make promises the book spends its whole length declining. The copy tells the reader she is holding a mystery, a kind of book whose readers are guaranteed an answer to the question it poses. The book poses that question in its first chapter and never answers it, and it says so, through a brother who will not answer, a sister who decides not to ask, and an ending that closes every other question around the one it keeps. A reader who takes the copy at its word will read the book’s refusals as the writer’s failures. The last page then turns the refusal into a postponement: the mother rings, and the question the book had declined becomes the question of the next one, which means that this book did not end but stopped.

The case is not an argument against series or against borrowing a genre’s machinery. The household novel borrows a great deal from the mystery: an investigator, a changed lock, a document in a changed hand, a suspect, a gathering at the end. It is an instance of a confusion the chapter separates into its parts, between a convention a book uses and a promise it makes, and between a question a book declines and one it defers.

The chapter’s claim is that genre and series are both contracts with the reader, made partly outside the book, and that a book can keep, vary or decline any term of either, provided the reader is told in time.

A genre is a set of promises the reader brings to a book, and a series a set of promises she carries out of it. A book keeps faith with both when every convention it invokes is either kept or visibly declined before the reader has staked much on it, and when each volume closes the question it was built on and leaves open only questions it has shown to belong to the series. A book that breaks a promise it never declined, or spends its last page on a question it owed, has borrowed against a contract it does not mean to keep.

The claim has a consequence for revision. A book whose readers feel cheated by its ending is seldom mended at the ending. The repair is usually earlier, where the promise was made or should have been declined, and sometimes outside the text, in what the jacket says. The revision in Worked Analyses removes the last page and the question on the jacket, and finds that the book’s genre is already declared, by three decisions not to ask.
