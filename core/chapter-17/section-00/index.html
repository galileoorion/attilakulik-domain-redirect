Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

Series and Genre

Continue in Reference

Promises the reader brings to a book, and promises she carries out of it

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 17. Part IV: Beyond the Book and Into Practice.

In brief. A genre is a set of promises a reader holds before the first page; a series is a set of promises she carries past the last. A book keeps faith with both when a central promise it invokes is kept or visibly declined before the reader has staked much on it, and when each series volume closes the question or stage its form promises to close. The promise inventory lists each convention a book invokes, where it first appears, and whether the book keeps, varies or declines it. The volume table records each volume’s own question, any step on a continuing series question and the threads it carries. Use the chapter when early readers say a book was not what they expected, when a draft borrows a genre’s machinery without its payments, or when you are planning a second volume or have been asked to write one.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Name the core promise of the genre a book declares, and say where the book keeps it.
  2. List the conventions a draft invokes and decide, for each, whether to keep, vary or decline it.
  3. Declare a decline early enough, and replace it with a promise the book keeps, so that the reader takes the decline as design.
  4. Give each volume of a series a question it closes, a step on the series question and an entry a new reader can use.
  5. Diagnose an undeclared decline, a genre in costume, a volume that owes its question, a deferred answer and a recap at the door, and revise them.

Contents

The chapter begins with the promises of genre and series and how readers hold them. It then shows how a book keeps, varies or declines those promises and how a series carries its questions from volume to volume, before turning to tests, analyses and exercises for use at the desk.

  1. The Problem
    A household draft that ends on a hook for a second book, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    Genre, convention, series and volume, and what other chapters keep
  3. How It Works
    How readers hold a genre’s promises, what a formula gives them, and what the evidence says
  4. The Taxonomy
    Five core promises and four forms of series
  5. The Mechanism
    How a promise is made, staked, kept or declined, and how a series carries its questions
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Eight questions, and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    Northanger Abbey, In the Woods, the Neapolitan novels, The Leftovers and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the model assumes, and why a convention is a promise rather than a rule

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests with a promise inventory drafted for the book as it stands. A writer planning a series should read the mechanism first, because a series question and the step each volume takes on it are easier to design than to find in the third book.

The full treatment

Because this chapter is new, a longer version expands the theory rather than correcting an earlier version. It also includes an extended catalogue of promises, series forms and failure patterns, the procedures for each test, further analyses (Bring Up the Bodies, Beach Read, 61 Hours and Twin Peaks), the household promise inventory in full, a glossary of the chapter’s terms and the sources.

See also: The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), for the genre contract made in the first pages; Arc and Theme (Chapter 12), for the arc a series hero carries; The Reveal (Chapter 15), for the declined reveal; The Ending (Chapter 16), for the series hook at a book’s last line.
