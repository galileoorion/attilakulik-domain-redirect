Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

A genre’s promise passes through three stages: the book makes it, the reader stakes attention on it, and the book keeps, varies or declines it. A series adds a fourth stage by carrying a promise into the next volume. Two instruments record these stages: the promise inventory for one book and the volume table for a series.

How a promise is made

A book signals a promise with semantic elements and confirms it through syntax. A body in the first chapter, a locked room or a stranger met in the rain is a sign. The promise takes shape when the book sets a relation in motion: an investigator seeks the killer, or a barrier keeps two people apart. Signs without that syntax make a mode, and the reader waits to learn which promise, if any, they serve. Syntax without familiar signs makes a variation: the reader recognises the relation and applies the promise in unfamiliar dress.

Some promises are made before the first page. In Paratexts (1987; translated by Jane E. Lewin, 1997), Gérard Genette named the paratext: material such as a title, cover, jacket copy or epigraph that presents a text. A bookseller’s shelf also cues readers but need not count as an authorial or publisher paratext in Genette’s sense. Writers may have limited control over these cues, and a publisher’s jacket may promise what the book does not. The text therefore needs to declare its genre within its first chapters, strongly enough to correct a misleading presentation the reader encountered before opening the book.

How a reader stakes, and when a decline is cheap

A reader stakes attention on a promise for as long as the book keeps it in view. The book can decline a question of fact cheaply if it signals the decline soon after raising the question. Keep that question before the reader for three hundred pages of clues and suspects, and the decline becomes costly: she has read every page in expectation of an answer. As a working estimate rather than a measured threshold, this chapter asks writers to make a decline of the core promise legible within the first quarter of the book. Readers who expect the promise may take a decline first signalled at the ending as a failure.

A declared decline has three parts. First, the book signals that it will leave the question open: a character will not answer, another chooses not to ask, a narrator says no answer is coming, or a frame gives away the end. Second, the book makes and keeps a replacement promise, usually a promise of significance as The Taxonomy defines it. Third, the declined question stays in view while the book closes its other questions around it. The Reveal (Chapter 15) asks the same of a declined reveal. Without an early sign, the decline is undeclared; without a replacement, it leaves a debt; without continued visibility, it looks like forgetfulness.

The promise inventory

The promise inventory lists the conventions a book invokes. Each row records a convention, its genre, where it first appears as a percentage of the text, whether the book keeps, varies or declines it, and where the result is shown. Build the inventory from the manuscript rather than the writer’s intentions. Its last column is the most useful: a declined convention with no entry there leaves readers waiting for a payment the book will not make. Worked Analyses gives the household novel’s inventory.

Variation is the third course, between keeping and declining. A book might keep the syntax but change the semantics, as in a romance set in a mortuary. It might keep the core promise but vary the obligatory scene, as when a declaration arrives by letter years late. Or it might keep the obligatory scene but give it to someone unexpected, as when the suspect exposes the answer. Readers can accept these variations while the core promise holds; a formula book may find its distinction in one of them.

How a series carries its questions

A series with a continuing arc carries two questions, and readers need to tell them apart. Each volume normally closes its own question at its climax, while the series question advances by a step the reader can name. A fully episodic series need not have a series question. If a volume leaves its own question open on the last page to bring the reader back, it uses the series hook described in The Ending (Chapter 16). Readers may then judge that volume by the answer it withheld. A series with a continuing arc can instead close the volume question and use the last page to advance the series question.

Threads that run across volumes go dormant between them. The gap lasts months or years for readers who follow the publication schedule, but may last less time for later readers. The Subplot (Chapter 14) sets limits on a thread’s dormancy within one book; between volumes, readers also feel the gap in their own time. A later volume therefore needs a re-entry that serves both returning readers, who may remember the story’s shape but not its mechanics, and new readers, who know neither. The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) gives the rule: deliver recap as present consequence rather than summary.

A series hero’s criterion must remain where the last volume left it, in the sense Arc and Theme (Chapter 12) gives the term. In an episodic series, the criterion generally stays stable and others change. In a series with a running arc, it moves a stage per volume, and each volume shows the move. The order of volumes is a further choice. A prequel is set before the other volumes: an analepsis at the scale of the series. Readers of the later-set volumes already know some outcomes, so the prequel needs a question those volumes leave open. Readers who start with the prequel lack that foreknowledge. This applies the strand question from Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) to a series.

The volume table

The volume table has one row per volume, written or planned, and seven columns. Record the volume question and where it closes; the series question and the step this volume takes on it, if any; the threads carried in and out and how many volumes they have been dormant; the hero’s criterion at the end; the re-entry device; whether the last page closes or hooks; and the form the volume implies. If two successive rows leave the series-step column empty despite a continuing question, the series may have stalled. If a row marks the last page as a hook while the volume question remains open, the volume may owe its reader a resolution.
