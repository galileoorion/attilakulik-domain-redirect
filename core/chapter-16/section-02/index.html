Chapter 16 · The Ending

2. Definitions

Continue in Reference

The working definition

The ending is the final unit of a novel. It is made of the climax, the last major confrontation or disaster, and the aftermath, everything between the climax and the last word. Two properties distinguish it from every other unit: nothing after it can correct, complicate or pay off what it delivers, and the reader rereads the whole book through it. The definition says nothing about resolution, satisfaction or happiness, which are things endings often do rather than what they are. It also makes the ending a stretch of text rather than a sentence: usually the last chapter or two, sometimes a whole part, as in Anna Karenina. An epilogue does not escape it, since an epilogue is part of the ending and not something after it.

Climax is not ending

The climax is the final disaster of the final goal–conflict–disaster sequence, in the sense Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2) gives the term. Aristotle’s Poetics divides a tragedy at its turning point into the desis, the tying of the knot, and the lusis, the unravelling, and on that account the aftermath is not an appendix but half the structure. The climax discharges the accumulated conflict and usually serves plot and catharsis; the aftermath establishes what the discharge means and usually serves character, theme and the final image. A book without a climax has no engine. A book without an aftermath has had its largest value shift without giving the reader a moment of lower pressure in which to register it.

The most useful way to think about the aftermath is as the novel’s own sequel, in Scene and Sequel’s sense of the word (Chapter 2): reaction, dilemma, decision, applied to the book. That explains a pattern every editor knows. An aftermath consisting only of reaction, a character grieving for thirty pages, feels indulgent; an aftermath that reaches a decision, however small, feels complete.

Resolution is not closure

The second distinction does more work and is named less often. Resolution is a property of the plot: the book’s questions are answered, or legibly and deliberately left open, and a reader can list them and check. Closure is a property of the reader: the felt sense that the book has arrived rather than stopped, which can be reported but not checked. The two come apart in both directions, and all four combinations occur in published fiction, as the table shows.

Closure felt Closure absent
Resolved The classical settlement: Jane Eyre, Emma The hollow tidy ending: every question answered and nothing felt
Unresolved The open close that closes: The Handmaid’s Tale, Beloved The deliberate refusal: Kafka’s unfinished The Castle, read as though finished

Only the top-right cell is a failure by default; the other three are choices. The table also explains a common revision mistake. A writer who diagnoses a flat ending as a resolution problem and answers more questions usually makes it flatter, because the mechanisms that produce closure are largely formal rather than informational. A Passage to India (1924) answers nothing on its last page: Aziz and Fielding cannot be friends, and the earth and sky say No, not yet and No, not there. The reader still feels the book is finished, because the refusal is stated in the book’s own terms, the failure of connection, and the sentence is a negation with nothing possible after it.

Adjacent terms

Several neighbouring words are used loosely. A denouement (French, “untying”) implies that entangled facts are being separated, which is a nineteenth-century plot’s aftermath; a modern aftermath often unties nothing and consists only of consequence, so this chapter prefers the neutral aftermath. An epilogue is displaced from the narrative along an axis of time, voice or vantage, as The Prologue (Chapter 10) describes a prologue, and must justify its exteriority; a coda, borrowed from music, is continuous with what precedes it and changes only its key, so it need justify only its length. The last line is the final sentence considered as a citable unit. Apparatus is the notes, appendices and other matter that follow a narrative, and can close a book on its own account. A series hook is a deliberate non-closure designed to create an appetite for a further volume; Failure Patterns and Series and Genre (Chapter 17) treat it.

What the chapter leaves to other chapters

A chapter break offers the reader a place to stop and the ending offers the place they must, so the temporary stopping place belongs to The Chapter Break (Chapter 6). The epilogue as a separated unit belongs to The Prologue (Chapter 10), the structure of the climactic scene itself to The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) and Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), and the convergence of threads to The Subplot (Chapter 14). The reveal that recasts a book belongs to The Reveal (Chapter 15); the arc and the theme the ending completes are traced across the whole book in Arc and Theme (Chapter 12); the last volume of a series belongs to Series and Genre (Chapter 17); and the order in which to revise a whole book, ending last, belongs to The Structural Edit (Chapter 18). Unfinished novels such as The Mystery of Edwin Drood fall outside the chapter, since a text with no ending cannot be analysed as having a bad one.
