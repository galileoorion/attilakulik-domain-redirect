Chapter 16 · The Ending

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern fails through a mechanism rather than a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent. At the ending the exceptions carry more weight than in any other chapter, since several of the patterns are committed by endings that are among the most admired in the language. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue has twelve patterns, among them the climax without aftermath, the endless aftermath, the mismatched final note, the ending that resolves the wrong question and the last-minute reversal.

The unearned rescue

Symptom. Resolution arrives through something the book never integrated: a sudden power, a stranger’s intervention, a document that appears, a fact withheld from the reader until it is needed.

Mechanism. The causal chain breaks at its last link. The objection, as Aristotle saw, is to externality rather than improbability: if the outcome came from outside the system the book built, the book did not determine its own ending, and the retroactive lens applies that verdict to every page. A second harm follows. A rescued protagonist has not acted, and since character is legible mainly in choice under pressure, the rescue dissolves the character domain when it falls due. Writers produce the pattern when they have painted the situation into a corner, or, most often, when they have not noticed that the resolution needs a fact the reader lacks, because the writer has it.

Remedy. Test 3 (Earned). Plant the element early and unremarked, so that the intervention becomes the arrival of something the reader knew existed; or change the outcome to one the existing system produces.

Exception. Jane Eyre (1847). On the point of accepting St John Rivers, Jane hears Rochester’s voice calling her across an impossible distance, a supernatural intervention at the moment of decision. It works because Brontë has built a world in which presentiment, dream and vision operate, and because the voice does not decide for Jane; it interrupts a decision she then makes herself. The rescue is unearned in mechanism and earned in preparation.

The explained ending

Symptom. A character or the narrator sums up what the book has meant, often in a penultimate scene and often beginning I suppose what I’ve learned is.

Mechanism. The thematic work moves from the reader to the text, and a test the reader could have concluded becomes a proposition to accept or reject. The ending’s most privileged position is spent on a paraphrase of the book. Where the narration has been scenic throughout, the explanation also breaks the voice contract at the moment the book can least afford it. The motive is sympathetic: the writer cannot bear the thought that the meaning might be missed, and by the end knows it well enough to state it.

Remedy. Delete the explanation and read the ending without it; in most drafts nothing is lost. Where something is, convert it into an act or an object, not back into speech.

Exception. Middlemarch (1871–72). The Finale explains, generally and at length, what to conclude about Dorothea and about lives like hers. The narrator has generalised for eight hundred pages, so the register completes the book’s mode instead of breaking it, and the generalisation extends the novel rather than paraphrasing it: it tells the reader what cannot be known about such lives, a proposition the book made available and had not stated. The fault is an explanation that replaces the book with a summary of itself.

The tidy-everything ending

Symptom. Every thread and question is resolved with equal, mechanical thoroughness, whatever its weight.

Mechanism. Uniform resolution destroys hierarchy. Emphasis is relational, so an ending in which everything is settled to the same degree says that nothing mattered more than anything else, contradicting the weighting the book spent its length establishing. It is also the place where a novel most visibly administers its own material, the shift from scene to inventory that makes last chapters read as thinner than their books.

Remedy. Cut resolutions rather than adding them. Remove the resolutions of the two least-weighted threads and reread; usually the rest gain force, and where something is lost, that thread carried more than the audit suggested.

Exception. The epilogue of Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows (2007), “Nineteen Years Later”. On a platform at King’s Cross the surviving friends, now married, see their children off to school; Harry’s younger son is afraid of being sorted into Slytherin; the last sentences report that the scar had not pained Harry for nineteen years and that all was well. Many readers and reviewers found it too neat, and Rowling has said she wanted it to be clear that Harry had triumphed and would not face Voldemort again. It works for the series’ readers because its tidiness is graded and formal: it takes one morning and a handful of families rather than the whole cast, it returns to the train with which the series began, and its last line answers the first wound of the first volume. A seven-volume contract for a closed world is discharged in the series’ own terms.

Death as resolution

Symptom. A central problem is resolved by a death, usually of the character who poses it.

Mechanism. Death is the most available terminal event in fiction and the easiest substitute for a decision: it is final, unarguable and requires nothing of anyone. When nobody chose it and none of the book’s forces produced it, the ending has been handed to chance, and the reader’s investment in a character’s capacity to choose is discharged into an event rather than an act.

Remedy. Ask whether the central problem would be resolved if the character lived. If not, the death is doing structural work; move it before the ending, so that the ending holds a decision made in its light.

Exception. Jodi Picoult’s My Sister’s Keeper (2004). Anna, conceived as a donor match for her sister Kate, who has leukaemia, sues her parents for the right to refuse a kidney, wins, and is fatally injured in a car crash on the way home; her kidney goes to Kate, who survives. Readers have argued for twenty years about whether the accident is a cheat, and the 2009 film reversed it, letting Kate die and Anna live, a change Picoult publicly regretted. The novel commits the pattern and keeps its readers because the arbitrary event lands on the book’s own question, whose body it is, and answers it with an irony no decision could have produced: Anna wins the right to refuse, and her kidney is given anyway.

Ambiguity used as evasion

Symptom. The book declines to settle its central question, and the refusal reads as an absence of decision rather than as one.

Mechanism. An open ending works by withholding devices the reader knows are available, so it needs the withholding to be legible. Now that the unresolved close is the convention of literary fiction, it no longer marks itself: an ambiguity must be specific, so that the reader can say what is undetermined and why the book cannot determine it, or it reads as default. It is also unfalsifiable, which is why it survives revision.

Remedy. Two questions. What, in one sentence, is ambiguous? An ambiguity the writer cannot state is not a design. And what would resolving it cost? If nothing, it is doing no work. An ambiguity that survives both needs a formal signal.

Exception. The Sopranos, “Made in America” (HBO, 10 June 2007), written and directed by David Chase. Tony sits in a diner while Journey plays on the jukebox, his family arriving one by one, a man in a Members Only jacket going to the washroom; the door opens, Tony looks up, and the screen cuts to black and silence. Many viewers assumed their signal had failed. The ambiguity is about one thing, whether this is the moment Tony is killed, and it is the series’ standing condition made formal: a man who could be killed at any meal, watched by a viewer who finally cannot see. Chase has declined to explain it, and later said only that the scene showed Tony could die, not that he did. The cut is the most legible signal a screen has.
