Chapter 16 · The Ending

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The domain model asks what kinds of work an ending owes the reader, so that “this ending isn’t working” becomes “it delivers plot and catharsis, gestures at theme and does nothing for character”. It is the move The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) makes for the scene. Its danger is that the domains are read as a schedule: an action climax followed by a chapter for character, a passage for theme and a closing picture. They are registers in which an ending is heard, not tasks to be done in order, and the strongest endings sound several in one image (The Mechanism). Nor are they equally obligatory. A detective novel that fails plot resolution has failed, since its contract is about knowledge; a lyric novel of consciousness may resolve nothing and be complete. The weighting follows genre, not merit.

Plot resolution

The reader receives answers to the questions the book raised, or a visible refusal to answer them. A question can be answered, declined on the page or dropped, and only the third is a defect. The rule that follows costs a sentence to obey: a declined question must be visibly declined, by a character who asks and is not told, a narrator who notes the absence, or a document found to be incomplete. The Handmaid’s Tale (1985) never says what became of Offred, and its Historical Notes stage a conference of scholars failing to find out, which turns the gap from an omission into the subject. The domain also settles accounts with The Subplot’s attention tax (Chapter 14): a thread that took eighty pages and receives one sentence has been under-paid, and a thread that took eight and receives a chapter misstates the book’s hierarchy.

Character-arc completion

The reader receives the final state of the change, or of the refusal to change, that the book has tracked. Arcs complete in behaviour, not statement, and the most reliable device is the repeated situation answered differently, which uses material already paid for and lets the reader make the comparison. In Emma Donoghue’s Room (2010), five-year-old Jack has spent his life in one locked room with his mother, treating its objects, Rug and Lamp and Plant, as companions; at the end, after the escape and a long adjustment outside, he goes back, finds the room smaller than he remembers and says goodbye to the same objects before leaving. The objects are the ones he began with, and his relation to them has reversed. Two refinements matter. An arc may complete as a refusal: in The Remains of the Day (1989) Stevens recognises on the pier at Weymouth what he gave up, and then turns at once to the problem of learning to banter. And an arc needs a constant, something visibly unchanged against which the change can be read; if everything about a character is different, the reader registers replacement rather than development.

Thematic closure

The reader receives the book’s final position on the question it has been testing. Theme is a question the book tests, not a proposition it asserts, and the ending’s job is to conclude the test. An ending in which a character says we can never really know the people we love delivers a proposition the reader may dispute; an ending in which three people read a document proving that the man they loved kept something from them, and none of them says anything, delivers an experience. A generalisation in the last pages acquires the authority of privileged position, which makes it the strongest carrier and the easiest to abuse. A narrator may state the theme at the end only if the narrator has been stating things all along, as Eliot’s has for eight hundred pages of Middlemarch.

Emotional catharsis

The reader receives the release of a pressure the book has built. Catharsis is not caused by the ending; it is a release of pressure created upstream, so a writer who finds an ending emotionally flat and adds feeling to it usually adds embarrassment, when the deficiency lies earlier, in the access The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13) calls the empathy budget. The dependable carriers are physical: people in one room who have been apart, a hand taken, someone sitting down, and silence after argument. The Grapes of Wrath (1939) ends with Rose of Sharon, whose baby was stillborn, feeding a starving stranger in a barn; nothing in the plot is resolved, and the book’s pressure, the argument that survival is collective, is discharged in one act.

The final image

The reader receives the last thing the book shows, which is the door they leave by. There is no neutral last sentence: a novel that ends on a character making tea has told the reader that tea is what the book was for. Four variables can be set deliberately. Scale narrows to a detail or widens to a panorama. Direction in time looks back, stays, or projects forward. Grammar closes harder when the sentence is short, declarative and ends on a stressed syllable. And presence decides who is in the last clause: Wuthering Heights ends with Lockwood at three graves among moths and heath, and the withdrawal of everyone the book was about is the closure.

A proposed sixth: formal closure

A reader at the last page is also finishing an artefact with a pattern of its own. Formal closure is the completion, modification or disruption of the book’s structural pattern rather than its story: the frame that returns, the repeated chapter shape altered at its last instance, the apparatus that follows the narrative. It is The Opening Pages’s formal contract (Chapter 9) falling due, the conventions of person, timeline, division and apparatus that an opening declares. Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) is the strongest case for the domain. Its narrative closes thematically in total defeat, He loved Big Brother, and is followed by an Appendix on Newspeak written in the past tense by a scholar for readers who evidently do not live in Oceania. No plot question is reopened, but the artefact now contains a vantage from which the regime is over, and a reader who notices the tense has been given a hope the story denied.

The objection is that formal closure is a means by which the other domains are served, not something the reader receives, and that mixing ends and means weakens a diagnostic model. The defence is that it has distinct carriers, distinct failures (the frame opened and never closed, the epistolary novel that drifts into plain narration) and distinct remedies (close the frame, or remove it from the opening), and that a reader can feel a book’s form left unfinished after its theme has closed. This chapter adopts the domain for diagnosis, conceding that it sits at a different level of description from the other five; The Structural Edit (Chapter 18) has since ruled on it: rejected as a domain of the ending, since in both test cases the formal pattern carries and the payment lies in another domain (the Appendix’s past tense is received as hope, the Six Feet Under montage as the series’ theme), and adopted at the scale of the book as the form audit, a test of the book’s form that asks of every pattern it declares whether the pattern is completed, modified for a reason the book shows, or removed from where it was declared. The objection holds at the ending; the defence holds for the audit.

Proposals from earlier chapters

Four earlier chapters left their proposed categories for this one to judge. The Opening Pages’s formal contract (Chapter 9) is adopted at the scale of a book: formal closure is that contract discharged, and an unclosed frame is a failure only where a frame was declared. The Dialogue Exchange‘s silence domain (Chapter 3) is rejected as a domain of the ending. Its test case, the household mother’s unexplained silence, is served at the ending by plot resolution, as a question visibly declined, and the siblings’ silence as they read is a carrier of catharsis; here the objection The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) conceded, that silence is a means and not a value that changes, holds, and the domain stands in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) at the scale of the exchange. The Chapter Break’s recasting close (Chapter 6), the chapter ending that turns the reader back to what has happened, stops discriminating at a book’s last line, where nothing comes next and every close looks back; it remains a type of chapter ending, and the question it leaves, how far a close may recast the whole book, passes to The Reveal (Chapter 15), which answers it with the teller rule: a close may falsify only what a teller in the book could have shaped. The Prologue’s last ordinary day (Chapter 10) is confirmed as a type of prologue: the household ending pays off the two objects of the father’s last ordinary day, the good pen and his unfinished In case, and leaves two of its details unredeemed, which Worked Analyses records as the type’s risk.
