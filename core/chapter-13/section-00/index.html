Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

The Viewpoint Shift

Continue in Reference

Who is granted a mind, how many, and when

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 13. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

In brief. A viewpoint shift moves the narration from one character’s consciousness to another’s. Behind every shift lies a decision this chapter calls the grant: which characters the reader is allowed inside, for how much of the book, and from when. The grant is a structural decision rather than a local one, because each new viewpoint adds coverage but divides depth, and because the characters left outside are shaped by being left there. The chapter’s tool is an admission test: a viewpoint must name two jobs it does that no cheaper instrument could do, and one of them must be something other than carrying information. Use the chapter when readers cannot tell your viewpoint characters apart, when a draft feels thin although every scene is anchored, when a new viewpoint arrives late, or when you are tempted to explain a difficult character by going inside her.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Distinguish a structural shift from head-hopping, and say whether a book has earned the right to move between minds inside a scene.
  2. State for any viewpoint character which jobs the grant does and which cheaper instruments it displaced.
  3. Name a draft’s viewpoint architecture and audit its page-share.
  4. Price a late grant, and decide whether to bound it to a single chapter.
  5. Say what the allocation does to the characters who receive no viewpoint, and defend it.

Key terms

Viewpoint shift; the grant, with its recipient, extent, onset and termination; head-hopping, the structural shift and omniscience; focalisation (zero, internal and external; fixed, variable and multiple) and the focaliser; voice, person and narrative distance; the reflector; psycho-narration, narrated monologue and quoted monologue; character-space, character-system and character compression; the situation model; levels of intentionality; narrative empathy; the domains of a grant (access, dramatic irony, contrast (viewpoint domain), sympathy engineering, structural pacing) and the proposed epistemological domain; the cheaper instruments; the knowledge boundary; viewpoint architecture; the bounded grant; the first grant and the later grant; page-share. Each is defined at first use and collected in the consolidated glossary.

Contents

The first files say what a viewpoint grant is and why it is expensive, the middle files what a grant can do and how its cost is managed, and the last are for use at the desk.

  1. The Problem
    A household draft with too many viewpoints, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The shift, the grant, head-hopping, and the terms confused with them
  3. How It Works
    Who perceives, what a grant delivers, what happens to the people left outside, what a shift costs, and the age of the head-hopping rule
  4. The Taxonomy
    Five domains a grant can serve, a proposed sixth, and a ruling on the authority domain of The Prologue (Chapter 10)
  5. The Mechanism
    The admission test, where the distinguishing work lives, the first and later grant, the ceiling, and the architectures
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Ten questions on one page, and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a published work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    Middlemarch, The Vegetarian, Me Before You, The Affair and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the shape of an allocation says

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests and the page-share audit in the exercises; the coda, which sets limits on the chapter’s central metaphor, is best read early.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds the complete chapter, The Viewpoint Shift: the fuller narratology, the cognitive literature and its limits, a history of the head-hopping rule, thirteen architectures, twelve failure patterns, eight close readings (among them Bleak House, Wide Sargasso Sea and Beloved), viewpoint in other forms and in translation, seminar questions and the sources.

See also: The Transition (Chapter 5), on how the reader is carried across the gap a shift opens; The Chapter Break (Chapter 6), on where the gap falls; The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), whose formal contract requires a plural architecture to be declared early; The Subplot (Chapter 14), whose attention tax is the same scarcity at the scale of a plot line; The Reveal (Chapter 15), on the disclosure a withheld viewpoint so often holds back.
