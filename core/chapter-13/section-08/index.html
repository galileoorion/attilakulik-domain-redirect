Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings show grants made, refused and bounded; a screen reading shows multiple focalisation where the medium’s default is the outside; and the household case shows a grant being decided.

George Eliot, Middlemarch (1872): a reallocation in one sentence

Chapter 29 opens in the manner the novel has trained its reader to expect, then breaks off:

One morning, some weeks after her arrival at Lowick, Dorothea—but why always Dorothea? Was her point of view the only possible one with regard to this marriage?

In this opening the narrator performs the argument of this chapter. Its first clause is the ordinary machinery of an opening, a time and a place, set up so that the break registers as a break. Dorothea names a major character the reader has followed across the preceding chapters, and always identifies that expectation as something built by repetition, and so built on purpose. Point of view names the object under discussion in the reader’s own vocabulary, and this marriage fixes the scope: not everything, but the one subject on which two vantages will now be shown to differ. The pages that follow enter Casaubon, insisting that he has an intense consciousness and is spiritually hungry like the rest of us.

The turn is locally bounded: Eliot takes the reader inside Casaubon for a stretch and returns, within a narration already free to move among minds. It is sympathy without exculpation, since nothing about the marriage improves and only the reader’s licence to find him merely absurd is withdrawn. And the turn is legible here because it was prepared earlier: the narrator has generalised, addressed the reader and moved between minds since the first pages, so the mobile omniscient architecture already holds the right of free movement. In a novel that had established strict limited narration, the same sentence would signal a change of convention that needed preparation. A chapter-scale turn toward a familiar character can require less new orientation than the arrival of a stranger, and why always Dorothea? is, in a clause, the question Woloch’s study spends its length asking.

Han Kang, The Vegetarian (2007; translated by Deborah Smith, 2015): the centre refused

Yeong-hye stops eating meat after a dream, and the novel follows the consequences in three parts, each held by someone else: her husband, Mr Cheong, in the first person; her brother-in-law, an artist, in close third; her sister, In-hye, in close third. Yeong-hye receives no sustained narrating section, though brief monologues in the first part give partial access to her voice, and when asked why she has changed she says only that she had a dream.

The architecture is three sequential bounded grants around a refused centre, and each grant serves contrast (viewpoint domain) by showing a different appetite trained on the same woman: the husband’s convenience, the brother-in-law’s art and desire, the sister’s duty. The order moves the vantage closer in kinship and sympathy, and In-hye’s part comes nearest to understanding without entering. That is the epistemological domain at a small cast’s scale: no account adjudicates, and the reader who wants Yeong-hye’s reasons is left where her family is. The refusal also makes character compression the novel’s subject rather than its by-product. Yeong-hye is largely seen from outside by three people who need her to be something, and the reader is bound to their failure to see her. A draft that “solved” the novel by adding her chapter would explain her, and the book’s argument is that she is not available to be explained. Broad structural claims are often more stable across translation than sentence-level claims about distance, which here must be tied to Smith’s English.

Jojo Moyes, Me Before You (2012): bounded grants around a withheld decision

The novel is narrated in the first person by Louisa Clark, a carer for Will Traynor after the accident that left him quadriplegic. A third-person prologue briefly follows Will before the accident. Four later chapters leave Lou: one each in the voices of Will’s mother, Camilla, his nurse, Nathan, his father, Steven, and Lou’s sister, Katrina. Will never narrates a chapter, and his interior after the accident is withheld. Moyes has said that his was the one mind she could not enter, “because I wanted his intentions to be one of the central tensions of the book.”

The book shows the bounded grant working in commercial fiction at low cost. Each of the four chapters goes to someone already present, so there is no cold start; each is single, so the reader learns that someone else occasionally speaks rather than that a rotation has begun; and each lets the reader see Lou’s situation from a position she cannot occupy, the mother who has agreed to her son’s request, the nurse who knows his body, the father, the sister, which serves sympathy and, where they know what Lou does not, dramatic irony. The first of those four chapters arrives in chapter 8, after Lou has held the opening chapters. The third-person prologue has already shown that the book can leave her, and the later visits are brief and bounded. And Will shows that a character can remain vivid without sustained access to his mind: after the brief pre-accident prologue, the reader sees his later life from outside, and the refusal keeps the book’s central decision in the one mind the reader cannot read. The household novel’s mother is built on the same principle.

A screen reading: The Affair (Showtime, 2014)

Screen narration can present observable behaviour directly and can also convey a character’s interior through voice-over, point-of-view shots, editing and performance. The first season of The Affair, created by Sarah Treem and Hagai Levi, builds it by allocation instead. Each episode is split into two parts, one remembered by Noah and one by Alison, the lovers, and the versions of shared moments differ in detail, down to who made the first move; a police detective’s interviews, set in the future, frame the recollections as testimony. This is Genette’s multiple focalisation, the same events through different characters, and the season serves the epistemological domain cheaply because the declaration is total from the first episode. When the second season adds the perspectives of Helen and Cole, the two spouses, it makes later grants to characters the viewer has watched from outside for a season, which reduces the work of introducing them as new vantages.

The household revision case: should Dov ever hold a viewpoint?

The situation. Dov has been observed only from outside. In the doorway he gives Elena the new key and withholds the reason (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3); Nadia knows him through calls she does not answer (The Opening Pages, Chapter 9); his sale of the shop reaches the reader by report (The Subplot, Chapter 14); the ledger is first seen as an object in The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4), and the looser hand that follows the father’s entry of 9 March is discovered by Elena in The Chapter Break (Chapter 6) without her reading it. The question is whether to grant him a viewpoint, and in what form.

Version A, the draft. Dov as a rotating viewpoint from page one:

Dov had been at the shop since seven, which was earlier than he needed to be and later than he wanted. The buyer’s agent was due at eleven. He had the papers in the folder on the passenger seat and had not told either of his sisters, and he told himself this was because Elena would want to argue about the valuation and Nadia would want to argue about everything else … Someone had to keep the place open. Someone had to answer the phone. He had been answering the phone since March and neither of them had asked him what it cost.

Diagnosis. It fails necessity, since the sale, the secrecy, the deadline and the resentment all reach the reader from the sisters’ vantages. It serves access and a thin sympathy, and no contrast, because Dov’s words of appraisal differ from his sisters’ in subject, not in mind; swapped into Elena’s chapter with three nouns changed, the paragraph survives, so it also fails distinguishability. It passes the investment test only because nobody has investment on page one. The largest failure is character compression. A focalised Dov knows what his mother said to him, and must either think about it or be seen conspicuously not thinking about it, so the mother’s silence becomes a reported silence and the book’s one deliberately open thread is closed by the allocation. The household novel is an investigation, two sisters reconstructing what was withheld from them, and granting the withholder a vantage turns it into a study of a man with a secret, with nothing strong enough to replace the question it spends.

Version B, the revision. One chapter, placed after Nadia finds the good pen in the coat pocket and before the three of them read the ledger together, at about 78 per cent of the book. It dramatises the evening of 9 March and the decision to go on writing:

The last entry in his father’s hand was three lines long and the third line stopped halfway, so that the doctor’s name — misspelled, then misspelled differently, then got right — trailed off into a scratch. The name meant nothing to him. Dov sat with it for a while. The leather was faintly warm from the lamp. He looked for the good pen where it was always kept, beside the till, and it was not there, and he did not think about that then; he thought about it for years afterwards. What he used was a ballpoint from the drawer that stuck. He wrote the date. He wrote the count. He did not write anything else, because his father had not written anything else on the ninth either, and it seemed to him that the ledger had a way of being written in, and that if he did it wrong someone would be able to tell.

What the revision did. It reduced the grant to the minimum that carries the payload, one chapter, and moved it to the point of greatest prior curiosity, reducing the work of entering Dov’s mind. It chose a decision rather than a fact: the fact that someone kept the ledger going was delivered by the object about half a book earlier, and the grant supplies only what an object cannot, the choosing. It serves three domains: sympathy (from outside, the continued ledger reads as forgery or as the start of the sale; from inside it is filial), contrast (Dov protects by keeping quiet, as his father did, where his sisters want to expose) and dramatic irony (the reader carries into the reading of the ledger what the sisters do not know). It built distinguishability from the four cheap places: he notices the pen’s absence and the warm leather where Nadia would count and Elena would look at a lock; he appraises how the ledger is written in, a category neither sister has; he thinks his father. And it made the knowledge boundary the chapter’s engine. Dov does not know where the pen is, though the reader, who has seen the father pocket it in the prologue and Nadia find it, does. The name on the third line means nothing to him, and in the four months he keeps the ledger he writes forward and never reads back, so he does not know his father was ill, then or at any point before the three of them read the ledger together. He does not know why the locks were changed, and the chapter does not tell the reader. The narration’s the doctor’s name belongs to its small retrospective gap, he thought about it for years afterwards, not to what Dov understood that evening.

What the revision has not solved. A single chapter that succeeds creates an appetite the book then refuses, and some readers will feel the refusal as discipline and others as a pleasure withheld; that cost is paid every time a grant is bounded. The chapter solves Dov and leaves the mother: she changes the locks, speaks once at a remove and is never entered, a defensible allocation that a reader entitled to ask why always the siblings? can challenge with Eliot’s authority. A vantage granted just before a scene in which its holder is judged looks arranged, and the defence is that it excuses nothing: Dov still signs the sale without telling his sisters, and the entries after 9 March are still, in the plainest description, someone else writing in his father’s book. And Dov’s never reading back over four months needs a reason the reader can believe; the ledger had a way of being written in begins to supply one, and the chapter may need a sentence more.
