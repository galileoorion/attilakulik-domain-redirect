Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

A third of the way into a first draft of the household novel, the writer has given each of the three siblings a viewpoint in rotation: a chapter for Elena, a chapter for Nadia, a chapter for Dov, and round again. The rotation felt fair while drafting, since each of them has a stake in the father’s death and the shop. Two early readers have now sent notes. The first says she cannot always tell Nadia’s chapters from Dov’s when she covers the chapter numbers, because both of them count things, worry about money and resent the others. The second asks why the book never lets her inside the mother, who changed the locks and will not say why, and suggests a fourth strand in the rotation so that the mother can explain herself.

The draft and the suggestion are illustrations for this chapter, not the novel as it stands; they are useful because the two readers have, between them, named both halves of the problem the chapter treats.

Coverage and depth

Imagine two finished manuscripts of the same length, both about this family after the death. The first is told entirely through Elena. Everything the reader learns, she learns by being present, by being told, by inferring or by being misled, and whole regions of the story are closed: Dov’s dealings with the buyer, the mother’s reasons, the father’s last months. The book is badly informed. It is also, after four hundred pages, in possession of one person so thoroughly that the reader can predict what she will notice in a room before she notices it.

The second is told through all four in rotation. Nothing is closed. Dov’s negotiations are dramatised and the mother’s reasons supplied. The book is well informed, and it holds four people at a quarter of the depth each. The arithmetic is plain at novel length: in a book of 90,000 words, one viewpoint has all 90,000 words in which to become known, and four equal viewpoints have 22,500 each, about the length of a long short story, with the further cost that the reader has been asked to begin again with each change.

Neither manuscript is better by construction. What separates them is a decision about how a finite quantity of reader attention and identification is distributed across a cast. That decision is made once, at the level of the whole book, and then honoured or broken at every scene boundary. Coverage is additive: each new viewpoint adds the scenes that character can attend. Depth is divisive: each new viewpoint divides the same pages and the same accumulated familiarity. A book cannot buy coverage without spending depth, and it cannot keep depth without accepting blindness.

The people left outside

The second reader’s suggestion points at the less obvious half. A character seen only from outside, in a book whose other characters are seen from inside, is not neutral in the reader’s mind. The mother in the household novel is present throughout, changes the locks, speaks once at a remove through Dov, and is never entered, and the reader treats her silence as the book’s standing question. Give her a strand and the question is answered, whether or not the writer meant to answer it, because a mind the reader is inside cannot keep its reasons from the reader without visibly refusing to think about them. The allocation of viewpoint is also an allocation of what stays unknown.

The household novel, as the canon of this edition fixes it, settles both halves. A prologue is focalised through the father on his last ordinary day; the main narrative belongs to Elena and Nadia in close third person; Dov is seen from outside until a single chapter near the climax; and the mother is never entered. This chapter is the reasoning that reaches that settlement and defends it, and the household revision case in Worked Analyses tests it.

Viewpoint is an allocation, not a technique: a distribution of a finite resource across a cast, whose shape can be read, argued over and revised, and which shapes the characters it leaves out as surely as those it lets in.

The rest of the chapter states the claim’s terms, sets out the theory it depends on, names what a grant can be for, and gives the tests that turn the claim into a revision plan.
