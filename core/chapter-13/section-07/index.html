Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below fails through a mechanism, not a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent. The first pattern is a rule that names a convention rather than a mechanism, and it takes more space than the others because the convention is so widely taught as a law. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue has twelve patterns, including the redundant vantage, the omniscient slip, the corpse’s-eye view and the viewpoint granted only to be killed.

Head-hopping

Symptom. Inside one continuous scene the narration reports the interior of more than one character, moving from what she thinks to what he thinks without a boundary.

Mechanism. The usual account is that the reader, having bound the scene to one mind, must rebuild it around another without warning. That is true of one configuration and false as a general account. What produces the unease is a mismatch between what the narration does and the convention the book has set: a novel confined to one mind for eighty pages has taught confinement, so an excursion on page ninety-one reads as an accident and costs the reader some confidence in the book. Two further distinctions decide most cases. Governed movement goes somewhere to compare, correct or judge; opportunistic movement goes where the plot needs a fact the current focaliser cannot supply, and readers detect the difference without naming it. And free access needs a narrator substantial enough to be the reader’s constant, an audible intelligence with a temperament and opinions; transparent close third person has none, so when it moves there is nothing to hold.

Remedy. Decide the architecture, limited or mobile, and write the decision down. If limited, replace each straying sentence with what the focaliser could observe or infer. If mobile, establish the right in the first pages and exercise it throughout, including where it is not needed. Do not do both: a manuscript limited by default and mobile at its crises fails in both directions. The rule as it should be taught is not never change viewpoint within a scene but do not exercise a right the book has not established.

Exception. Middlemarch (1872). In the marriage of Lydgate and Rosamond the reader is given the same exchange from both sides in consecutive paragraphs, his exasperation and her serene incomprehension, each reasonable from inside, and the marriage’s tragedy consists in the reader’s ability to hold both at the moment each speaks; alternating chapters could not produce it. Eliot also theorises the practice, in the parable of the pier-glass at the opening of chapter 27, whose random scratches seem to arrange themselves in circles around any candle held to it: every mind organises the same events around itself, and a narration that can move the candle can show the circles are not there.

The information mule

Symptom. A viewpoint character whose sections exist to deliver plot information the protagonist cannot reach, a colleague who overhears or an official with the file, and who has no want of her own.

Mechanism. The costliest instrument applied to the cheapest problem. The reader enters a mind and finds it furnished only with the plot, and because an inside view implies significance, she keeps a place open for someone who will not matter.

Remedy. Test 1 (Necessity), then one of three moves: reassign the scene to an existing vantage; convert the content to report, document or inference; or give the mule a want that acquiring the information costs her, which turns an access grant into one that also engineers sympathy.

Exception. Wilkie Collins’s The Woman in White (1860) is built largely of information mules and remains a lasting popular novel. Its minor narrators, a housekeeper, a cook, a doctor, testify once and vanish without arcs, and the structure works because Collins frames the book as evidence given as before a judge, each witness speaking only to what she can attest. In a novel of testimony narrowness is a witness’s virtue; a mule fails in a novel of consciousness and succeeds in one where the reader has been told what a narrator is for.

The diluted ensemble

Symptom. Five, six or eight viewpoint characters in a book without the length to develop any of them; coverage is total and investment slight, and readers find the book hard to get into without knowing why.

Mechanism. The trade between coverage and depth settled by default. Each added vantage adds a set of relations to the reader’s load, and each section opens with a beat of who is this again? even when the anchor is correct, a failure of memory rather than of anchoring.

Remedy. The page-share audit, then conversion to hub and spokes: most diluted ensembles are hubs whose author will not admit the hub. A second remedy, less often tried, is to shorten the visits, so that the reader samples rather than settles.

Exception. Bernardine Evaristo’s Girl, Woman, Other (2019) grants twelve women their own sections, in four chapters of three, each section ranging across a whole life. It avoids dilution by not cycling: each woman holds the reader once, at length, so no section has to be re-entered, and each new section is tied by family or friendship to one already read, so that identification is cheap. The novel then gathers most of them at a party after the opening of Amma’s play, which converts coverage into convergence. An ensemble is dilute when its members compete for the same reader function, and robust when each performs a different one.

The unearned late arrival

Symptom. A new viewpoint character appears at the two-thirds mark or later, holds several chapters, and pulls attention from vantages the reader has invested in; the reader skims to get back.

Mechanism. The three charges of the later grant, formal surprise, attention withdrawal and cold start, incurred together where the reader is least willing to begin again.

Remedy. Bound the grant; or plant the vantage earlier, even briefly, so that its arrival is a return; or solve the plot problem underneath, which is usually that the climax has been placed where the protagonist cannot reach it.

Exception. Jean Rhys’s Wide Sargasso Sea (1966) opens its third part in the voice of Grace Poole, a servant who has not narrated before, for a page or two, and never again. By every criterion above it should fail. It succeeds because it performs the novel’s argument in its form: the book is about who may speak of a woman called mad, and the last voice before Antoinette’s final section is her keeper’s, the person Jane Eyre used to explain her. The arrival reads as the book’s meaning, not its author’s convenience.

The antagonist chapter that spoils its own suspense

Symptom. A book built on a mystery grants a viewpoint to the person who holds the answer, and the reader is told what the book was going to be about.

Mechanism. The trade of the dramatic-irony domain made without noticing: curiosity converted into suspense when the rest of the machinery was built for curiosity, so the investigators have nothing left to find.

Remedy. Decide which engine the book runs on and allocate accordingly; if the antagonist’s vantage stays, supply a replacement question at least as strong as the one spent.

Exception. Gillian Flynn’s Gone Girl (2012) commits the pattern twice and profits both times. Its first part alternates Nick’s present-tense account of his wife’s disappearance with Amy’s diary, so that the antagonist holds a vantage from the second chapter, disguised as a victim’s document. Its second part opens on Amy alive, I’m so much happier now that I’m dead, and the diary is revealed as a fabrication planted for the police. The mystery is spent at the midpoint, deliberately, and replaced by questions the first half could not ask: whether she will win, and what the marriage will become.
