Appendices

Appendix D — The Household Novel

Continue in Reference

Every chapter of this book revises one invented novel, and this appendix gathers what the chapters establish about it: the book’s form, its people, its objects, the order of its events in story time and in the telling, the plants and their payoffs, and what is left open. It is a summary, written for a reader who meets the household in a chapter’s case and wants to know where the material came from. Where a fact rests on a revision that a later chapter proposes rather than one the earlier chapters already contain, the appendix says proposed and names the chapter, since those revisions are argued for in their chapters and left for the author to confirm. The chapter that treats each fact is given by number; each chapter’s household case is in its Worked Analyses part, and in full in its Reference text.

The book

The household novel is a contemporary literary novel of family life, quiet and domestic in register, set in an unnamed British town with a ring road. Its subject is what a family withholds from itself, and the question it runs on is not what happened? but what do these three do with what they can reconstruct? (The Ending, Chapter 16). Arc and Theme (Chapter 12) proposes that the themes the chapters name in passing (what the living owe the dead’s silences, ritual as a way of not quite facing loss, records outliving the people who keep them, counting set against unmeasured time) are aspects of one governing question, first put in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3): what the members of a family owe each other in the way of truth. Each member of the household answers it differently, and the ending answers it by arrangement, three people reading one record, rather than by a statement.

Series and Genre (Chapter 17) proposes that the book is a standalone: a literary family novel in the mystery mode, which keeps the mystery’s promises about who wrote in the ledger after 9 March, who took the ledger and what the father knew, and declines one, why the locks were changed, and declares the decline. There is no second volume, and nothing on the last page points to one.

The form is fixed by the chapters’ cases. A prologue is told from the father’s viewpoint on his last ordinary day (The Prologue, Chapter 10). The main narrative is close third person, past tense, divided between Elena and Nadia (The Viewpoint Shift, Chapter 13; The Subplot, Chapter 14). Dov is seen only from outside except in one bounded chapter, the evening of 9 March, near the climax (The Viewpoint Shift, Chapter 13). The mother is never given a viewpoint. The narration keeps a small retrospective gap over its characters, as in is what she’d tell people later (Scene and Sequel, Chapter 2) and He would remember, later (The Prologue, Chapter 10), which lets it see past a character’s rationalisations without leaving her head. The book’s English is British: a flat, the till, Ma.

The people

The father is never named, nor is the family’s surname. He kept the shop’s ledger for eleven years in a careful slanting hand with crossed sevens, recording prices and repairs and the ordinary arithmetic of keeping a small shop alive. He used the good pen, a fountain pen, for the ledger only, and kept it by the till in its case; he lost it twice in the year he died. He hooked his thumb into his left cuff before he spoke, a habit Ma called thinking with his hands, and wore the cuff through; he wore the coat behind the counter every winter, over the ledger (The Descriptive Detail, Chapter 4). He was ill, wrote the illness into the ledger (dates, a doctor’s name misspelled twice and corrected once, and a list of what needed doing if he ran out of time), and told nobody. His last entry is dated 9 March and breaks off halfway along its third line. He died in March. His line, said to Nadia the second time he lost the pen, when she asked why a stock count needed it, is “Because someone should write it down properly. In case anyone ever needs to check” (Backstory and Flashback, Chapter 7); she thought he meant the shop. On the prologue’s day he thinks only In case, and could not have said in case of what (The Prologue, Chapter 10).

The mother, Ma, changed the shop’s locks in March and never says why. She has no scene of her own and speaks once, at a remove, through Dov in the doorway: “She said you’d ask that” (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3). Whether she knew of the illness is not established, and the book keeps it so. Her silence is the novel’s one deliberately open thread: no chapter explains it, gives her a viewpoint or gives her an account of herself.

Elena is the investigator. She drives a truck, has had a key to the shop since she was twelve, thinks in questions and notices locks. She learns in Dov’s doorway, across town from the shop, that her key no longer fits (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3); in the truck afterwards she thinks of telephoning her mother, decides against it, and drives to the shop (Scene and Sequel, Chapter 2). At the register drawer she smells fresh biro ink and finds the ledger; it falls open near the back, she sees without reading that her father’s hand stops at 9 March and a looser hand goes on, and she turns back to the first page to read from the beginning (The Transition, Chapter 5; The Chapter Break, Chapter 6). Her reading is interrupted before she reaches the illness entries. When she comes back the drawer is empty, because Dov has taken the ledger home (The Subplot, Chapter 14). The Middle (Chapter 11) proposes that she decides then not to ask him who took it, because she had asked him once; Arc and Theme (Chapter 12) proposes that she keeps her suspicion from Nadia with “I’d tell you”, and that at the convergence, when Dov brings the ledger to the shop, she lays her palm on the cover, does not open it, and rings Nadia. Her rule changes there from find out for yourself to what concerns the three of them is read by the three of them, and it is not extended to her mother.

Nadia counts things, and counting is how she manages grief. Seventeen months before the novel opens she left the flat over the shop for one with heating. When the book opens, four months after March, she has stopped answering Dov’s calls and is counting register receipts that nobody else thinks need counting (The Opening Pages, Chapter 9). She has learned not to touch the coat, and knows how many days it takes the dust to show again on its shoulders between brushings (The Descriptive Detail, Chapter 4). About two-thirds of the way through the book she puts her hand into the coat’s pocket, the coat still on its hook, finds the good pen and remembers her father’s line (Backstory and Flashback, Chapter 7); The Subplot (Chapter 14) proposes her reason, Dov’s request that the flat be cleared by the end of the month. At the end she goes up to the flat, takes the coat down and puts it on, gathering the sleeves so that they do not drag (The Ending, Chapter 16).

Dov, the brother, is seen from outside. In the doorway he gives Elena the new key and withholds the reason: “She said you’d ask that. She didn’t say I had to answer” (The Dialogue Exchange, Chapter 3). On the evening of 9 March he found his father’s broken-off entry, looked for the good pen, did not find it, and wrote the date and the count with a ballpoint from the drawer; he has kept the ledger in that way since, a looser hand at first and then not (The Viewpoint Shift, Chapter 13). He did not know his father was ill and does not know why the locks were changed. The Reveal (Chapter 15) proposes why he never learned: in eleven years he had never read a page of the ledger, since reading it back had been his father’s business. His subplot is a chain (The Subplot, Chapter 14): he wants to sell the shop; the buyer wants the building emptied by the end of the month; he clears the register drawer, taking the ledger home, asks his sisters to clear the flat without saying why, and signs the sale without telling them; at the convergence he brings the ledger to the shop and says “It’s all there. From March, it’s mine” (Arc and Theme, Chapter 12), without knowing what is there.

Others. A boy about the age Dov once was knocks on the shop window about the sign in the prologue and is waved off. The buyer is never named.

Story order and book order

In story time the events run as follows. Eleven years before his death the father begins the ledger. Some seventeen months before the novel opens, Nadia leaves the flat. In the year of his death he loses the pen twice and says his line. In early March comes the prologue’s last ordinary day: the till counted twice, the boy waved off, the sign left on OPEN, the pen pocketed. On 9 March his last entry breaks off and Dov begins writing. Later in March the father dies and the mother changes the locks. From March to July someone brushes the coat every week and Dov keeps the ledger. The novel’s present begins in July, four months after the death, and runs to the buyer’s end-of-month deadline, so nearly all of it falls within a few weeks.

The book tells these events in a different order, and the positions below are those the chapters give, as fractions of the book.

Read down the Planted column and thirteen of the sixteen chapters of Parts I to III appear in it, which is the sense in which the ending is built by the whole book. Read down the Paid column and nothing load-bearing is paid by material that appears first in the ending, except the list (row 12), which the book keeps there because no earlier chapter could show it without a sister reading the illness entries or Dov reading back.

What the book leaves open

Some questions are left open by design, and a writer extending the case should keep them open. The mother’s reason for changing the locks is never given, and the book declares that it is declining the question: Dov refuses it in the doorway, and Elena decides three times not to ask (in the truck, at the empty drawer and at the convergence). Who brushes the coat is never said; The Structural Edit (Chapter 18) proposes a sign that the sisters do not ask, and records that the sign makes the mother the likeliest brusher by elimination. Whether the sale completes is not stated. And the ending gives the father the last word through his own phrase, not through a scene.

The chapters also leave a set of problems unsolved, and say so in their cases: whether the prologue’s In case is a hair too large; whether paying the boy at the glass and the sign in one scene reads as arranged; that the convergence carries more payments than any other scene; that the move from the shop to the flat in the last paragraph is made in a clause; and that every proposal above still waits on the author.

Drafts are not facts. The rejected drafts in each chapter’s The Problem and in each Version A, among them the solicitor’s letter and the mother’s account in the first draft in The Ending (Chapter 16), the father’s dated strand in the draft in Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8), and the mother’s ringing on the last page in the draft in Series and Genre (Chapter 17), exist to be diagnosed, and nothing in them binds the novel.
