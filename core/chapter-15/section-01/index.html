Chapter 15 · The Reveal

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

A draft of the household novel reaches its convergence about four-fifths of the way through. The reader knows the situation. Four months after their father’s death, his three adult children have been reconstructing what he left. Elena learned in Dov’s doorway that their mother had changed the shop’s locks in March, began to read the shop’s ledger at the register drawer, and came back to find the drawer empty. Nadia, who counts things, has been asked by Dov to clear the flat above the shop and has found their father’s good pen in the pocket of the coat that still hangs there. Dov, who gave Elena the new key and kept the reason, has been keeping the ledger in a looser hand since the evening of 9 March, has signed away the shop, and took the ledger home when he cleared the drawer for the buyer. Now he brings it to the shop, where Elena is. The draft reads:

Dov put the ledger on the counter. “It’s all there. From March, it’s mine.” He kept his hand on the cover. “And before that. I took it home and I read it, Lena, the whole thing. He was ill. He’d been seeing someone since the autumn. There are dates, there’s a doctor. He knew.”

Elena then rings Nadia, Nadia comes, and the three of them read the ledger together, as the ending in The Ending (Chapter 16) has them do.

The draft’s writer added Dov’s last four sentences after an early reader asked a reasonable question: why would a man who has the ledger at home for weeks not read it? The question was right and the answer was wrong. A second reader’s note shows what the answer cost: By the time they opened the book I already knew what was in it, and so did they. The scene in which three people read their father’s record together, which the book has been building towards since its prologue, has become a scene in which two people confirm what a third has told them. The largest truth in the book, that their father knew he was ill, wrote it down and told nobody, arrives as a sentence of hearsay, before the reading, from the one person who has had no reason to look for it.

The fault is a matter of structure rather than prose. A reveal is a late disclosure that changes what the reader understands of pages already read, and this one has three jobs. It must reach back: the till counted twice in the prologue, the father’s two words In case, the doctor’s name that meant nothing to Dov on the evening of 9 March, all of which the reader took one way, must now be taken another. It must arrive by recognition, received by the people it concerns at a moment of the book’s own making. And the delay before it must have a reason inside the book. The draft fails the third job and so loses the second: it solved the plausibility problem by spending the reveal, when the problem asked for a reason for the delay.

The chapter’s claim is that every reveal makes these three demands, and that most failed reveals fail one of them.

A reveal is earned when the pages before it bear two readings, the one the reader made and the one the disclosure makes, and when something inside the book, a character who does not know or a teller with a reason not to say, explains why the truth came when it did. It may reinterpret whatever the double reading reaches, and falsify only what a teller in the book could have shaped.

The claim has a consequence for revision. A reveal that readers guess early has too many passages that bear only the second reading; one that makes readers feel cheated has a passage that bears only the first, stated on the narration’s own authority; one that lands without force was delivered as information rather than received as recognition. None is mended by adding clues or explanations at the moment of disclosure. Each is mended earlier, in the passages that prepare the reveal and in the reason for the delay. The revision in Worked Analyses deletes Dov’s four sentences and adds one sentence to his chapter of the evening of 9 March, just before the convergence. The sentence gives his not reading a reason and makes his It’s all there mean something he does not know.

The claim also settles a question this book has carried since The Chapter Break (Chapter 6), which proposed the recasting close, a chapter ending that changes the meaning of the chapter it closes, and asked how far a close may recast a whole book. The answer, argued in The Taxonomy, is the second sentence of the claim: as far as the double reading holds, and no further than a teller’s authority reaches.

See also: the full treatment, on the plausibility of withheld knowledge; The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), whose household case recorded that Dov’s never reading back “needs a reason the reader can believe”.
