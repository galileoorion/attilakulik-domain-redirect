Chapter 15 · The Reveal

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below fails through a mechanism rather than a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent or offset by another effect. The full treatment has a longer catalogue, among them the coy narrator, the serial reveal, the red herring that eats the book and the withholding viewpoint.

The cheat

Symptom. Readers feel tricked rather than surprised. They go back over the book and find a passage that the reveal makes false, and they resent the book rather than admire it.

Mechanism. The cheat is a passage before a reveal that supports only the first reading and is stated on the narration’s own authority, so that the reveal requires the narration to have lied. It fails test 1 (Double reading) on the first-reading side and test 2 (Teller). The motive is usually haste: the writer, wanting the false reading firmly held, writes it as fact rather than as a character’s belief, or writes a viewpoint character’s thoughts so that they exclude what she knows.

Remedy. Tests 1 to 3 (Double reading, Teller and Withholder). Find the passage, then find a withholder for it: reassign the false statement to a character who believes it, or to a teller whom the reveal will name; or rewrite it as an equivocation, true under both readings. If the passage is in the mind of a character who knows the truth, show her attention fixed on something else, and make that something matter.

Exception. The Usual Suspects (Bryan Singer; written by Christopher McQuarrie, 1995). Much of the film is shown in flashback while Verbal Kint tells his story to a customs agent, and the images, which a viewer ordinarily trusts as a camera’s record, show events Kint is inventing from the notices pinned to the office wall. The film commits the cheat on screen’s terms and succeeds because it has framed every flashback as Kint’s telling from its first minutes, so its last sequence names the teller and his motive. The screen’s authority was borrowed by a teller, and the reveal returns it.

The giveaway

Symptom. Readers guess the reveal early, sometimes in the first third, and the disclosure lands flat. The writer, told so, is often surprised, because no single clue seemed obvious.

Mechanism. The giveaway is a reveal whose preparation contains passages that support only the second reading, or so many passages supporting both that their density becomes a reading of its own. It fails test 1 (Double reading) on the second-reading side. A common cause is the coy narrator, who signals a withheld fact with if she had known then, which tells the reader there is a fact; another is a clue described with more attention than its first reading warrants.

Remedy. Test 1 (Double reading), then test 4 (Alignment). Give each clue a first reading that a reader would accept without effort, which usually means a reason in the scene for the detail to be there. Cut the weakest clues and every phrase that announces withholding. If readers will guess regardless, decide whether to let them, and make the alignment deliberate.

Exception. Columbo (NBC, from 1971). Each episode shows the murder and the murderer in its opening act, so that the viewer holds the solution before the detective arrives, and the series’ pleasure is watching him reach what the viewer knows. The format commits the giveaway on purpose and succeeds because it has moved the reveal’s work from the viewer’s discovery to the murderer’s, and the successor question, how he will be caught, is stronger than the one it gives away.

The reported reveal

Symptom. The largest truth in the book arrives in a sentence of dialogue or a page of explanation, and the scene that should have been its recognition becomes a confirmation of what the reader has been told.

Mechanism. The reported reveal delivers the truth as information rather than as recognition. A character who learned it offstage tells it, or the narration explains it, and the reader and the other characters are passive recipients. It fails test 7 (Recognition), and usually test 4 (Alignment), because the teller learned the truth before the reader in a scene the book did not show. The household draft in The Problem is a clean instance.

Remedy. Tests 3 (Withholder) and 7 (Recognition). Ask why the character who reports the truth learned it first, and whether the book needs that; if not, keep him ignorant and give his ignorance a reason. Then let the truth be found by the people it concerns, in a scene, by an act: a page turned, a name read, a door opened. Keep explanation after the recognition, short, and ideally unspoken.

Exception. Christie’s Murder on the Orient Express (1934). The solution is a speech: Poirot assembles the suspects and explains, and the reader receives the truth as information. It succeeds because the puzzle mystery’s contract, in the sense of The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), makes the explanation the payoff the reader came for, and because the speech contains a recognition after all, the suspects’ as they are told what they did and wait for Poirot’s decision.

The idle reveal

Symptom. The reveal is surprising and fairly prepared, and readers shrug. Asked what changed, they can say what they learned but not why it mattered.

Mechanism. The idle reveal changes the reader’s understanding of the past and nothing else: no character acts differently, no relationship alters, and the ending means what it would have meant without it. It fails tests 5 (Successor) and 6 (Consequence). The motive is often a writer’s pleasure in a secret for its own sake, or a reveal kept from an earlier draft whose plot has since moved away from it.

Remedy. Test 6 (Consequence). Tie a decision to the reveal: someone who learns the truth must now choose what he would not have chosen. If no decision can be found, ask what the book’s question is and whether the reveal answers it; a reveal that answers a question the book is not asking belongs in another book or nowhere.

Exception. Great Expectations (1860–61). Late in the novel Pip works out that Estella is the daughter of Magwitch and of Jaggers’s housekeeper, Molly. He tells Jaggers and Wemmick, and nobody does anything with the knowledge: Estella never learns it, and Magwitch is told only that his daughter lived. The reveal succeeds because its consequence is meaning rather than action. It completes the book’s pattern of hidden origins, so that the lady Pip built his expectations around came from the same convict as his fortune.

The reveal from outside

Symptom. The truth arrives by a letter, a stranger, a confession from someone the reader has barely met, or a coincidence. Readers call it convenient.

Mechanism. The reveal from outside draws the disclosure from beyond the book’s causal system, so that no character’s choice brings it about and nothing the reader watched prepared its source. It fails test 8 (Source) and usually test 7 (Recognition). The Ending’s draft ending (Chapter 16), in which a solicitor’s letter and the mother’s account explain everything, is the household’s own instance; The Subplot (Chapter 14) describes the same failure as a convergence by coincidence.

Remedy. Test 8 (Source). Route the truth through a character the book has followed, and make its arrival the result of something that character did. If a document must carry it, the document should be one the book has shown, and its finding should be a choice.

Exception. Henry Fielding’s Tom Jones (1749). The truth of Tom’s birth arrives late, from Mrs Waters, who was the Jenny Jones once blamed as his mother, and from a deathbed message that Blifil suppressed. It succeeds because the comic contract has promised restoration from the first book, so that the outside source reads as Providence keeping a promise rather than as the author rescuing a plot.
