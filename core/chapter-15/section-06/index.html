Chapter 15 · The Reveal

6. Diagnostic Tests

Continue in Reference

The eight tests are for revision, and they need the two documents defined in The Mechanism: a double-reading table for the reveal under test and a disclosure map for the book. Tests 1 to 3 (Double reading, Teller and Withholder) read the preparation, tests 4 (Alignment) and 5 (Successor) the timing, and tests 6 to 8 (Consequence, Recognition and Source) the reveal’s arrival. Record a sentence for each, not a verdict.

Test 1 — Double reading

Does every passage bearing on the reveal support both a first and a second reading?

Failure. A row with only the first is the cheat; a row with only the second is the giveaway.

Exception. Christie’s Murder on the Orient Express (1934), whose clues point everywhere, because the suspects planted them.

Test 2 — Teller

Is every passage the reveal falsifies inside the account of a teller whom the reveal names and whose motive it explains?

Failure. The cheat: the narration made to lie on its own authority.

Exception. Lewis Carroll’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865), which ends as a dream and voids nothing, since it never offered its events as fact.

Test 3 — Withholder

What keeps the truth from the reader until the disclosure, and can the reader see it, at the latest when the reveal comes?

Failure. Paralipsis without a reason: the book, not a character, kept the secret.

Exception. The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), whose withholder is invisible until the last chapter, by design.

Test 4 — Alignment

Does the reader learn each fact with, ahead of or after the characters, and is that the alignment the scene needs?

Failure. A recognition the reader has already undergone alone, or suspense never built.

Exception. Hitchcock’s Vertigo (1958), which shows the audience the deception two-thirds through, against advice, and turns the rest into suspense.

Test 5 — Successor

For a reveal before the end, what question replaces the one it answers, and is it as strong?

Failure. A second half that reads as an epilogue.

Exception. Muriel Spark’s The Prime of Miss Jean Brodie (1961), which tells early who will betray Miss Brodie and keeps the reader on why.

Test 6 — Consequence

Does any character act differently because of the reveal, or does the ending mean something it would not otherwise?

Failure. The idle reveal.

Exception. Atonement (2001), whose closing reveal comes when nobody can act, and makes that impossibility its subject.

Test 7 — Recognition

Is the truth received by a character, in scene, in an act, or delivered as information?

Failure. The reported reveal.

Exception. Ambrose Bierce’s “An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge” (1890), whose truth reaches only the reader, at the instant it ends the character.

Test 8 — Source

Does the disclosure come from inside the book’s causal system?

Failure. The reveal from outside.

Exception. Wilkie Collins’s The Moonstone (1868), solved by an experiment devised by a man introduced late, whose method rests on clues the book has kept in view.

Reading the results together

Failures cluster, and the cluster locates the repair. Tests 1 (Double reading) and 3 (Withholder) failing together are the commonest pattern in a reveal readers call a cheat: a passage gives only the false reading and nothing in the book accounts for it. The repair is usually to find a withholder first, a character who did not know, or a teller with a motive, and then rewrite the passage as an equivocation from inside that withholder’s view. Test 1 (Double reading) failing on the giveaway side while test 4 (Alignment) passes means the reader is ahead of the characters by accident rather than design; either thin the clues or make the alignment deliberate and use the suspense.

Test 5 (Successor) failing alone, in a book whose reveal falls at the middle, means a turn reveal has answered the book’s question without replacing it. Move the reveal later, or find what the characters must now do that they could not do before, and make that the second half’s engine. Tests 6 (Consequence) and 7 (Recognition) failing together describe a reveal that is information and nothing else: the reader learns, and nobody acts or recognises. The repair is a recognition scene in which someone receives the truth and does something with it. Test 8 (Source) failing is rarely solved by better preparation of the outside source; route the truth through a character the book has followed.

A clean pass on all eight, with readers who still guess the reveal early, usually means the double-reading table has too many rows, not that any row is wrong. Cut the weakest third of the clues and test again.
