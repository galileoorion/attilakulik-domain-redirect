Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

The fork

Every novel contains the past. The decision is the register in which it is delivered, and the two errors are symmetrical. A flashback used where backstory would do reads as bloated: the reader is made to live through a moment that needed only reporting. Backstory used where a flashback was needed reads as flattened: a moment the reader should have felt arrives as a fact, and when a later scene depends on its weight the dependency fails silently, so that the writer looks for the fault in the later scene. The second error is harder to find because its symptom appears somewhere other than its cause.

Percy Lubbock’s The Craft of Fiction (1921) gave influential attention to scene and picture and often favoured dramatic presentation; this chapter treats the two as a fork, not a hierarchy. A direct line from Lubbock to the later maxim show, don’t tell is harder to establish. In Genette’s measures of duration backstory sits in the summary band (much story time, little text) and flashback in the scene band (the two roughly equal). The Transition (Chapter 5) asks how much text to spend crossing a gap; this chapter asks how much to spend on the far side.

A flashback is a scene, so The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) applies to it in full: someone wants something, meets resistance, and something is different at the end. A past passage with no want and no resistance has no engine, and a flashback without a change of value is the most expensive form of summary there is. Nor is the fork a spectrum: half-dramatised past, a fragment of dialogue in a stretch of précis, has a scene’s length and a summary’s engagement, so push a doubtful passage to one end and see which version the book prefers.

The one-sentence compression check

The one-sentence compression check settles the fork. Compress the cutaway to one sentence of summary and read the surrounding pages with that sentence in its place. If nothing the reader needed to live through is lost, the material was over-dramatised backstory; if the compression flattens something the later pages depend on, the flashback has earned its place. Write the sentence out, because a writer imagining the compressed version imagines it carrying everything the full version carried. Read it in context, two pages on, not in isolation. And expect a third outcome: some passages compress with nothing lost at all, including any reason for their existence, and the verdict then is to cut, which in first drafts is the commonest result.

Fourteen registers

The fork describes only the degree of dramatisation, and a working writer needs a finer repertoire. The Reference chapter sets out fourteen temporal registers: thirteen ways of handling past material and prolepsis as a forward-looking counterpoint. The table compresses what each buys and what it costs.

# Register Buys Costs
1 Clause of implication Density with little interruption Little room to make a moment felt
2 Participial aside Biography without stopping Needs a narrator licensed to comment
3 Summary paragraph Long extents cheaply Reads as a report if extended
4 Iterative habitual A whole era in a sentence; the normal Cannot carry a singular event
5 Dramatised flashback The reader lives it The most expensive unit; suspends the present
6 Embedded oral tale The teller’s bias as content; a listener Two narrators to sustain
7 Found document Authenticity; another diction Must be plausible as itself
8 Involuntary memory The past as something that happens to a character Unmotivated by plot
9 Repeating analepsis Meaning by accumulation Every pass must visibly differ
10 Braided timeline A sustained parallel argument Both strands must progress
11 Reconstructed past Reconstruction as the reader’s activity Several accounts to hold
12 Unreachable past The gap as subject; maximum curiosity Must be legible as a refusal
13 False memory corrected The reader recruited into a misreading Needs a specific false belief
14 Prolepsis The intervening book as a completion Can neutralise suspense

Registers 1 to 4 are easily overlooked when a draft leans heavily on summary paragraphs and dramatised scenes. A useful discipline is to attempt each piece of past material as a clause first and promote it only when the clause fails. Genette’s analysis of frequency separates the singulative (telling once what happened once), the repeating (telling several times what happened once) and the iterative (telling once what happened many times), and the iterative is the register that establishes what was normal, without which a dramatised exception has nothing to be an exception to. Register 8 complicates the rest: Proust’s madeleine is an unmistakably sensory trigger, even though the recollection is not there to advance an immediate plot question. A rule requiring every trigger to serve the plot would misread it. Register 12 works only if the reader can tell she is being refused and why, which The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) states as the question whether a gap is in the character or in the author.

The table’s use is substitution testing: rewrite a failing cutaway in three other registers, one much shorter and one much longer, and judge only when all four exist, in place. The most revealing substitution is into the embedded oral tale, because turning past material into something a character says aloud, to someone, for a reason, forces two questions a flashback lets a writer avoid: why now, and why to this person.

Trigger and return

A trigger is the element in the present scene that motivates the cutaway, turning an authorial decision into an event in the story: an object, a sensation, a phrase, a question asked, a place entered, a silence, a date, a document, a resemblance, or nothing at all. The object is the strongest, because it survives the return: a smell is over when it is over, but a pen is still in the hand on the far side, and the return sentence has something to work with. The requirement of a trigger is a consequence of narrative distance, not an independent rule. Close third-person narration often stays near a consciousness, so an untriggered departure may feel abrupt; confident omniscient narration has made no such promise, which is why Tolstoy can simply go. Over-triggering is a fault too: a stopped clock that recalls the day time stopped shows the machinery, and the best triggers are slightly oblique.

The return is where the cutaway is paid for, and its rule is that the present must have changed by the time the narrative comes back to it, in a way the reader can locate. There are four kinds of change, in rising strength: changed knowledge, in which the reader knows something that recolours the scene; changed understanding, in which the character does; a changed question, in which the present scene now carries a question it did not have; and changed action, in which the character does something other than what she was about to do. The changed question is the most generally available and the least used, since it needs no plot event.

Where the multiplying work lives

Revision looks for multiplicity in a more dramatic memory, but most of the work is done in four humbler places. The trigger object exists in both timelines and can carry plot, character, theme and reader knowledge at once if it is already load-bearing elsewhere in the book. Tense and aspect are the cheapest carriers English has: He had always kept it by the till establishes in eight words a history a scene would need three hundred for, and characterises, since always is somebody’s claim. The first sentence after the return is the only one that can work on both timelines at once, and drafts spend it on re-anchoring. And the sentence not written, the one that would state the cutaway’s significance, is the commonest single improvement available, since a connection the reader makes is held more firmly than one supplied.

For a present-driven draft, it can help to treat fully dramatised past scenes as a flashback budget: check whether each one earns its space, especially when a clause would have delivered the same material. The useful allowance varies with the book, and no fixed number is implied. Multiplicity also has a ceiling: when every remembered detail is significant and the return changes the present in the designed way and no other, the cutaway reads as a mechanism. A past needs some surplus, detail that is merely true; Roland Barthes’s “The Reality Effect” (1968) argues that the apparently useless detail signifies reality itself.
