Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

The readings run from a single sentence to a whole book’s design, then to the screen and the household case.

The Death of Ivan Ilyich: the sentence that opens a life

Tolstoy’s novella (1886; translated by Louise and Aylmer Maude) gives its first chapter to the funeral: colleagues calculating what the death means for their promotions, the widow asking about the pension, and Peter Ivanovich escaping to his game of cards. The second chapter opens the retrospect, which is the rest of the book, with this sentence:

Ivan Ilych’s life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible.

Fourteen words do all the preparation for forty-five years. Ivan Ilych’s life declares the scope, not an episode but a life. Had been is the only signal of departure: the pluperfect tells the reader she is going back, with no trigger, since nobody in the present is remembering, and no bridging language. Most simple and most ordinary characterises the man and warns the reader off expecting event. And therefore carries the theme on a conjunction: not but, not and yet; the ordinariness is the cause of the horror. And most terrible, placed before its evidence, turns the forty-five years from a chronicle into a case the reader is invited to test. The sentence feels blunt, not efficient, which is the usual signature of multiplicity done well.

Because the death comes first, the retrospect is a completing analepsis, filling a gap the reader knows ends at that funeral, and the missing trigger is licensed by a narrator who never promised to stay inside anyone’s head. The retrospect also changes speed as it goes. It begins with years compressed into paragraphs, summary and iterative, and slows until the last days take pages, so the reach shortens while the pace slows, and the two together give a long retrospect the sense of arrival it otherwise lacks.

The Sense of an Ending: the document that corrects a memory

Julian Barnes’s novel (2011; Booker Prize 2011) is narrated by Tony Webster, retired, in two parts. The first is his account of his school friend Adrian Finn, of his girlfriend Veronica, and of the letter he wrote when Adrian and Veronica became a couple, not long before Adrian killed himself. The whole of Part One is retrospective narration, and the reader has only Tony’s version. In Part Two, forty years on, Veronica’s mother leaves Tony five hundred pounds and Adrian’s diary, Veronica withholds the diary, and she gives Tony a copy of his own letter. He reads it, and it is malicious in a way his memory had erased.

In this chapter’s terms Part Two is a repeating analepsis delivered through a found document, and its register is the false memory corrected. Test 9 (Completing or repeating) asks what the second pass adds, and the answer is the most a repetition can add: the same event in the narrator’s own hand, contradicting his account of it. The reader is recruited into the misreading by the only means available, the narrator’s confidence, and charged for it by a document she cannot argue with. The correction works because Part One gave a specific belief to overturn, not a blank. Barnes then repeats the move at smaller scale: the novel’s last revelation corrects a second assumption, about whose child a man Veronica shows Tony is, and Tony has to revise again.

The novel is useful to anyone writing the household book, whose theme is records outliving the people who keep them. A record’s authority in fiction comes from its having been made at the time, by someone with no idea of how it would be read later, which is what the father means when he says in case anyone ever needs to check.

Where the Crawdads Sing: a braid whose reach shortens

Delia Owens’s novel (2018) opens with two boys finding a body below a fire tower in 1969, then turns to 1952, when Kya, aged six, watches her mother leave their marsh shack and not come back. The book then moves between the investigation in 1969 and Kya’s life from 1952, the past strand advancing year by year through her abandonment by the rest of her family and her survival alone in the marsh. The investigation strand also advances, but over a shorter span; the past strand covers years. Its reach therefore shortens with every chapter, and the reader feels the past coming to meet the present, the momentum a long analepsis gains by approach. When the past strand reaches 1969 the two become one narrative for Kya’s arrest and trial.

The book is a test case for the proposed structural domain. Many 1969 chapters, taken alone, deliver little, and the content domains would mark them weak; their work is architectural, keeping the destination in view so that every chapter of Kya’s childhood reads as a step towards a body. The reader-knowledge domain runs through the same design. By the trial she knows Kya’s life as the jury cannot, and the verdict is read against that knowledge. The last pages, after Kya’s death decades later, add a found document: her partner, Tate, finds a poem alluding to the death and the shell necklace the dead man wore, and the reader must reconsider earlier events. The poem is a new clue, though poetry has appeared earlier in the novel.

A screen reading: Lost and Arrival

The season 3 finale of Lost, “Through the Looking Glass” (ABC, 23 May 2007), relied on three seasons of a convention. Many episodes had paired the island story with scenes from a character’s past, so when the finale cut to Jack, bearded and despairing in a city, viewers could read the scenes as flashbacks. The last scene revealed them as the future: Jack has left the island, and tells Kate, We have to go back. It was the series’ first flash-forward. Denis Villeneuve’s Arrival (2016), from Ted Chiang’s novella “Story of Your Life” (1998), plays the same move on a single viewer’s assumptions: the scenes of the linguist Louise with her daughter, Hannah, are presented as memories of a dead child and revealed as the future she will choose, knowing how it ends.

Both turn the convention of the register against the viewer. A cut to another time is assigned a direction by its grammar, and that assignment is itself an assumption a narrative can plant and overturn, which is the corrected assumption at its most economical: no new fact about any event, only about when it happened. In Arrival the correction also changes the emotional domain, since grief for a child already lost becomes a decision made in full knowledge.

The household revision case: the pen in the coat

About two-thirds of the way through the household novel, Nadia, in the flat above the shop, puts her hand into the pocket of her father’s coat, which still hangs on its hook by the door, and finds the good pen: the fountain pen he used only for the ledger and kept by the till in its case. The reader saw him pocket it in the prologue, on his last ordinary day (The Prologue, Chapter 10). The line he speaks in this cutaway is quoted at the novel’s close (The Ending, Chapter 16). The cutaway is the middle term between an object planted and a line returning.

Version A, the draft. The draft opens a new section with Nadia at nine, helping with the stock count for the first time, on a Sunday in November with the shop closed: a radiator making its noise like something clearing its throat, two chairs at the counter, tea, a bus shaking the window, the marks made in fives and the sevens crossed. She asks why he is using the good pen, and he puts the cap on it, looks at her over his glasses, and says, “Because someone should write it down properly. In case anyone ever needs to check.” She thinks he means the shop, goes back to her column of fives, and the section ends in the past as the light goes. It runs to about 320 words.

Diagnosis. No present question is open when the cutaway begins, because there is no present at the point of departure (test 1: Momentum). It compresses to one sentence, He had once told her, when she asked why a stock count needed the good pen, that someone should write it down properly, in case anyone ever needed to check, with nothing lost (test 2: Register). There is no trigger, a conspicuous gap in this close third-person passage (test 4: Trigger), and no return (test 5). Its reach is about twenty-five years and its extent one afternoon, a potentially costly combination, with a radiator, a bus and two chairs built and thrown away (test 6: Reach and extent). It contradicts the canon: the conversation took place the second time he lost the pen, in the year he died, between adults, and the draft turns it into a formative childhood scene, an explanation where the book needs an illumination (test 7: Chronology). And it spends at leisure, cushioned by tea, the line on which the ending depends. The writing is not at fault; the failure is register, placement and motivation, which is the usual case.

Version B, the revision.

Nadia found the pen in his coat pocket, right where she remembered him keeping it — except it wasn’t the register pen, the plain black one. It was the good pen, the fountain one he only used for the ledger, the one he wasn’t supposed to carry loose because he’d lose it. He’d lost it twice that year already, and made Ma buy a new nib.

She had asked him about it once, the second time. Why he needed the good pen for a stock count nobody was going to read twice. He’d looked at her over his glasses like she’d caught him at something. “Because someone should write it down properly,” he said. “In case anyone ever needs to check.” At the time she’d thought he meant the shop.

She had not thought, until just now, standing by the door with her hand in the pocket of a coat still on its hook, to ask him what he’d actually thought anyone might need to check.

The revision changed the register, not the content: a dramatised scene of 320 words became an exchange of a few lines, entered through a clause, and the sentence the compression check produced turned out to be the father’s own. It attached the cutaway to an object already load-bearing, so the trigger was found, not invented, and remains in her hand after the return. It moved the exchange out of childhood, which removed the determinism, restored the canon and shortened the reach from decades to months, so that no context needs building: the reader already knows this shop. It is a completing analepsis, since the prologue showed the pen pocketed without saying why. It rewrote the return as a changed question: Nadia comes back holding a question she did not have when she reached into the pocket, and the present scene now has to carry it. And it declined the explanatory sentence: nothing says the father knew he was ill, and the reader assembles it. It reframes a detail the reader already has, the pen carried loose, from habit to decision, which is reader knowledge at its cheapest, and it opens the book’s argument about records.

What the revision has not solved. The last sentence is the return, the changed question, the thematic statement and the emotional beat at once, close to the ceiling on multiplicity, and an editor might ask whether At the time she’d thought he meant the shop has already done its work. The trigger is very apt: the defence is that the aptness was established by the prologue, two hundred pages earlier, and a reader meeting the scene alone would find it neat. Nadia has learned not to touch the coat (The Descriptive Detail, Chapter 4), so the scene before this one must give her a reason to put her hand in its pocket now, and that reason is not yet written. The revision in The Prologue (Chapter 10) gives the prologue a fragment of the line, In case, as the father’s unfinished reason when he pockets the pen, so this cutaway is partly a repeating analepsis, and test 9 (Completing or repeating) asks what the second pass adds: the rest of the line, the occasion, the listener and Nadia’s misreading, which is enough, though the reader will have heard the first two words before Nadia understands them. And one version was never tried: Nadia telling Elena about the exchange, which would give a listener and the chance that Elena reads their father differently.
