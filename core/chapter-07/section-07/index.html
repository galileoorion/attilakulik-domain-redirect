Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below is described by its mechanism and ends with a published work that commits it and succeeds, because the pattern fails through the mechanism, not the surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the feature is harmless. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue adds seven more: the unmotivated cutaway, the redundant flashback, the nested flashback, the better past, the italicised memory, the prologue relocated as a flashback, and the undeveloped repeat.

The momentum killer

Symptom. The cutaway arrives at the most tense moment of the present and stops it, with a payoff smaller than the interruption.

Mechanism. Tension is not preserved during an interruption; it is replaced. A reader given six pages of other material does not hold the earlier tension in reserve but builds a new engagement, and the old tension has to be restarted from a lower level on return. The failure is one of scale, not kind, since a short cut at a peak can sharpen it.

Remedy. At a peak, measure the interruption in sentences. If the material needs more, it is in the wrong place: run test 8 (Placement).

The exception. At the opening of the second part of Les Misérables (1862) Hugo interrupts Jean Valjean’s story with a long account of the battle of Waterloo, fought years before, and the interruption is defensible because the book’s subject is the relation between single lives and historical forces. A book may decide that its interruptions are its argument, and then it must make them long enough to be read as one.

The info-dump backstory

Symptom. Paragraphs or pages of compressed history with no scene-level engine, read as a report rather than felt as consequence, often where a family’s history or an invented world tempts the writer to deliver a whole system at once.

Mechanism. Two failures together. Delivered early, exposition spends an asset that would have generated curiosity. And information is not memorable without a container: a reader given four generations of a family before she cares about anyone in it has nowhere to store the material and retains little of it. The writer, who knows the history intimately, fears the reader will be lost without it; but the reader needs to believe the writer knows the history, which confident specificity achieves, not to know it herself.

Remedy. Ask of each expository sentence which character would say it, to whom and why, and attempt the material as a clause or an iterative sentence before giving it more room.

The exception. Balzac’s Père Goriot (1835) opens with long accounts of the boarding house, its economics and its lodgers’ antecedents, an info-dump by any later standard. The accounts are causal: the novel’s argument is that the lodgers are what the rent makes them, so the rent must be specified before the people can be understood. A book with a determinist thesis needs its determinants on the page.

The second-chapter flashback

Symptom. The novel leaves its present for an extended visit to the past before the end of its second chapter.

Mechanism. A departure is measured against a first narrative, and a first narrative not yet consolidated cannot be departed from. The reader has not formed the model the cutaway would interrupt, so the departure reads as the book not having decided what it is, and the past material has no present to bear on.

Remedy. Delay the first substantial cutaway until the reader could describe the book’s present situation in a sentence, which is usually later than it feels.

The exception. Where the Crawdads Sing (2018) opens on a body found in 1969 and turns at once to 1952, to a six-year-old girl watching her mother walk away from their shack in the North Carolina marsh, and from then on moves between the two strands. It commits the pattern in its first pages and does not suffer from it, because it declares a braid instead of interrupting a present: the reader learns within a few chapters that the book has two timelines and that one is travelling towards the other. The Death of Ivan Ilyich escapes the pattern another way, by making its first chapter, the funeral, complete in itself.

The untethered return

Symptom. The cutaway ends and the present scene resumes as though nothing had happened.

Mechanism. The deficit is never discharged: the reader paid for the departure and receives nothing where payment was due. The cutaway itself may have been excellent, and the fault may lie in one sentence. It persists because the return is written last and treated as re-entry, not payoff.

Remedy. Rewrite the first sentence after the cutaway to carry one of the four changes, preferably a changed question, which is available in nearly every case.

The exception. “El curioso impertinente”, the novella read aloud at the inn in Part I of Don Quixote (1605), runs across three chapters and returns the story to the state it was in. Cervantes was criticised for his interpolations, answered the criticism inside Part II (1615) and largely gave the practice up there. The untethered return was a recognised convention that ceased to be one, and Cervantes’s change of practice is the clearest evidence that this prohibition is historical, not structural.

The childhood-explains-everything cutaway

Symptom. A single childhood scene, usually of humiliation, loss or a parent’s failure, is offered as the origin of an adult’s defining trait.

Mechanism. Three faults at once. Structurally it is the expensive cell, a long reach and a short extent. Psychologically it proposes a model of personality in which one event determines a life, which readers do not recognise from experience. And narratively an explained character is a closed question. The pattern persists because it is what the phrase character motivation is usually taken to mean, and screen convention has made a single traumatic memory the standard sign of an inner life.

Remedy. Prefer illumination to explanation; move the material nearer, since a recent memory usually makes the same point without the determinism; or deliver it in a register that claims no causal authority, the iterative or the clause.

The exception. Tana French’s In the Woods (2007) is built on a childhood afternoon in 1984, when three children went into a wood near Dublin and one came out, found against a tree with his shoes full of blood and no memory of what happened. That boy is now the detective who narrates, working a child’s murder in the same place. The novel takes the pattern’s premise, an adult organised around one afternoon in childhood, and refuses the scene: the memory never returns and the 1984 case is never solved. The origin explains the man by being missing, which turns the device’s reductiveness into its subject. Faulkner’s Sutpen, whose origin arrives at four removes, makes the same turn by another route.
