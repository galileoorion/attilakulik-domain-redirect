Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

3. How It Works

Continue in Reference

The deficit claim in the problem file, which the Reference chapter calls the interest deficit (the attention a cutaway spends before it has delivered anything), is a metaphor, and a writer revises better when she knows what it stands for. Five strands of theory answer five practical questions.

What does a temporal departure ask of the reader?

Research on narrative comprehension gives the cost a possible mechanism. Rolf Zwaan and Gabriel Radvansky’s review of the field (Psychological Bulletin, 1998) describes readers building a situation model, a mental representation of the state of affairs the text describes, tracked along dimensions including protagonist, space, time, causality and intention. A shift on a dimension can require an update and affect reading time. Catherine Emmott’s Narrative Comprehension (1997) adds the reader’s contextual frame, the running record of who is present, where and in what configuration, and distinguishes switching to a new frame from recalling an old one. A flashback may require the reader to keep the present available while building a past situation and then return to it. Keisuke Ohtsuka and William Brewer (Discourse Processes, 1992) presented listeners with five differently ordered versions of a narrative and tested their grasp of event order: the canonical version was easiest, while the flashforward version was the most difficult.

These findings support caution about temporal reordering, not a general ranking in which a backward shift is harder than a forward one. The experiment tested comprehension of short passages heard aloud, not the artistic value or difficulty of whole novels; it cannot predict how any particular reader will experience Absalom, Absalom!.

What is the reader leaving, and how far?

Genette’s first narrative answers the first half. Since anachrony is measured against it, a departure needs some temporal baseline to be felt as one. A long flashback in a book’s second chapter may leave that baseline unclear, unless the book has already established a frame or declared a multi-timeline design.

The second half is answered by two measures. Reach (Genette’s portée) is how far back the cutaway goes from the present of the first narrative; extent (amplitude) is how much story time the cutaway itself covers. They are independent, and the table sets out the four combinations.

Short extent Long extent
Short reach Yesterday’s argument, dramatised: cheap and underused The last six months of a marriage, summarised: cheap, the natural home of summary
Long reach One afternoon thirty years ago: potentially expensive if it needs a new context A childhood or a war: potentially a second strand, needing an architecture

A long reach with a short extent can be expensive when the reader must build a vanished context, other ages, another place, a superseded set of relations, for one scene’s use. That possible imbalance between construction and occupancy, rather than childhood itself, is a reason to question a childhood flashback. Reach can also change while a cutaway runs: a long analepsis that moves forward closes the distance to the present, as Nelly Dean’s chronicle in Wuthering Heights starts about thirty years before Lockwood’s present and runs on until it meets it, and the reader feels the closing as momentum.

Genette’s further distinctions locate the trouble. An external analepsis lies wholly before the first narrative begins and may revise what the reader believed about its antecedents; an internal analepsis falls inside its span and may repeat or contradict an earlier account of those events; a mixed analepsis starts outside and runs in. Within the internal case, a completing analepsis fills an ellipsis the reader knew was there, and arrives as an answer, while a repeating analepsis returns to material already narrated in order to recolour it, and is worth the reader’s time only if the second telling differs. The Good Soldier and Beloved are built almost entirely of repeating analepses and survive because each return differs from the last, which is the warning against reading the cost model as a prohibition.

Can the cost be paid in advance?

Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978) answers this. He distinguishes exposition that is preliminary (delivered up front), delayed (action first, antecedents later) and distributed (dispersed and never gathered), and three interests a narrative can raise. Suspense is uncertainty about the future, discharged when the outcome arrives; curiosity is uncertainty about the past, discharged when the gap is filled; surprise is the discovery that there was a gap at all, opened and filled at once. A cutaway that answers a question the reader is already asking is not an interruption but a payment. The household novel uses this: from the drawer onwards the reader wants to know why the looser hand in the ledger begins on 9 March, so when Dov’s chapter goes back to the evening of 9 March (The Viewpoint Shift, Chapter 13) it pays a debt the book contracted a long way earlier. Hence the rule: open the gap before you fill it. Prolepsis can do related work from the other side, since a reader told what will happen may want to know how. The Death of Ivan Ilyich uses a different arrangement: its first chapter establishes a funeral as a frame, and the second turns back to the life that ended there.

When is the past the story?

Paul Ricoeur’s Time and Narrative (1983–85; translated by Kathleen McLaughlin and David Pellauer) supplies the counterweight: locally a cutaway interrupts, but at the scale of the book the arrangement of past against present is the book’s meaning. Trauma theory pushes further. Cathy Caruth’s Unclaimed Experience (1996) describes traumatic experience by its belatedness: an event not fully registered when it happened returns intrusively, so that it cannot be told in sequence and possesses the present rather than preceding it. Sethe’s rememory in Beloved, a past that persists as a place someone else can walk into, is its fictional form. Caruth’s model is contested, by Ruth Leys (2000) on its clinical basis, by Dominick LaCapra (2001) on its collapse of acting out into working through, and by Stef Craps (2013) on its Eurocentrism; for a writer the upshot is the same either way. The conventions of fragmentation and return exist and readers recognise them, and a book that borrows them without the subject, fragmenting material that is merely withheld, has taken on obligations it cannot meet.

The dates of the convention

The term flashback has early cinematic uses, and Maureen Turim’s Flashbacks in Film (1989) examines devices such as the dissolve, the voice-over and the return to a face in film history. Earlier narrative held its past differently. When Eurycleia recognises Odysseus by his scar in Book XIX of the Odyssey, Homer breaks off for some seventy lines to tell how he got it, and Erich Auerbach’s Mimesis (1946; translated by Willard Trask, 1953) argues that nothing is suspended while he does, because Homer’s narrative has no background held in reserve. The chapter’s preferred cutaway, with a motivated trigger and a consequential return, depends on a suspended foreground. These are practical conventions for certain present-driven novels, reinforced by workshop teaching; they are not rules for all narrative.
