Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The domain model asks one question of any cutaway: what does it buy? It turns the instruction tighten your backstory into separable questions with separable remedies. The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) sets out four domains for any unit of narrative, plot, character, theme and texture, and proposes a fifth, the relational domain, which The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) adopts for dialogue. This chapter audits a cutaway’s relational work under character, as The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4) does for description, and adds a fifth domain of its own, reader knowledge, because control over when the reader learns something is the cutaway’s distinctive power. It proposes a sixth, the structural domain. Its danger is mechanical application: a writer who learns that good cutaways serve several domains starts loading them until they visibly perform.

Plot: the cutaway explains what is happening now

The plot domain buys the causal history behind a present situation, carried by the causal antecedent, the origin, the plant, the precedent and the correction. It is the domain a writer can see a need for in advance, and therefore the one most often served thinly: a cutaway written so that the reader will understand why the shop is failing satisfies a requirement and does nothing else. Causal information compresses, so if the whole return on a scene is the shop had been losing money for years, the scene should be a sentence. The precedent, which shows that this has happened before, is undervalued: it turns an event into a pattern at little more cost than an instance. In Faulkner’s Absalom, Absalom! (1936) Thomas Sutpen’s boyhood humiliation at a plantation door is offered as the origin of his whole design, but at four removes, as something Sutpen told General Compson, who told his son, who told Quentin, who tells Shreve, so that it is explained and questioned at once.

Character: the cutaway explains who someone is now

The character domain buys the history behind a present behaviour, through the formative moment, the measure of then against now, the withheld justification, the recolouring and the habitual. It is the domain where the flashback register is most often right, because the gap between knowing and feeling is widest here: a reader told that a man was humiliated at fourteen has a fact, and a reader who watched it has an experience she will apply to everything he does. It is also where drafts assume a person is explained by an origin, so two things a character cutaway can do need distinguishing. Explanation answers why is he like this? and closes the question. Illumination shows something that makes the present behaviour legible without making it determined, leaving the reader to construct the relation. The difference in practice is usually one sentence, the one that draws the connection, and declining it is nearly always the better choice. Stevens’s memory in The Remains of the Day (1989) of serving at a conference while his father died upstairs is given without one sentence linking it to the man narrating, and the reader makes the link. A recolouring, which changes how established behaviour reads, has a strict ordering rule: the behaviour must already be in place, so the cutaway must come late, and Beloved (1987) withholds the full account of Sethe’s act until the reader has been inside her ordinary competence and love.

Theme: the cutaway restages the argument across time

The theme domain buys evidence that the book’s concern is not particular to one moment, by restaging it under other conditions: the ironic parallel, compounding cost, the lesson the present refutes. It is the domain most easily claimed rather than shown, and the test is whether the rhyme survives the deletion of every sentence that points it out. Its peculiar resource is that past and present can share a page, so the reader performs the comparison herself. The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886) builds its whole retrospect on this domain, each episode of career, marriage and house an instance of one claim made at the outset.

Texture: the cutaway shapes the reading

The texture domain buys a change of rhythm, register or sensory palette set against the pressure of the present. Craft teaching distrusts it, because this flashback is here for the rhythm can justify anything, and a change of pace is available more cheaply from a short scene of a different kind, a paragraph of description, a chapter break or a sequel in Scene and Sequel’s sense (Chapter 2). A cutaway justified by texture alone is usually the most expensive solution to the problem it solves, which is why texture appears in strong cutaways as a secondary function. Delay at peak tension is a real technique within limits: an interruption of a few lines can sharpen a peak, and an interruption of six pages replaces the tension with an engagement in other material. Measure a delay at a peak in sentences, not pages. Proust’s madeleine shows how sensory texture can carry memory and a meditation on recovering the past at once.

Reader knowledge: the fifth domain

The fifth domain buys not information but information timed. Like other narrative units, a cutaway can revise what the reader thinks she knows. Its particular resource is to change when she learns about earlier events, and that order can change their meaning. Its carriers are dramatic irony created or resolved, the corrected assumption, calibrated suspense and the reframed detail. Placement relative to the present scene decides the effect, as the table shows for a fixed cutaway moved through three positions.

Position Reader’s state during the present scene Effect
Before Knows what the character does not Dramatic irony: she watches the mistake approach
During, interrupting Learns it as the scene runs Recontextualisation in real time, the highest-risk placement
After Rereads a scene already read Retrospective correction: she feels her own misreading

Each makes a different book, but a draft that placed its cutaway where the writer happened to think of it has usually chosen the second. The reframed detail is the cheapest member of the set: it adds no fact, but makes a detail the reader already holds mean something new, and it is worth looking for in any revision. The household novel’s pen is the case worked in the worked analyses. The over-delivered form is the reversal treadmill, in which each cutaway overturns the last until the reader stops forming beliefs, which destroys the mechanism, since a correction needs a belief to correct. In Great Expectations (1861) the novel plants a specific false account of Pip’s past, that Miss Havisham is his benefactor, and demolishes it by Magwitch’s account of his years since the marshes; the reveal depends on the belief having been specific.

A proposed sixth: the structural domain

The five domains so far describe what past material delivers. Some cutaways are chosen for what they do to the shape of the book. A cutaway serves the structural domain when its main function is architectural: it establishes, maintains or completes a pattern in the book’s design, and something like it would be needed at that point even if its content were somewhat different. Three forms recur. The braided timeline alternates a present and a past strand on a declared rhythm, as Kate Morton’s The Forgotten Garden (2008) moves among 1913, when a little girl is left aboard a ship departing London and later found in Australia, 1975, when Nell, who learned on her twenty-first birthday that she was that child, goes looking for where she came from, and 2005, when her granddaughter Cassandra takes up the search; in Delia Owens’s Where the Crawdads Sing (2018) a strand beginning in 1952 runs forward until it reaches the investigation of 1969. The returning frame opens a situation the book departs from and comes back to, as in Wuthering Heights and Tayeb Salih’s Season of Migration to the North (1966; translated by Denys Johnson-Davies). The recurring approach circles a withheld event, each approach nearer, as Beloved circles the shed.

The case for admitting the domain is that in these structures placement is set by the design rather than the content, so a model that judges each cutaway against the present question it interrupts has no words for a departure that is expected; that the structural function has failures of its own, strands progressing at incompatible rates, a frame that never closes, a centre reached too early, which no improvement to individual cutaways repairs; and that a chapter of the past strand can be load-bearing without being locally interesting, which the content domains mark as weak. The case against is respectable: alternation may be texture at book scale, a recurring approach may be reader knowledge seen from far away, and a domain answering what does this do for the book? sits oddly among domains answering what does this deliver here?

This chapter adopts the structural domain for diagnosis, with two conditions. It is unavailable before the pattern is declared, in practice before its second or third instance, so a braided book must declare its architecture early. And it is the easiest justification to assert and the hardest to check: it’s a dual-timeline novel, so this chapter belongs here is a claim about the contents page, not the chapter. Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) rejects it as a domain of the cutaway: in The Forgotten Garden and Where the Crawdads Sing the past material recurs with a question of its own, so it is a strand, judged by Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture’s tests (Chapter 8), and material that recurs without one fails there as an explaining strand.

Must every domain be served?

No. Two domains are the working target, three is strong, and five is often a sign of strain; the count exists to catch the single-domain cutaway that should have been a sentence. The weighting is book-specific, a crime novel’s cutaways running on plot and reader knowledge and a novel of consciousness’s on character and texture. The table summarises what failure looks like in each domain.

Domain Under-delivery Over-delivery
Plot The missing antecedent: the reader feels she skipped a page The explained present: nothing may simply be true
Character The unexplained refusal The psychological receipt: a documented cause for every trait
Theme The decorative past, connected to nothing The illustrated thesis: a parallel the reader completes early
Texture The flat cutaway: the past sounds like the present The postcard: the past out-writes the book
Reader knowledge The flat reveal: a new fact that recolours nothing The reversal treadmill
Structure (proposed) The undeclared pattern Alternation kept up past its purpose

Read across a manuscript, the table usually shows failures gathered in one column, which says more about the draft than any single row.
