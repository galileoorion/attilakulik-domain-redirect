Chapter 14 · The Subplot

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern fails through a mechanism rather than a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent. At this scale several exceptions are among the most admired novels in their languages, which suggests that the rules describe a present convention more closely than they describe the novel. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue has thirteen patterns, among them the vestigial subplot, the comic-relief thread, convergence by coincidence, the digression and the thematic alibi.

Parallel play

Symptom. Two chains run the length of the book without affecting or commenting on one another, and the reader reads one of them impatiently.

Mechanism. No convergence of either kind. The second chain’s kernels never become the main plot’s, and no analogy or contrast is marked, so the reader cannot turn the diversion into meaning. The failure is not that the threads never meet but that nothing in either changes because the other exists. It survives revision because each thread is satisfying alone, and revision scene by scene cannot see it.

Remedy. Establish a relation and mark it: bring compared scenes nearer, give the threads a shared object or decision-shape, let one thread’s people know the other’s exist. Or accept that there is no relation, and take the weaker chain out of the book.

Exception. Anna Karenina (1875–77). Anna’s chain and Levin’s are joined causally at the start, since Vronsky’s pursuit of Anna leaves Kitty free, in time, to marry Levin, and they touch once more late in Part Seven, when Stiva takes Levin to Anna’s house and he is charmed for an evening; nothing follows. What holds the novel together is analogy in Richard Levin’s sense, marked constantly across eight parts: two households, two jealousies, two encounters with death, two people trying to live truthfully, one destroyed by the attempt. Tolstoy, defending the book’s architecture in a letter, located its connection not in the plot or the characters’ relations but in an inner linkage. The rule therefore concerns relation, not intersection.

The main-plot hijack

Symptom. The secondary thread becomes more compelling than the primary. Readers skip ahead to it; the writer notices that its chapters are the ones she enjoys writing.

Mechanism. The book’s centre of gravity has moved and its structure has not. Investment follows interest, not outline, so the main plot’s climax lands on a reader whose investment has gone elsewhere, and an event of great significance arrives with little feeling. Two benign causes keep the pattern common: secondary threads are written with less anxiety, because less rides on them, and the subplot is usually the newer idea.

Remedy. The promotion test, honestly answered. Then promote the thread, restructure the book as a double plot, or, less often, reduce the subplot deliberately. Leaving the arrangement as it stands is the one response that does not work.

Exception. Vanity Fair (1847–48). Amelia Sedley is the nominal heroine and Becky Sharp the thread that eats the book; Thackeray’s attention follows Becky, and so does the reader’s. The novel survives because it makes the hijack its subject. Its subtitle is A Novel without a Hero, and the narrator’s management of the reader’s preferences demonstrates that we would rather watch an adventuress than a good woman, and that the preference is not to our credit. A hijack the book knows about is a structure; one it has not noticed is a fault.

The late-arriving thread

Symptom. A thread introduced in the second half, or braided in twice early and then absent for two hundred pages, converges decisively at the climax, and the convergence lands on weight the book has not built.

Mechanism. Reinstatement failure compounded by an investment shortfall: the reader is asked for a feeling proportionate to an investment she had no opportunity to make. The result is not confusion but flatness at the moment of greatest intended effect. Late threads are often the solution to a plotting problem found in a third draft, and solutions arrive where the problem was noticed rather than where they need to be planted.

Remedy. Braid backwards. A thread converging at the climax should appear in the quarter before it and be established in the first third; two short crossings in the dormant stretch usually cost a few hundred words.

Exception. War and Peace (1865–69). Platon Karataev enters in the fourth volume with no prior existence, and Pierre’s change in the prisoners’ shed, where he meets him, answers the question the previous thousand pages have failed to answer. Karataev succeeds because he is not a thread but a term: he has no chain, and what converges is not a plot but the question Pierre has carried since his first conversations about freemasonry. A late arrival can pay off an early investment when the investment was in the question rather than in the thread, and the question is still open.

Convergence by encounter

Symptom. The threads converge because the characters meet: they are in one room at the end, introduced, talking. Nothing else changes.

Mechanism. A meeting is not a convergence. Delete the encounter and both chains proceed unaltered, so the convergence test returns nothing. The pattern is a common false positive because a meeting looks like what convergence is supposed to be, and outlines mark it as the point where threads join. It is partly a habit borrowed from the screen, where actors sharing a frame do real work; prose has no shared frame, and a meeting must produce a consequence or it has produced only an image.

Remedy. Ask what each character wants from the other and make the wants incompatible. Convergence is conflict, not contact; if nobody wants anything from anybody, the encounter is a curtain call.

Exception. Love Actually (Richard Curtis, 2003). The film braids ten love stories, linked mainly by kinship and employment (the Prime Minister’s sister, Karen, is married to Harry, who is tempted by his secretary; a junior member of the Prime Minister’s household is the object of his own attention), and it opens and closes at the arrivals gate at Heathrow, where several of its characters are reunited. As plot, the closing gathering converges nothing. It works because the encounter is the film’s claim, made in the voice-over to the opening montage of arrivals: that love is visible wherever people meet. When the meeting is the argument, an empty encounter is the form of the point. Ulysses gives the case in prose, where Bloom and Stephen’s meeting over cocoa leads nowhere and is felt against a convergence that has been happening all day by theme.

The romance bolted on

Symptom. A love story runs beside a plot to which it is unrelated: the detective’s relationship, the soldier’s sweetheart. Its obstacles are misunderstandings, and its outcome could be reversed without changing a scene elsewhere.

Mechanism. The engine test passes and the tax test fails. A romance chain is easy to build correctly because its shape is culturally familiar, and the familiarity is what makes a detached one inert: the reader recognises the shape at once and so attends to little in it. The pattern persists through commercial expectation in several genres and through a writer’s sense that a protagonist needs a private life.

Remedy. Make the relationship about what the main plot is about, a site of the same conflict rather than a refuge from it. A romance whose obstacle is the value the protagonist will not give up becomes a character testing ground.

Exception. Middlemarch (1871–72), the thread of Fred Vincy and Mary Garth. It presents as the most detachable thing in the novel, a courtship comedy in which an idle young man must find a respectable occupation, and it barely touches Dorothea. It succeeds because it is the novel’s control case on vocation: Dorothea wants a life of consequence and is denied one, Lydgate wants one and loses it, Bulstrode buys one with concealed money, and Fred, the least serious person in the book, is the only one who acquires a usable trade, taught slowly by Mary’s father. Mary’s refusal to marry an idler is what forces the training, and reversing the courtship’s outcome would change the novel’s argument about work. A romance is bolted on when its outcome is thematically inert and integral when its outcome is one of the book’s answers.
