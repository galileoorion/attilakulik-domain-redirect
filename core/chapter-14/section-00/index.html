Chapter 14 · The Subplot

The Subplot

Continue in Reference

What a second story costs, and what it must supply

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 14. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

In brief. A subplot is a second chain of causally linked scenes, with its own goal, conflict and consequence, running beside the main plot and competing with it for pages and attention. It is the largest optional unit in a novel, and every page it takes is a page not given to the story the reader came for, so it must supply something the main plot cannot supply by its own nature: a counterpoint, a test of character, relief, a wider world, or a convergence in which its turning points become the main plot’s. The chapter’s tool is the convergence test: find the point at which deleting the thread would break the main plot rather than merely thin it. Use the chapter when a secondary thread feels like filler, when readers prefer the subplot, when a late convergence lands flat, or when a book with several threads cannot decide which is primary.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Distinguish a subplot from a recurring character, a digression and a motif.
  2. Name the domains a thread serves and what it supplies that the main plot cannot.
  3. Locate a thread’s convergence point, and distinguish convergence from a meeting.
  4. Audit page-share and dormancy, and price a thread’s resumption.
  5. Decide whether a thread should be cut, converted, promoted or recognised as a co-plot.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds the complete chapter, The Subplot: Levin, Empson and the Victorian publishing economy in full, fourteen braiding and convergence structures, thirteen failure patterns, eight close readings (among them Middlemarch, Anna Karenina, Les Misérables and King Lear), the subplot in the short story, the novella and the series, seminar questions and the sources.
