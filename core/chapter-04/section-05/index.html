Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

The domains say what a detail can do. The mechanism says how one detail does several things, how a detail comes to mean more than it names, and how a detail planted early comes to mean something later.

Multiplicity under a constraint

Multiplicity, making one unit of text do several jobs, was introduced at the scale of the scene in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1). At the scale of the detail it becomes a question about emphasis: which particulars should the reader notice, and which can contribute quietly? The situation-model research discussed in How It Works does not say how many details a novel contains or how many any reader will retain. One detail can serve several domains, but it does not occupy a measurable single slot in memory.

The section turns on a useful distinction. Compression puts several things into a small space; it can succeed, but an overstuffed clause may call attention to the effort. Selection changes what is described, perhaps finding one object that already suggests a plot fact, a relation or a date as well as texture. In revision, compare a compressed sentence with one built around a different detail and judge which serves the passage.

One sentence, six domains

In chapter 8 of Great Expectations, Pip is taken into Miss Havisham’s room and reports: It was when I stood before her, avoiding her eyes, that I took note of the surrounding objects in detail, and saw that her watch had stopped at twenty minutes to nine, and that a clock in the room had stopped at twenty minutes to nine. The original sentence has forty-six words. Pip’s avoidance of her eyes directs his attention to the objects. He has already noticed that the watch and chain have not been put on, so the watch is a personal possession rather than one worn on her body. The matching stopped time suggests a deliberate act and a prior event; its precision may also support a reading of arrested time. These are possible functions of the sentence, not a measure of what every reader retains.

Where multiplicity lives

Multiplicity can arise in four plain places as well as in metaphor. The first is the choice of noun: chokecherry, not tree. The second is which attribute is reported: a coat’s colour may provide texture, while its frayed cuff can suggest time and habit. The third is an object’s condition and who maintains it. In Mick Herron’s Real Tigers, Slough House is described as a place where things are “either broken or mended”; that condition can also be read as a comment on the staff’s careers. The fourth is grammatical aspect: had once been, used to, still hung. A metaphor can also connect functions, but the writer should consider what its comparison brings into the scene.

Multiplicity can become conspicuous. When every detail seems charged, some readers may see the arrangement more readily than the room. The chapter calls the point at which a passage begins to feel strained the density ceiling: a revision prompt, not a measured threshold. A writer can test whether some details should carry one modest purpose or remain apparently incidental. A highly charged passage may also be a deliberate formal choice.

What a detail names and what it implies

A detail can be considered at two levels. Denotation is what it names in context; connotation is an association or implication that may arise from that choice: mood, class, foreboding or judgement. A chipped teacup and a bone-white teacup might be the same cup, but their descriptions invite different associations, which depend on the scene and reader. The pair is a useful counterpart to text and subtext in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3). In a semiotic account associated with Barthes, a first-order sign can become the signifier of a further meaning. Connotations can change across cultures and periods; neither those changes nor a detail’s effect can be ranked by a universal rate.

Twelve strategies, and substitution

“Choose a better detail” may be hard to act on; a repertoire of named strategies offers options. A descriptive strategy is a way of selecting and presenting a detail. This chapter uses twelve. Metonymy evokes a thing through something associated with it (Gatsby’s shirts can suggest his wealth; Charles Bovary’s cap can suggest Charles), while synecdoche uses part for whole or whole for part (a hand for a worker). An estranged detail may withhold the familiar name; a catalogue may make order do the work. A negative detail names an absence, often implying an expected state and its deviation. A comparison’s voice or viewpoint matters; a focalised detail is selected through a perceiving mind. In Barthes’s account, an apparently superfluous detail can contribute to a reality effect. Repetition can be identical or varied. Ekphrasis is vivid verbal presentation of a visual subject, often a made object in modern examples. Lessing’s Laocoön (1766) discusses Homer’s presentation of Achilles’ shield as it is made; this offers a way to test a description in motion, not proof that process always surpasses a still image. A technical register describes through a trade’s vocabulary.

The repertoire can support substitution. If a passage feels flat, name its current strategy, then try a focalised detail, a negative detail or a technical register and compare the effects. The exercise changes the principle of selection as well as the wording.

The motif: how a detail planted early means something late

A single detail can suggest a theme; a return can develop or complicate that suggestion. Genette’s narrative frequency describes how often an event is told relative to how often it happens: once for once (singulative), several tellings of one event (repetitive), or one telling of repeated events (iterative). A motif is different: a detail, image or phrase recurs and gains significance, often in separate scenes. Those scenes can each be narrated singulatively. Identical repetition can itself be expressive, as Dickens’s repeated fog shows; variation gives readers another basis for comparing appearances.

No fixed number of appearances makes a motif legible. Two can establish an echo; further returns can alter it. An unemphatic first appearance is one option, while a conspicuous first appearance can create anticipation. A final return may be changed or explicitly interpreted. Fitzgerald’s green light at the end of Daisy’s dock figures in three scenes: Gatsby reaches towards it before Nick identifies it; when Daisy visits, Nick comments that its former significance to Gatsby may have diminished; and in the closing passage Nick connects it with the future Gatsby pursued. The middle and final scenes both contain commentary. The object remains while its place in Gatsby’s and Nick’s understanding changes.
