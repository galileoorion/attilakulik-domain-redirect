Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

The domains say what a detail can do. The mechanism says how one detail does several things, how a detail comes to mean more than it names, and how a detail planted early comes to mean something later.

Multiplicity under a constraint

Multiplicity, the practice of making one unit of text do several jobs at once, was introduced at the scale of the scene in The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1). At the scale of the detail it becomes a constraint. A reader keeps few particulars from a book, and the number does not rise with the writer’s effort; as an illustration only, a novel of ninety thousand words might hold a few thousand descriptive details, of which a reader will remember a few dozen. The scarce resource is therefore not accuracy but retention, the question is not how much to describe but which details will be kept, and a detail that carries three domains occupies one place in the reader’s memory and pays for three.

The section turns on one distinction. Compression says five things in the space of one, by revising a sentence, and it feels like effort and fails as an overstuffed clause. Selection finds the one thing that already contains five, by changing the object, and it feels like ease, a lucky noun. A writer who tries to make a slack detail carry more by adding modifiers has compressed. The move is to replace the detail: to ask what other true thing about the object, or what other object in the room, would give the same texture while also carrying a plot fact, a relation or a date.

One sentence, six domains

In chapter 8 of Great Expectations, Pip is taken into Miss Havisham’s room and reports: I noticed that her watch had stopped at twenty minutes to nine, and that a clock in the room had stopped at twenty minutes to nine. The sentence has no figure, no unusual word and no rhythm to speak of, and it repeats seven words verbatim. In twenty-six words it reaches all six domains. I noticed is the child’s selection, attention going to clocks rather than to the woman (character), and the adult narrator’s refusal to interpret it. Her watch is worn on the body, so the stoppage is personal before it is domestic, and a house with watches and clocks is a class (world). Had stopped is pluperfect, a completed event and therefore a before (temporal), and an enigma with an answer the novel will supply (plot). Twenty minutes to nine authenticates by its precision before it signifies (texture), and makes arrested time the book’s condition (theme). The second clock turns an accident into a policy, and the repeated phrase performs the arrest it reports. The sentence does not feel efficient; it feels like a boy noticing two clocks. That is the mark of selection: the reader experiences plainness while the text does several jobs. A draft of the sentence would probably have contained the word eerie.

Where multiplicity lives

Revision usually looks for multiplicity in metaphor, but most of the work is done in four plainer places. The first is the choice of noun: chokecherry, not tree. The second is which attribute is reported: a coat’s colour is a texture move, and its frayed cuff a temporal and character move at the same cost. The third is the state of a thing and who maintains it, since objects in fiction are nearly always repaired, neglected, borrowed or dusted weekly by someone unnamed, and condition is where plot, character, relation and time meet. Mick Herron’s spy novels about Slough House, the shabby London office where the intelligence service parks its failures, describe it as a place where everything is “either broken or mended”, and the condition of the building is the condition of its staff’s careers. The fourth is grammatical aspect: had once been, used to, still hung. Metaphor can multiply too, but it is the riskiest instrument, because a figure that misses draws attention to the author and a simile always brings a second object into the scene.

Multiplicity has a ceiling. When every detail is significant, emphasis is destroyed, because emphasis is relational and a detail lands as important only if its neighbours do not. The reader cannot tell what to hold, and begins to see the author arranging the room. A described passage therefore needs some details doing one thing and a few that appear to do none; the chapter calls the point past which a passage reads as strain the density ceiling. A passage in which every element is charged has become allegory, a different contract.

What a detail names and what it implies

Every detail works on two layers. Denotation is what it names, the literal referent any reader of the language would agree on. Connotation is what arrives because this true thing was said rather than another: mood, class, foreboding, judgement. A chipped teacup and a bone-white teacup can be the same cup, and neither report is more accurate, but the first implies neglect or a small grief nobody has attended to and the second implies formality and something about to break. The choice of which true attribute to report is where meaning enters the sentence. The pair is the descriptive counterpart of text and subtext in The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3). In the semiotic tradition Barthes drew on, connotation is a second-order sign: the whole first sign, the chipped cup, becomes the signifier of something further. Two things follow. Connotation is cultural, supplied by the reader’s repertoire, so it ages faster than structure; and the first order is not cancelled, since a detail that stops being plausibly present in order to carry its freight has become an emblem, and the reader feels the object leave the room.

Twelve strategies, and substitution

“Choose a better detail” cannot be acted on; a repertoire of named strategies can. A descriptive strategy is a way of selecting and presenting a detail with its own gain and cost. The Reference chapter sets out twelve. Metonymy names a thing by something associated with it (Gatsby’s shirts for his fortune), and synecdoche names a whole by a part (Charles Bovary’s cap for Charles). The estranged detail withholds the name; the catalogue makes its order do the work. The negative detail names what is absent, establishing at once the expected state, the deviation and a perceiver who noticed it. The comparative detail arrives through a simile, which is always somebody’s; the focalised detail is chosen by a perceiving mind; the reality-effect detail signifies only that a world is being reported; and repetition may be identical or varied. The ekphrastic set piece describes a made object at length, and Lessing’s Laocoön (1766) observed that Homer describes Achilles’ shield by narrating its making, so that an extended description survives better as a process than as a picture. The technical register uses a trade’s vocabulary and buys authority fast.

The repertoire’s use is substitution. A passage that is not working is often the right material in the wrong strategy, so name the strategy it uses (most flat passages are unfocalised catalogues), rewrite it as a focalised detail, then as a negative detail, then in a technical register, and compare before choosing. The exercise changes the principle of selection rather than the wording, and the principle is where description succeeds or fails.

The motif: how a detail planted early means something late

A single detail cannot carry theme, but a detail that returns can. Genette’s category of frequency gives the terms: a narrative may tell once what happened once (singulative narration), tell several times what happened once (repeating narration), or tell once what happened many times (iterative narration). A motif narrates the same object singulatively on several occasions and leaves the reader to make the comparison. An identical repetition delivers no new information, so the reader, who treats repetition as marked, looks for the addition, finds none, and attributes the repetition to the author. A varied repetition delivers a difference, legible because the object is constant: the object is the ruler and the change is the reading. The object’s own state is the strongest thing to vary, then who stands in relation to it, then its context, then the words used for it.

Three working rules follow. Three appearances is the practical minimum, since one is an object and two may pass as coincidence. The first appearance should be unemphatic, planted as world detail and promoted only in retrospect; a motif announced at its introduction is a symbol, and the reader watches it instead of the story. And the last appearance should be the most changed and the least explained. Fitzgerald’s green light at the end of Daisy’s dock shows all three: Gatsby reaches towards it before Nick knows what it is; once Daisy is in the room, Nick suspects that for Gatsby its “colossal significance” has vanished; and on the last page it has become the future that recedes before us. One lamp, three states of a man’s relation to it.
