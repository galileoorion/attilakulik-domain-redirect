Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a screen reading and a revision case. The method is the same throughout: name the strategy, audit the domains, and say what the passage would lose under substitution.

Great Expectations: a room made of what has stopped

Pip is taken into a room lit only by candles, where a woman sits in a wedding dress that has gone yellow. The room is described almost entirely by what is missing or arrested. There is no daylight. Her watch and the clock have both stopped at twenty minutes to nine. She has one shoe on, and the other lies on the table near her hand. On a long table under a canopy stands the bride-cake, so overgrown with fungus and cobweb that its shape can no longer be made out, with spiders running in and out of it.

The strategy is the negative detail, and the domain at work is time. Almost nothing is said of the architecture, the size of the room or the furniture as furniture; what is specified is state: stopped, unworn, yellowed, overgrown. Substitute a spatial description, with the position of the door and the pattern of the carpet, and every fact would still be true and the passage worthless. A place can be fully described in temporal terms and barely in spatial ones, which is the strongest single argument for the temporal domain set out in The Taxonomy.

By the count test the passage is extravagant, yet it is the opposite of an inventory, because its order carries an argument. It moves from the woman to her clothes, to the instruments of time, to the room, to the cake, to the vermin: from the human centre outward to the decay consuming it, which is the direction of the novel’s account of her. The bride-cake is also a motif’s first appearance. Miss Havisham later points her stick at the long table and tells Pip that this is where she will be laid when she is dead, so the object changes in the reader’s understanding without changing physically.

Beloved: a figure is somebody’s

Sethe’s back is scarred from a whipping. In Toni Morrison’s Beloved (1987) it is first described by Amy Denver, the white girl who finds her on the run, as a tree, a chokecherry tree, with trunk and branches and even blossom. Sethe has never seen her own back and keeps Amy’s word for it.

The strategy is the comparative detail, and the reading turns on who owns the figure. Its denotation is raised, branching scar tissue. As Amy speaks it, its connotation is something growing and beautiful, something that can be looked at; what it conceals is an act of torture and a system in which a person is property. It reveals Amy, a girl who reaches for the prettiest figure to hand, kind and evasive in one gesture, and it reveals Sethe, who must use somebody else’s word for what she cannot see. Had the tree been the narrator’s simile, the novel would have made a wound decorative. Because it is Amy’s, the reader receives the image’s beauty and its inadequacy together, as a fact about how the unbearable is spoken of. When Paul D later touches the scars, he thinks of them not as growth but as the work of an ironsmith, and the motif’s variation carries the novel’s question of whether what was done to Sethe can be seen as anything but manufacture.

The chapter’s tests reach the edge of their competence here. The removal test and the domain audit both work, and neither touches what matters: whether a figure of speech may be allowed to make a wound bearable to look at. That is an ethical question about description, and the Coda returns to it.

Klara and the Sun: the estranged perceiver

Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun (2021) is narrated by Klara, a solar-powered “Artificial Friend” bought as a companion for a sick girl. The novel is a sustained case of what Shklovsky described in Tolstoy’s horse: the human world reported by a perceiver who does not share its names. Klara believes the Sun gives her nourishment and may heal the girl, and she comes to fear a machine working in the street outside, which she calls the Cootings Machine, because its pollution blocks the Sun. The reader sees a piece of street machinery and its pollution; Klara sees an enemy. Nothing in her description is false, and the distance between the two readings is the book’s pathos.

Ishiguro also uses perception itself as a descriptive detail. When a scene is emotionally ambiguous, Klara’s visual field breaks into panels, and in one such moment “that whole section of the room had become partitioned”, with the Mother’s narrowed eyes repeated from box to box. The partition is a fact about how Klara sees, reported as plainly as the furniture, and the reader infers from it what Klara cannot name: that the scene is too much for her to resolve. Emotion is delivered by its occasion. The plausibility of attention is solved by construction, since everything Klara notices is what such a mind would notice; and the cost is that the estranged register must be held for the whole book, which Ishiguro manages by keeping its vocabulary small and returning to the same few objects, the Sun above all.

A screen reading: Parasite

Bong Joon-ho’s Parasite (2019) makes its decisive detail of the one sense a camera cannot record. The Kim family have taken work in the Park household under false identities. Hidden under the living-room table one night, Ki-taek, now the Parks’ driver, hears Mr Park tell his wife that the driver’s smell reminds him of an old radish, or a rag that has been boiled, the smell of people who ride the subway, and that it “crosses the line”. At the garden party that ends the film, Park recoils and holds his nose at the smell of the man who has been living in the bunker beneath the house, and Ki-taek stabs him.

Film can only deliver smell through attention: someone notices, someone reacts, and the reaction is filmed. That constraint turns the detail into a test of the plausibility of attention made visible, and it makes smell a motif that varies by who notices it and in whose hearing. The detail carries plot (it provokes the killing), character (Park’s fastidiousness and Ki-taek’s shame), theme (a class line no disguise can cross) and the relation between the two men, and the first remark is unemphatic enough to pass as a rich man’s complaint.

The household revision case: the coat on the hook

The draft is the hallway paragraph quoted in The Problem: Nadia climbs to the flat above the shop where her father lived, and the narration lists a narrow dim hall, a worn carpet, a ticking radiator, coats, an umbrella stand, the post, a mirror, free newspapers and a smell of dust and cooking, ending with it was strange to think that her father would never come through the door again.

Run through the tests, the draft fails nearly all of them. Removed, it costs only the fact that she went in. Every element is a category detail, true of any hallway in contemporary British fiction, and any element could be swapped for another (a shoe rack for the umbrella stand) without change. Any narrator could have written it, and a woman entering her dead father’s flat does not survey the furniture evenly. It offers eleven details, of which at most two are meant to be kept, and it is almost all sight. It is shuffle-invariant, and its principle of selection is “because they were there”. Two further faults sit outside the tests. The last sentence names the feeling the details should have produced, and the mirror is a promise the draft never means to keep, since a mirror in a hallway in a novel about a dead man reads as an announcement.

The revision:

The coat still hung by the door, army green gone the colour of dishwater, and someone — meaning kindness — had brushed the shoulders free of dust every week since March. He had worn it every winter behind the counter, over the ledger. The left cuff was frayed through where he used to hook his thumb before he spoke, a habit their mother called thinking with his hands. Nadia had learned not to touch it. She had also learned, without meaning to, how many days it took the dust to show again on the green, because she watched, and did not brush it herself, and told herself this was not the same as counting.

The revision replaced the inventory with one object, at about the same length (112 words against 109): a coat was chosen instead of a hallway, which is selection rather than compression. Its principle can be stated in a sentence: these details because they are the things nobody has dealt with since March. It reports different true attributes, the colour as a fact about elapsed time and the cuff rather than the buttons. Everything in it is attached to a perceiver in a state, since Nadia is not surveying a hall but managing one object. A stated emotion became a maintained object, a coat somebody brushes and somebody else refuses to. And two of its strongest facts are negative details, things that do not happen.

The domains can be read off it. Since March dates the death and lets the reader place the scene. The ledger is named once, in a clause about where the coat was worn, as a familiar object of the household; it is planted without emphasis so that when Elena finds it in the register drawer, in The Chapter Break (Chapter 6), the reader recognises it rather than meeting it, and a coat worn at the till is where the father’s pen will turn up. Nadia’s arithmetic of dust, and her refusal to be the one who brushes, show a counting way of managing grief without the word grief. The father exists in the cuff. The world is a household of quiet, unspoken caretaking by someone nobody names. Not the same as counting stages the book’s argument about ritual as a way of not quite facing loss. And three carriers make duration the paragraph’s subject: the pluperfect had brushed, the habitual used to hook and every winter, and the wear itself. The coat, the cuff and the habit are planted so that the ending, in which Nadia takes the coat down and puts it on, gathering the sleeves so they do not drag, is a varied return rather than a new gesture.

Four things remain unsolved. The paragraph is near the density ceiling: almost every clause carries two domains; it is slightly relentless read aloud, and it needs an ordinary sentence beside it. It has no reality-effect detail, nothing simply there, and the remedy is to add an object that means nothing (a bag by the door, a light nobody switches on), not to make the existing details work harder. The ledger clause leaves the present hallway for the father’s habit; it passes the plausibility test only because the coat is the shop’s coat, and a stricter reading would ask whether Nadia, managing one object, thinks of the counter at all. And the register is recognisable: wry, restrained close third with a counting protagonist is a current literary idiom, so the revision passes the genericness test at the level of the object and is marginal at the level of the voice, a problem for The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13) rather than this chapter.
