Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) names plot, character, theme and texture as domains of purpose, with a relational domain also proposed there and adopted by The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3). At the scale of a detail, this chapter adds world as a fifth domain and considers relational effects under character. It then proposes a sixth domain for time. These are revision lenses, not a required inventory of functions.

Two possible misuses are worth naming. One treats the domains as slots to fill and revises a passage until each is represented; the other assigns a domain to a detail after the fact merely to justify keeping it. A difficult-to-explain function may be a reason to examine the passage, though readers can respond differently.

Plot: the detail matters to what happens

A plot detail gives the reader information the action will need. It may plant an object a later scene requires, orient the reader in space, mark a threat or material constraint, or supply a clue. Chekhov’s gun is commonly used as craft advice about conspicuous emphasis creating expectations; it is not a measured rule about how soon a detail must pay off. Research on situation models shows that readers can track spatial relations when a task or context supports doing so. In a scene that depends on movement, placing the relevant spatial facts before they become decisive may help the reader follow it.

In the first chapter of Great Expectations (1861), the convict on the marshes demands food and a file from Pip. The file connects with the leg-iron Pip has seen and leads him to steal from Joe’s forge in chapter 2. In Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart (1958), Okonkwo earlier fires a gun at his wife and misses; at a funeral, his gun explodes and a piece of iron kills a boy, causing Okonkwo’s exile. In both works, an object that belongs to the represented world also becomes important to the plot.

Character: the detail reveals a person

A character detail may reveal the person perceiving, the person perceived, or both. When a perceiving mind selects what to notice, the choice can contribute to characterisation. Two people enter one kitchen: one registers scorched milk and an unpaid bill, the other the light on the tiles. Those different selections invite different inferences. A useful revision question is whether this perceiver, wanting what she wants in her present state, would notice the detail. This chapter calls that the plausibility of attention.

Hilary Mantel’s Wolf Hall (2009) opens on the boy Thomas Cromwell knocked flat on the cobbles by his father, and what he registers, dazed and bleeding, is the boot: its stitching is coming undone, and a hard knot in it has caught his eyebrow and opened another cut. A boy being kicked does not survey a yard. He sees the one object a few inches from his face, and the selection gives both his state and the novel’s habit of attention to how things are made.

Relations to objects can reveal a person: what someone maintains, lets go, has mended or continues to wear. The household case uses one such carrier to show a relation. Who dusts a thing, who may move it and who has stopped touching it may characterise more than one person within a clause.

World: the detail builds the setting’s reality

A world detail can help a place feel inhabited and convey its practices. One method is normalisation, a term this book takes from The Opening Pages (Chapter 9): a rule appears through use rather than explanation. A character who steps round an offering bowl at the threshold without comment may imply a household custom; a narrator can also explain the custom when the story needs it. A specific noun such as awl can carry more material information than tool, provided readers have enough context to understand it.

An unfamiliar cue may need context for a reader to understand its role. N. K. Jemisin’s The Fifth Season (2015), winner of the 2016 Hugo Award for best novel, introduces terms such as orogenes, comms and the Fulcrum in scenes while also providing explanation and a glossary at the back. There is no established two- or three-detail norm for familiar or unfamiliar settings.

Theme: the detail carries meaning

A theme detail may suggest what a book is about on its first appearance or develop meaning through a return or contrast. Eliot’s 1919 essay on Hamlet gives a well-known formulation of the objective correlative: objects, a situation or a chain of events can evoke an emotion. The term predates Eliot, though his formulation is influential. In To the Lighthouse (1927), the boeuf en daube at dinner can be read as contributing to concerns with transience and made things without standing for either as a simple symbol.

Texture: the detail shapes the reading

A texture detail can give a reader a felt condition: mood, pace, bodily sensation or pleasure in the prose. Its effect depends partly on placement; description may slow a moment, build suspense or interrupt an awaited answer. Prose can name smell, temperature and the feel of cloth, while film often suggests these through image, sound and a character’s response. Neither medium has an exclusive claim to nonvisual sensation.

A proposed sixth: the temporal domain

The five-domain model folds time into world. This chapter proposes separating it as the temporal domain: a detail belongs to it when its work is to establish duration, sequence, decay, repurposing, season or interruption, that is, not what the world is but what has happened to it.

Three arguments support the proposed temporal domain. It has useful carriers, including perfect and pluperfect forms (what had once been), participles such as mended and worn through, habitual forms, wear, residue and maintenance. These can also serve plot, character and world; the category draws attention to effects those domains might overlook. Readers track time as a dimension of situation models. Margaret Atwood’s The Handmaid’s Tale (1985) opens with its narrator sleeping in “what had once been the gymnasium”; the phrase marks a change of use. Jon McGregor’s Reservoir 13 (2017) covers thirteen years after the disappearance of Rebecca Shaw. Its first chapter opens with the search, and New Year fireworks recur later as part of the village’s yearly cycle. The calendar can be read as one way the novel represents the community’s changing relation to her absence.

The case against a separate temporal domain is substantial. Another category adds to a writer’s checklist, with no principled stopping point; one could also propose a social or bodily domain. A frayed cuff can suggest wear, household and a person’s habit at once. Distinct carriers do not by themselves prove a distinct domain. This chapter adopts the sixth domain as a prompt for noticing aspect, wear and maintenance while acknowledging that these may also be features of other domains. Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8) also considers temporal orientation.

Must every detail serve several domains?

No. A detail can perform one modest job; another can serve several. Some apparently incidental details may contribute to texture or to the reality effect described by Barthes. No measured proportion is required. Consider coverage at the level of a passage rather than demanding several functions from every sentence. The domains are lenses, not slots: ask which helps explain the passage’s selection and whether another suggests a useful revision.
