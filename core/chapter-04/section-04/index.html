Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) named the domains in which a scene can do work, its domains of purpose: plot, character, theme and texture, with a relational domain proposed there and adopted by The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3). At the scale of a single detail the list changes in two ways. A fifth domain, world, is needed, because a scene can leave its world implicit and a detail is very often the only place a world becomes visible. And the relational domain is audited here under character, since a detail can show what lies between two people only by showing what one of them does with an object: who dusts it, who may touch it, who has stopped. The five are set out below, followed by a proposed sixth.

Two misuses are common enough to name first. One treats the domains as slots to fill and revises a passage until each is represented; the other assigns a domain to a detail after the fact in order to keep it, and if the domain must be argued for, the reader will not feel it.

Plot: the detail matters to what happens

A plot detail gives the reader information the action will need. It plants an object a later scene requires, orients the reader in space, marks a threat or limit before it activates, supplies a clue presented as ordinary, or sets a material constraint (money, distance, a door that sticks). Chekhov’s rule about the gun on the wall is a rule about the economy of attention, and it is usually stated backwards: it does not say that every object must be used, but that a conspicuously placed object creates an expectation. The remedy for an unredeemed plant is usually to demote the detail, not to invent a use for it. Orientation has its own rule, which the research on situation models supports: establish the geography one step before it is needed. Given at the moment of need, it reads as convenience; given afterwards, it reads as a cheat.

In the first chapter of Great Expectations (1861) the convict on the marshes demands two things of Pip, food and a file. The file confirms the leg-iron revealed by the first glimpse of the man, makes Pip a thief within a page, and creates the debt the whole novel repays. In Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart (1958) Okonkwo’s gun, which has already been fired at his wife and missed, explodes at a funeral and kills a boy; it has been in the novel as an ordinary possession of a titled man, and becomes the instrument of his exile without ever having been flagged. A plot detail at its best cannot be distinguished from a world detail until it is needed.

Character: the detail reveals a person

A character detail reveals the person perceiving, the person perceived, or both. The first is the strongest. If description is selection, and the selection is made by a perceiving mind rather than by a disembodied narrator, then every descriptive choice is also characterisation, whether the writer uses it or not. Two people enter one kitchen: one registers the scorched milk and the unpaid bill under the fruit bowl, the other the light on the tiles. Nothing has been said about either, and both have been established. The rule this yields resolves more draft problems than any other in the chapter. Whether a detail is plausible is a question about the perceiver, not the world: would this person, wanting what she wants, in her present state, notice this? The chapter calls this the plausibility of attention.

Hilary Mantel’s Wolf Hall (2009) opens on the boy Thomas Cromwell knocked flat on the cobbles by his father, and what he registers, dazed and bleeding, is the boot: its stitching is coming undone, and a hard knot in it has caught his eyebrow and opened another cut. A boy being kicked does not survey a yard. He sees the one object a few inches from his face, and the selection gives both his state and the novel’s habit of attention to how things are made.

The perceived person is better revealed through relations to objects than through features: what someone maintains, lets go, has mended badly, wears that no longer fits. The least used of these carriers is the one that marks a relation, and it is the one the household case relies on. Who dusts a thing, who may move it, who has stopped touching it: these facts characterise two people at once and cost a clause.

World: the detail builds the setting’s reality

A world detail persuades the reader that a real place exists, with rules, and that the book knows them. Its mechanism is normalisation, a term this book takes from The Opening Pages (Chapter 9): rules are shown working rather than stated, and the instrument is the perceiver’s indifference. A character who steps round the offering bowl at the threshold without comment has established a custom and a household in thirteen words; a narrator who explains that offering bowls are placed at thresholds has established an explanation. Naming is the cheapest world-building available, since a specific noun (awl, not tool) carries social and material information an adjective would have to state, and a proper noun implies a community that agreed on it.

The technique has a limit. A cue summons only what the reader already has in stock, so a world the reader has never seen must pay a higher descriptive tariff. N. K. Jemisin’s fantasy novel The Fifth Season (2015), which won the Hugo Award for best novel, pays it with names first and explanations late or never: the reader meets orogenes, comms and the Fulcrum in use, among people who do not need them explained, and the glossary is placed at the back of the book rather than the front. The reader builds the system from instances, and what she builds is hers. The norm of two or three details per room is a norm for the familiar.

Theme: the detail carries meaning

A theme detail intimates, without stating, what the book is about. Meaning at this level is seldom a property of one detail; it lies in a relation, either between a detail and its return later in the book, or between a detail and the feeling of the scene it sits in. The first is the motif, treated in The Mechanism. The second is ironic contrast: a detail whose plain meaning is cheerful or ordinary, set in a scene that is neither. Eliot’s objective correlative, coined in his essay on Hamlet (1919), states the domain’s ambition: emotion travels by its occasions and not by its names. In To the Lighthouse (1927) the boeuf en daube at the dinner, which took three days to make and will be eaten in one evening, carries the novel’s argument about transience and made things without ever standing for it.

Texture: the detail shapes the reading

A texture detail gives the reader a felt condition: mood, pace, the body in the room, pleasure. It is the domain first drafts lean on by default, because a room can always be described, and so the one revision must question hardest. Description controls reading speed in both directions, and what matters is its placement relative to the reader’s question: description inserted while the reader is waiting for an answer is felt as delay, which is suspense if she believes the answer is coming and obstruction if not. Prose can also supply smell, temperature and the feel of cloth, none of which a screen delivers directly, and a draft that describes only what a camera could photograph has given up the medium’s one advantage over film. Some description exists to be enjoyed, on condition that the book has earned the indulgence.

A proposed sixth: the temporal domain

The five-domain model folds time into world. This chapter proposes separating it as the temporal domain: a detail belongs to it when its work is to establish duration, sequence, decay, repurposing, season or interruption, that is, not what the world is but what has happened to it.

Three arguments support the separation. The domain has its own carriers, and several of them are grammatical: perfect and pluperfect aspect (what had once been), the past participle as adjective (mended, worn through), habitual forms (he would always), wear and residue, an object out of date within the frame, and maintenance or its absence. No other domain is carried by tense and aspect, and “look at your aspect choices” is advice the world domain will never generate. It has its own failures: the undated world, in which nothing has been repaired, outgrown or let lapse, so that the place seems built for the story; and decay as mood, rust and dust applied evenly as atmosphere rather than as a record. And readers track time as a separate dimension of the situation model. Margaret Atwood’s The Handmaid’s Tale (1985) opens with its narrator sleeping in “what had once been the gymnasium”; change the verb to a simple past and the world is unchanged while the history vanishes. Jon McGregor’s Reservoir 13 (2017) works the domain at the scale of a book. A thirteen-year-old girl, Rebecca Shaw, goes missing from a village in the Peak District, and each of the thirteen chapters covers one year, opening with the New Year fireworks and returning through the swallows, the hawthorn, the well dressing and the harvest festival. The search fades and the cycle continues, and the novel’s argument about what a community does with an absence is carried by its calendar.

The case against is not weak. Five categories can be held in mind during revision and six are harder, with no principled stopping point (a social domain, a bodily one). A temporal detail is almost never alone; the frayed cuff is wear, household and a man’s habit at once, which suggests that time may be a feature of details rather than a kind. And distinct carriers do not by themselves prove a distinct domain, since character is also carried by grammar. The chapter adopts the sixth domain for diagnosis, because aspect, wear and maintenance are the carriers a flat passage most often lacks and the cheapest to supply, and concedes the parsimony objection. Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture (Chapter 8), where time is the material, adopts the domain at the scale of a book as the domain of orientation, the details that tell a reader which time a passage is in, and leaves the parsimony objection standing for the single detail.

Must every detail serve several domains?

No. Single-domain details are the majority and must be, since a novel consists largely of details doing one modest job. Some details serve no domain, and a proportion must, for the reason Barthes gives. Coverage is a property of passages, not sentences. And the domains are lenses, not slots: the useful question is which domain is this passage’s principle of selection, and whether another would make a better passage.
