Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Description is the area of craft in which prohibition has done most damage, because nearly every prohibition targets a surface feature (long paragraphs, adjectives, lists, weather) and the great descriptive writing commits all of them. The five patterns below are therefore described by mechanism, and each ends with a published work that commits the fault and succeeds, since a pattern that is sometimes right has to be diagnosed rather than banned. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue adds eight more: the wallpaper detail, the adjective pile-up, the info-dump disguised as description, the mirror self-description, the travelogue, the brand-name shortcut, weather as mood-ring, and the exhaustive body inventory.

The inventory passage

Symptom. A list of accurate, unselected details: the room had a table, two chairs, a window and a rug. Furniture is listed rather than seen.

Mechanism. No principle of selection. The reader looks for the reason these facts were chosen from thousands, finds none, and concludes, correctly, that the writer was not choosing. The flatness comes from the absence of a claim, not from the length or the listing. An unselected list is not neutral either: the reader still reads a principle into it, and the one she finds is that the writer had nothing in mind.

Remedy. Do not edit the list. Choose a principle (what this perceiver avoids looking at, what the rent explains, what nobody has touched since March) and rewrite from it, usually with far fewer items, or with one object instead of a room.

Exception. Balzac opens Père Goriot (1835) with an inventory of the Maison Vauquer that runs for pages, down to the oilcloth and the rent each lodger pays. It works because it has a principle: the novel’s argument is that the lodgers are what their money makes them, and every object is priced. An inventory with a causal principle is a catalogue, and the shuffle test proves it, since it loses its argument when reordered.

The static motif

Symptom. A charged detail repeated in the same words, scene after scene, with nothing about it or around it developing.

Mechanism. No difference to measure. Each return adds nothing to the reader’s model, so she attributes the repetition to the author, and the first appearance is cheapened in retrospect, because the reader now understands that the detail was the writer’s favourite rather than the book’s evidence.

Remedy. Change the object’s state or who stands in relation to it at each return, or cut the returns to two and let the second one matter.

Exception. The opening of Bleak House (1853) puts the word fog at the head of clause after clause with no variation, and the passage is among the best-known openings in English. The repetition is local and dense rather than distributed across a book: it happens within one passage, where it reads as incantation and enacts the undifferentiated condition it describes, a city and a court in which nothing can be made out and nothing moves. Identical repetition across a novel reads as forgetfulness; within a paragraph it can be the technique.

The unfocalised detail

Symptom. In close third or first person, a detail no one present could have registered: what lies behind the character, what the room looked like before she entered, another person’s face while unobserved.

Mechanism. A breach of the narrative’s own access. The reader has been taught a contract, that this book sees what she sees, and the breach is felt as a wobble even by readers who could not name it, because the situation model has been filled from a source it did not expect. The fault is not unrealism but inconsistency.

Remedy. Give the detail to the perceiver, by finding what she would notice that implies the same fact, or move it to a passage where the narration’s access is wider and declared.

Exception. George Eliot’s Middlemarch (1872) has a narrator who moves between minds, comments, and describes what no one present sees, and it is among the achievements of the form. The access was declared from the start rather than breached, so the reader never holds a contract the narration later violates. The fault is never wide access; it is unannounced access.

The symbol announcing itself

Symptom. A detail so plainly meaningful that the reader stops seeing the object: the broken clock, the caged bird, the dead tree in the garden, arriving at the right emotional moment and described at the right emotional length.

Mechanism. Connotation has consumed denotation. An object no longer plausibly present in the room has become an emblem, the reader’s attention moves from the story to the author’s intention, and she begins to distrust the other details too, suspecting each of carrying a message.

Remedy. Reduce the emphasis, not the meaning. Let the object appear first as ordinary world detail, give its announcement to a character rather than to the narration, or give it a plain use in the story.

Exception. The second season of Breaking Bad (AMC, 2009) opens four of its episodes with black-and-white flash-forwards in which the only colour is a scorched pink teddy bear, floating in Walter White’s pool and then bagged as evidence; the four titles, read together, spell “737 Down Over ABQ”, and the season ends with two aircraft colliding over Albuquerque and the bear falling into the pool. Few symbols announce themselves louder. The device works because the announcement is an enigma with a literal answer: the bear is debris before it is meaning, and the season makes the viewer earn the reading. The Great Gatsby does the same in prose with the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, which a character, not the narrator, finally reads aloud as God.

The sense monoculture

Symptom. Everything is seen. Across a manuscript, description is visual with an occasional sound, and smell, temperature, texture and the body’s own report are absent.

Mechanism. A forfeited advantage. Prose is the one narrative medium that can deliver non-visual sensation directly, and a book that describes only what a camera could photograph has taken on the camera’s limits without its speed. Visual description also places the reader at a distance from the scene, since sight is the sense that works across space, whereas smell, touch and temperature put her inside it.

Remedy. Run the sense tally, then find the single non-visual detail that carries most and let it replace a visual one. Smell repays the search, because it is bound to memory and film cannot deliver it.

Exception. Alain Robbe-Grillet’s Jealousy (1957; translated by Richard Howard) is visual by programme: shadows measured on a veranda, rows of banana trees counted, a stain on a wall returned to again and again, and no interior life stated. The narrator never names himself and is never named. The monoculture is the position, a refusal of the interiority the novel had relied on, and the reader infers a jealous watcher from the obsessive precision of what is seen.
