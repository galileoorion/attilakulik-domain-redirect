Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Rules against long paragraphs, adjectives, lists or weather can obscure what a passage is trying to do. The five patterns below are revision prompts rather than prohibitions. Each includes a published work in which a similar feature serves a purpose.

The inventory passage

Symptom. A list of accurate, unselected details: the room had a table, two chairs, a window and a rug. Furniture is listed rather than seen.

Mechanism. Without a discernible principle of selection, a list can seem generic. The issue is not its length or the fact of listing, but whether its contents and order contribute to the passage.

Remedy. Try choosing a principle (what this perceiver avoids looking at, what the rent explains, what nobody has touched since March) and revising the list around it. Fewer items or one object instead of a room may help.

Example. Balzac opens Père Goriot (1835) with an extended description of the Maison Vauquer, including furnishings and the lodgers’ payments. Prices and material conditions help organise the social portrait; this is one way to read the catalogue’s effect. Reordering its details would change that progression.

The static motif

Symptom. A charged detail repeated in the same words, scene after scene, with nothing about it or around it developing.

Mechanism. If each return seems to add little in context, repetition may lose its force. Exact recurrence can also be intentional, so assess its effect before revising it.

Remedy. Change the object’s state or who stands in relation to it at each return, or cut the returns to two and let the second one matter.

Example. The opening of Bleak House (1853) repeats fog across successive clauses while varying its location and associations. The repeated word can read as incantatory. Exact recurrence can also work across a longer text when the context gives it force.

The unfocalised detail

Symptom. In close third or first person, a detail no one present could have registered: what lies behind the character, what the room looked like before she entered, another person’s face while unobserved.

Mechanism. If a passage has established close access to one character’s perception, an unexplained shift in access may feel inconsistent. Check whether the narration has already allowed a wider view.

Remedy. Give the detail to the perceiver, by finding what she would notice that implies the same fact, or move it to a passage where the narration’s access is wider and declared.

Example. George Eliot’s Middlemarch (1871–72) has a narrator who moves between minds and comments on events. Wide access is an established feature of its narration.

The symbol announcing itself

Symptom. A detail so plainly meaningful that the reader stops seeing the object: the broken clock, the caged bird, the dead tree in the garden, arriving at the right emotional moment and described at the right emotional length.

Mechanism. If the narration stresses an object’s symbolic meaning, some readers may attend to the device before the scene. That effect may be useful or distracting, depending on the work.

Remedy. Reduce the emphasis, not the meaning. Let the object appear first as ordinary world detail, give its announcement to a character rather than to the narration, or give it a plain use in the story.

Example. Four episodes of Breaking Bad’s second season (AMC, 2009) open with largely black-and-white flash-forwards featuring a pink teddy bear. The four episode titles spell “737 Down Over ABQ”, and the season later reveals the bear as debris from a collision over Albuquerque. The conspicuous image can work as an enigma with a literal answer. In The Great Gatsby, George Wilson reads the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg as the eyes of God; the novel also gives Nick’s account of the billboard.

The sense monoculture

Symptom. Everything is seen. Across a manuscript, description is visual with an occasional sound, and smell, temperature, texture and the body’s own report are absent.

Mechanism. Prose can name smell, touch, temperature and bodily sensation as well as sight. A strongly visual passage may be effective; the question is whether its sensory range serves the scene. Other narrative media can also convey nonvisual sensation through words, sound, performance and reaction.

Remedy. Tally the senses, then consider whether a nonvisual detail would strengthen the scene. Smell can be useful when it matters to a character’s memory or judgment; film can suggest it through dialogue, sound, images and reactions.

Exception. Alain Robbe-Grillet’s Jealousy (1957; translated by Richard Howard) is visual by programme: shadows measured on a veranda, rows of banana trees counted, a stain on a wall returned to again and again, and no interior life stated. The narrator never names himself and is never named. The monoculture is the position, a refusal of the interiority the novel had relied on, and the reader infers a jealous watcher from the obsessive precision of what is seen.
