Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

3. How It Works

Continue in Reference

The chapter needs only the theory its method uses. Each strand below starts from a question a writer asks in revision, then gives the source that answers it.

Why not simply cut the description?

Ian Watt’s The Rise of the Novel (1957) argues that formal realism helped distinguish the emerging novel from earlier prose fiction by presenting individual experience through particulars of time, place and person. Ordinary names, dated time, named streets and objects that are owned or worn are examples of the circumstantial particularity he discusses. These are central to the realist mode in Watt’s account. For revision, his argument offers a reason to preserve particulars that establish a book’s world. Spareness does not by itself turn a novel into a fable or parable; the effect depends on the work. Particularity can authenticate without being a faithful transcript of an actual room.

How much should I describe?

Erich Auerbach’s Mimesis (1946; translated by Willard R. Trask, 1953) opens by comparing two ancient texts. In Book 19 of the Odyssey the old nurse recognises Odysseus by a scar above his knee, and the poem turns aside for roughly eighty lines to explain it. Genesis 22, the binding of Isaac, gives fewer concrete particulars: it names the land of Moriah and says Abraham saw the place on the third day, but leaves the speakers’ appearance and most thoughts unstated. Auerbach calls the biblical manner “fraught with background”. The contrast offers two possibilities for a writer: extensive foregrounded description and a sparser mode that invites inference. Neither guarantees presence or depth. Auerbach treats both as successful styles rather than prescribing one of them.

Why does an odd angle make a reader see?

Viktor Shklovsky’s essay “Art as Device” (1917; translated by Lee T. Lemon and Marion J. Reis as “Art as Technique”) argues that perception can become habitual. In his account, art can renew perception by making the familiar strange, a process he called ostranenie, usually translated as defamiliarisation. His examples include Tolstoy’s descriptions of flogging and property as perceived by a horse. Describing an operation while withholding its familiar name is one strategy a writer can test; it may slow a passage and has a different effect when estrangement becomes a book’s sustained style.

What about the detail that does nothing?

Roland Barthes’s essay “The Reality Effect” (1968; translated by Richard Howard) challenges a strict demand that every detail serve plot or character. Analysing Flaubert’s “Un cœur simple” (1877), he discusses the barometer in Mme Aubain’s room. In his reading, it has no clear narrative function and can instead signify the category of the real: an apparently superfluous particular makes the text seem to report a world. Other critics may find social or historical associations in the barometer. His reality effect is a critical account of realism’s rhetoric, not a measured finding about what every reader needs to find a room convincing.

Three replies are available. The first treats authentication as a job, though job then risks becoming too broad to guide revision. The second asks about a passage’s selection as well as a sentence’s apparent function. The third treats the demand for an identifiable job as an aesthetic preference rather than a law. This chapter works from the second and keeps the third in view. Its practical question is whether the passage has a principle of selection. Barthes supplies no required proportion of incidental details, and no fixed ratio separates a convincing room from a list.

How many details will a reader keep?

Research on reading describes a situation model, a representation of a text’s state of affairs along dimensions such as protagonist, space, time, cause and intention (Rolf Zwaan and Gabriel Radvansky’s 1998 review). In experiments by Zwaan and colleagues, spatial discontinuities affected reading times when participants had learned a map of the setting, but not when they read without it; temporal, causal, goal-related and protagonist discontinuities affected reading times in both conditions. A craft inference is to establish spatial relations clearly when a scene requires the reader to follow movement. These studies do not count the descriptive details in novels, establish how many a reader will remember or identify a fixed memory threshold. Elaine Scarry’s Dreaming by the Book (1999) offers a theoretical account of how prose, including descriptions of motion, can guide imagination. A writer can test that possibility by describing an object in use. Scarry’s account is not an experimental ranking of descriptive techniques.

The conventions have dates

Descriptive conventions vary by work, form and period. Balzac opens Père Goriot (1835) with an extended description of a boarding house; Hugo devotes sections of Les Misérables (1862) to Waterloo and the Paris sewers. These examples do not establish a period norm or what every contemporary reader wanted. Later works also vary: some introduce a room through a few details, while others use catalogues or set pieces. This chapter has no corpus from which to infer a two- or three-detail count norm, a tenfold decline or the date when a workshop convention began. Photography, film and publishing formats changed, but that alone does not establish a causal decline in descriptive length or that writers were generally paid by length. Naomi Schor’s Reading in Detail (1987) discusses an older devaluation of detail and its later reconsideration. Treat selection as a choice rather than a dated universal rule.
