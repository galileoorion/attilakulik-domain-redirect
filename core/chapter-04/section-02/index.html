Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

2. Definitions

Continue in Reference

Craft discussion uses “description”, “imagery”, “detail”, “symbol” and “setting” almost interchangeably. They name units of different sizes that fail in different ways, so the chapter separates them before using them.

The working definition

A descriptive detail is a concrete, specific unit of sensory or physical information, chosen from an open-ended set of possible alternatives that go unmentioned, and included for what it contributes beyond plausibility or accuracy alone.

Each phrase of the definition does work. Concrete and specific rules out judgements: the room was messy is a verdict that withholds its evidence, whereas three coffee mugs, none finished, ringed the base of the lamp is a detail, countable and particular. The workshop slogan show, don’t tell is more useful as a question about where inference happens. A detail can invite the reader to infer a judgement that an abstract label supplies directly. Chosen from alternatives that go unmentioned is the centre of the definition: a detail’s meaning may depend partly on what was not said. Sensory or physical restricts the subject to the perceptible; a reported thought may be characterisation, and a summary of someone’s finances is exposition, but the state of their shoes is description. Work beyond accuracy is the purposive clause on which the taxonomy rests, and the one the chapter later complicates.

Scale

The unit stretches from a single modifier to a whole chapter, and its possible failures change with its size: modifiers may pile up, a named object may decorate, a detail sentence may go generic, a paragraph may become an unselected list, and a set piece may halt the story for the author’s knowledge rather than anyone’s need.

Gérard Genette, the French narratologist whose terms for narrative time are developed in The Transition (Chapter 5), calls a stretch of description during which story time does not advance a descriptive pause. A pause can be short or long, and description can also occur while events advance. The term draws attention to the relation between description and narrative time, rather than setting a minimum size for a descriptive passage. This chapter takes the detail sentence as its working unit, since that is a scale at which a choice is visible and can be revised.

Description is not imagery

Description and imagery are related but answer different revision questions. Imagery is the sensory and figurative texture of the prose, what the language may evoke in a reader’s senses. Description supplies information about the represented world. A page of metaphor can be rich in imagery while giving little information about a room; an inventory can describe a room without much figurative language, though its objects may still evoke images. Ask both what the reader can now picture or sense and what the passage has established. Nor is description the same as setting, which is a kind of content: a face or a meal can be described as readily as a place.

Adjacent terms

The terms below recur through the chapter. Each names something next to the detail rather than the detail itself.

Term Meaning Relation to the detail
Image A sensory impression produced by language The effect; the detail is the choice that produces it
Symbol An object that stands for an abstraction One possible implication
Motif A recurring detail, image or phrase whose repetition acquires significance, with or without variation A pattern extended through time
Metonymy, synecdoche Naming a thing by something associated with it; using part for whole or whole for part Two related descriptive strategies
Ekphrasis Vivid verbal presentation of a subject; in modern usage often a work of art (Greek, “speaking out”) Can form a set piece; Homer’s shield is an early, influential example
Focalisation Genette’s term for who perceives, as distinct from who speaks Decides whose selection a detail is
Objective correlative T. S. Eliot’s 1919 formulation of objects, a situation or a chain of events that evokes a particular emotion One account of how an external occasion can convey feeling

Symbol, motif and ekphrasis are special cases; treating them as the norm is how description becomes over-managed.

What the chapter leaves to others

This chapter considers exposition delivered as description chiefly when it hinders a passage; how antecedent information can be distributed is Backstory and Flashback’s subject (Chapter 7). The use of a descriptive pause to slow a sequence before a shock is also discussed in The Transition (Chapter 5), which examines narrative speed. The Opening Pages (Chapter 9) considers why readers may give an opening detail unusual weight. Gesture around speech belongs to The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3), and questions about whose perceptions a chapter can report belong to The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13). Prose rhythm matters, but this chapter does not examine it systematically.
