Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

A writer revising a quiet family novel has a scene in which Nadia, a woman in her thirties whose father died in March, goes up to the flat above the family shop where he lived. The draft paragraph reads:

Nadia came up the stairs into the flat above the shop. The hallway was narrow and dim, with a worn carpet and a radiator that ticked. There was a coat rack by the door with several coats on it, and an umbrella stand, and a small table where the post collected. On the wall was a mirror in a wooden frame, and beneath it a pile of free newspapers nobody had thrown away. The place smelled of dust and old cooking. It had been like this for as long as she could remember, and it was strange to think that her father would never come through the door again.

Nothing in it is wrong. Every clause is true of some hallway, the sentences are clean, and a workshop reader would probably call it nicely observed. Asked what she now knows that she did not know before Nadia climbed the stairs, the same reader has an answer of five words: Nadia went into a flat. The paragraph has spent a hundred words on a fact the first sentence delivered, and has then named the feeling that the details should have produced.

The infinite field

The difficulty is not peculiar to this writer. Any room contains an unlimited number of true things: the height of its ceiling, the three colours of paint under the current one, the temperature, the dust on each surface in a different quantity, the repaired leg of a chair and the wrong screws used to repair it. The world can be specified without end and the page cannot, so almost everything must be left out, and what remains is not a sample of the room. It is a claim about the room. This chapter calls the unlimited set of equally true alternatives the infinite field, and the writer’s work is choosing from it.

The draft fails at the choice and not at the observation, which is why it is hard to see from inside. Accuracy is not scarce. An observant writer can produce true, specific, well-made description indefinitely and at low cost, so description is the easiest prose in a novel to write and the place a stalled draft goes to rest. Nothing in an accurate paragraph is incorrect; it is merely inert, and inertness is invisible to the person producing it, because producing accuracy feels like craft.

The chapter’s claim

The chapter’s governing claim follows from the asymmetry between the world and the page:

A detail is not included because it is true. Everything in the room is true. It is included because it does something that the other true things would not.

The claim says that inclusion is a choice and that the choice is not neutral: a reader who cannot see the room reads the chosen facts as the salient ones, so selection argues whether or not the writer meant it to. It says that a detail confirming what the reader already assumed (a kitchen has a stove, a hospital smells of disinfectant) has taken the reader’s attention and returned nothing. It does not say that every detail must be symbolic, since symbolism is one job among many and the one least often right. It does not say that the writer must know the job while drafting; the tests in this chapter are for revision. And it does not say that a detail with no assignable job is a fault. A novel in which every detail is visibly purposeful reads as a machine, and the details that seem to do nothing are part of how a world persuades. That qualification, which comes from Roland Barthes and is set out in How It Works, runs through the whole chapter.

Where the chapter sits

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) asked of a scene what is true at its end that was not true at its start, and named the domains in which the change can register. This chapter asks the same question of a single detail: what does the reader know, feel or anticipate after it that she did not before? The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) gave dialogue its two channels, what is said and what is meant; description has the same pair, what a detail names and what it implies, and the same failure when the second channel is empty.

The chapter also plants. The household novel that runs through this book takes several of its principal objects from the revision of Nadia’s hallway: the father’s coat on its hook, the frayed left cuff, the dust on the shoulders, and, without emphasis, the ledger he kept for eleven years, which a later chapter’s discovery depends on the reader having already met. The ending, in The Ending (Chapter 16), is a varied return of the coat, and the account of the motif in The Mechanism is what makes such a return work.
