Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

A writer revising a quiet family novel has a scene in which Nadia, a woman in her thirties whose father died in March, goes up to the flat above the family shop where he lived. The draft paragraph reads:

Nadia came up the stairs into the flat above the shop. The hallway was narrow and dim, with a worn carpet and a radiator that ticked. There was a coat rack by the door with several coats on it, and an umbrella stand, and a small table where the post collected. On the wall was a mirror in a wooden frame, and beneath it a pile of free newspapers nobody had thrown away. The place smelled of dust and old cooking. It had been like this for as long as she could remember, and it was strange to think that her father would never come through the door again.

The paragraph gives the reader a plausible hallway: its light, objects and smell. It also states Nadia’s feeling about her father’s death. What remains unclear is why these particulars, rather than others, matter to her at this moment. The roughly hundred-word passage spends much of its space on an inventory before naming the feeling it might have allowed the reader to infer.

The infinite field

The difficulty is not peculiar to this writer. A room offers more describable particulars than a page can hold: its ceiling height, temperature, dust on different surfaces and, in some rooms, old paint beneath the current coat or a chair repaired with mismatched screws. Description can continue at ever finer levels of specificity, but a page must leave most possibilities out. This chapter calls that open-ended range of possible choices the infinite field.

The draft’s problem lies in its selection, though the observations are plausible. A writer can continue adding specific details without deciding which ones this scene needs. Such a paragraph may be accurate and still feel inert; that is easy to miss when the individual sentences seem well observed.

The chapter’s claim

The chapter’s governing claim follows from the asymmetry between the world and the page:

A detail is not included only because it could be true. It is included because of what it can do among the alternatives the writer might have chosen.

The claim says that inclusion is a choice: a reader who cannot see the room may treat the chosen facts as salient, whether or not the writer intended them to be. A familiar detail, such as a stove in a kitchen, may add little unless the scene puts it to use. The claim does not require every detail to be symbolic; orientation, pace and characterisation are other possibilities. Nor must the writer know a detail’s purpose while drafting: the tests in this chapter are for revision. A detail without an obvious plot or thematic job is not automatically a fault. Barthes’s account of the reality effect, set out in How It Works, offers one explanation for how apparently incidental details can help a represented world feel inhabited.

Where the chapter sits

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) asked of a scene what is true at its end that was not true at its start, and named the domains in which the change can register. This chapter asks the same question of a single detail: what does the reader know, feel or anticipate after it that she did not before? The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) gave dialogue its two channels, what is said and what is meant; description offers a related distinction between what a detail names and what it implies. A detail can also contribute without carrying a legible second meaning.

The chapter also plants. The household novel that runs through this book takes several of its principal objects from the revision of Nadia’s hallway: the father’s coat on its hook, the frayed left cuff, the dust on the shoulders, and, without emphasis, the ledger he kept for eleven years, which a later chapter’s discovery depends on the reader having already met. The ending, in The Ending (Chapter 16), is a varied return of the coat, and the account of the motif in The Mechanism is what makes such a return work.
