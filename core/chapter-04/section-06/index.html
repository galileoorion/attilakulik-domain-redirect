Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

6. Diagnostic Tests

Continue in Reference

The ten tests are prompts for revision. Run them on passages a reader found slow or that you suspect need work. Record a sentence, not a verdict: “this detail supplies mood and nothing else” is more useful than “fails test 2”. Tests 8 and 9 can be done with a pen to make a passage’s choices visible. A feature flagged by a test may also be an intentional part of a book’s design.

Test 1 — Removal

Cut the detail. What does the reader no longer know, feel or anticipate?

Possible concern. You cannot identify what the detail contributes, although its emphasis suggests a larger role.

Alternative. An apparently incidental detail may help establish a scene or register.

Test 2 — Denotation and connotation

Write one sentence about what it shows and one about what it implies.

Possible concern. The passage needs an implication, but this detail offers little beyond its literal report.

Alternative. A plain report may be exactly what the book’s register calls for.

Test 3 — Genericness

Would it be equally true of another place of this kind in another book?

Failure. A category detail that confirms what the name already supplied.

Exception. A cheap orienting cue, used sparingly.

Test 4 — Substitution

Replace it with two other true things. Do they imply different books?

Failure. The choice was not doing selective work; change the object.

Exception. Interchangeable detail in a deliberately uniform world.

Test 5 — Focalisation

Whose attention selected it, and does the selection reveal them?

Failure. In close narration, a detail written over the character’s shoulder.

Exception. Declared omniscience (Middlemarch).

Test 6 — Plausibility of attention

Would this person, wanting what she wants, in her present state, notice this?

Failure. A survey performed by someone in no condition to make one.

Exception. A perceiver given a reason to wait or look.

Test 7 — Recurrence

Where else does the object appear, and what has changed each time?

Possible concern. An emphasised appearance seems to promise a return that never comes, or repeated appearances have no clear effect.

Alternative. Identical wording can itself be expressive, within a passage or across a book.

Test 8 — Count

How many discrete details, and how many do you want kept?

Possible concern. The passage offers more detail than its intended emphasis can organise.

Exception. A catalogue with an ordering principle.

Test 9 — Modifiers and senses

(a) Count modifiers per noun. (b) Tally details by sense.

Possible concern. (a) Several modifiers obscure the object. (b) The visual emphasis does not serve the scene.

Exception. Nabokov’s cumulative modifiers; Robbe-Grillet’s chosen visuality.

Test 10 — Inventory

Shuffle the details. Then complete: these details, and not others, because ….

Failure. Shuffling loses nothing, and the answer is “because they were there”.

Alternative. An apparently unselected inventory may be deliberate; test its effect in context.

Four refinements sharpen the tests. In test 4, substitute objects as well as attributes: ask why the coat rather than the shoes or the shopping list on the table. In test 6, consider how fear, pain, urgency, grief or desire might direct a character’s attention; there is no fixed number of details such a character can notice. If a passage needs a wider survey, give the perceiver a reason to wait or look. In test 9(b), tally the senses actually used and decide whether another sensory detail would help; no measured share of visual detail defines a typical draft. In test 10, possible completions name a perceiver’s preoccupation, a causal argument, a temporal record or a formal order.

Reading the results together

Patterns across the tests may suggest a revision. If tests 1 and 10 raise the same concern, try a clearer principle of selection. If test 2 raises a concern, consider whether a different object or a plainer report would serve the passage. Tests 5 and 6 can prompt a check on whose attention governs the description. Test 7 can prompt a change in an object’s state or context. If test 8 raises a concern, choose details by their effect in the passage rather than cutting to a fixed number. If an implication seems too emphatic, try reducing its emphasis.

The tests lean towards the purposeful. Applied indiscriminately, they may encourage a book in which every detail visibly justifies itself. Use them to investigate passages that seem problematic, and judge any revision in the context of the whole work.
