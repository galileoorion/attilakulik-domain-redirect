Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

6. Diagnostic Tests

Continue in Reference

The ten tests are for revision, not composition; applied while drafting they produce a writer who cannot finish a first page. Run tests 1 to 3 (Contract, Scene and Momentum) first, since their failures make the others premature: there is no point tuning the voice of an opening that has no scene in it. Test 6 (Promise) needs the manuscript, not the opening alone. Record a sentence, not a verdict, because every test is failed on purpose by a book worth reading, and its job is to make a choice visible so that it can be confirmed as a choice.

Test 1 — Contract

Can you point to the words that open each contract within the first two pages?

Failure. An omission mistaken for a deferral.

Exception. Pride and Prejudice, which defers character behind a surplus of voice.

Test 2 — Scene

Can you complete “In these pages, X is trying to ___, and ___ is in the way”?

Failure. Description or exposition, not a scene.

Exception. Bleak House, whose fog is the argument.

Test 3 — Momentum

Within the first page, does something change or does someone want something, and after how many words?

Failure. A static opening.

Exception. The Stranger, whose flatness is its subject.

Test 4 — Voice

Could the first paragraph move into another novel, of another genre, unnoticed?

Failure. Interchangeable competence.

Exception. The Trial, whose official plainness is the effect.

Test 5 — Zero context

Given only the first two pages, can a reader say who, where and when, what kind of book, and what she wants to know?

Failure. A dependency on paratext.

Exception. Lincoln in the Bardo, whose title supplies the frame its first page withholds.

Test 6 — Promise

Do pages 30 to 40 belong to the same book as the opening, in tone, pace and stakes?

Failure. The borrowed hook.

Exception. Madame Bovary, whose opening narrator vanishes.

Test 7 — Inventory

Of the details placed in the first two pages, which are redeemed later?

Failure. Too many promises, too few kept.

Exception. Tristram Shandy, whose unkept promises are the joke.

Test 8 — Density

Read aloud: where does the breath fail, or a listener ask what is happening?

Failure. A page past the density ceiling.

Exception. The Benjy section of The Sound and the Fury, saturated by design.

Test 9 — Form

On what page does the reader learn the book’s structural rules: viewpoints, timelines, apparatus?

Failure. Structure arriving as a surprise.

Exception. Bleak House, whose second narrator arrives in chapter 3.

Test 10 — Abandonment

At which sentence would an indifferent reader stop?

Failure. The opening stops performing and starts explaining.

Exception. Père Goriot, whose inventory the book needs.

Three refinements sharpen the tests. In test 3 (Momentum), the published openings discussed in this chapter reach a verb of intention or change within about fifty words, an observation from the examples, not a count. In test 4 (Voice), retype your first paragraph among three published openings with the attributions removed and ask a reader to describe each voice in two adjectives; clear and fine are failures. In test 5 (Zero context), the reader must be someone else, since a writer cannot unread her own blurb.

Reading the results together

Failures cluster, and the cluster points to the first move. Tests 2 (Scene) and 3 (Momentum) together mean the opening is description or exposition: find the action underneath and start there. Test 4 (Voice) alone means competent, undistinguished prose, to be rewritten in the character’s diction instead of the author’s. Tests 1 (Contract) and 7 (Inventory) together mean too many promises and too few kept: cut named entities and concentrate the detail. Test 5 (Zero context) alone means supplying the missing frame inside the prose, or keeping the dependency on purpose. Test 8 (Density) alone usually means over-revision, a page polished until it has no air, and the remedy is to restore some sentences that do one thing. Test 9 (Form) alone means declaring the pattern in the first chapter.

Test 6 (Promise) alone means a mis-promise, and it carries a warning. When an opening and its book disagree, writers revise the book toward the opening, because the opening is the more finished object and often the more exciting one. The direction is usually wrong. The opening is two pages and the book four hundred, and the smaller object is the one to change.
