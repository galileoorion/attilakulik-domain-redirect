Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below is described by its mechanism and ends with a published work that commits it and succeeds, because the pattern fails through the mechanism, not the surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the feature is harmless. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue adds five more: the weather report, the overstuffed first page, the unanchored opening, the cast parade and paratext dependency.

The waking-up opening

Symptom. The novel begins with a character waking, lying in bed, getting up, washing and considering the day.

Mechanism. Waking is a passage into consciousness, not an action within it. The character has not been awake long enough to want anything, faces no obstacle and stands in no relation to anyone, so every contract is deferred behind physical events that are the same for everyone. Nothing can be at stake in a body becoming conscious. Drafts drift towards the pattern because it is the chronological start of the day and a comfortable run-up for the writer.

Remedy. Begin at the first moment the character wants something, which is often three or four paragraphs later. The deleted material is usually the writer’s warm-up, not the reader’s need.

The exception. Andy Weir’s Project Hail Mary (2021), a commercial science-fiction novel, opens with its narrator half-conscious while a voice asks What’s two plus two?; the question irritates him, and he drifts back to sleep. He wakes without his name or any idea where he is, beside two crew members long dead, and does not recover his name until the third chapter. The pattern succeeds because the waking is the disruption: his want, to know who and where he is, forms as he wakes, the obstacle is his own memory, and the question he shares with the reader drives the book. Wright’s Native Son succeeds by another route, since its alarm clock is an intrusion and what follows is not washing but a family dressing in one room, and then a rat. Where waking is the event, the pattern becomes a strong opening.

The info-dump opening

Symptom. History, worldbuilding or backstory arrives before any character or action: the political situation, the magic system, the family’s four generations.

Mechanism. Two failures, usually together. In Sternberg’s terms, delivering the antecedents early spends an asset that would otherwise have produced curiosity. And information is not memorable without a container: a reader given a nation’s history before meeting anyone who lives there has nowhere to store it and retains little, so the dump fails even at its own purpose. The writer’s fear that the reader will be lost is real, but the reader does not need to know the world early; she needs to believe that the author knows it, which confident specificity achieves.

Remedy. Turn exposition into implication. Ask of each expository sentence which character would say it, to whom and why; if no one would, the reader does not need it yet.

The exception. Dune opens with an epigraph from an invented history and then a scene thick with unexplained terms, Bene Gesserit, gom jabbar, Arrakis, almost none of them defined. It succeeds because the vocabulary arrives inside a tense scene between two people, an old woman testing a boy, and because the unexplained terms work as withheld information, producing curiosity, instead of as delivered information. An unexplained proper noun is an asset; an explained one is an expense.

The borrowed hook

Symptom. A gripping opening, a chase, a murder, a scream, that turns out to be a dream, an unconnected flash-forward, a scene from a genre the book does not belong to, or an event the next three hundred pages never resume.

Mechanism. The opening writes a cheque the book will not honour. Because of the primacy effect the reader forms her model of the novel from these pages, and everything after is read as a deviation from the promised book. Readers forgive a book that becomes more than it promised and do not forgive one that becomes less. The pressure behind the pattern is the advice to hook: the fastest hook available is an event more exciting than the book’s own material, and the mismatch is invisible to the writer, who knows what is coming.

Remedy. Run test 6 (Promise). If the opening and pages 30 to 40 read as different books, change the opening.

The exception. Daphne du Maurier’s Rebecca (1938) opens with a dream, Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again, the device the pattern most distrusts, since a dream presented as waking reality voids the reader’s investment. It succeeds because the dream is declared as a dream in its first clause and belongs to the book: the ruined house it visits is the book’s loss seen from afterwards, told in the voice that will tell the rest. The same distinction governs glimpses ahead, as the firing squad that opens One Hundred Years of Solitude shows: a flash-forward that belongs to the book is a structure, and one that belongs to a more exciting book is a fraud.

The withholding tease

Symptom. The narration conspicuously declines to name what the viewpoint character knows perfectly well: the thing she had done that summer, what was in the box, a protagonist called only he for six pages.

Mechanism. The voice contract is broken to serve the stakes contract. Close narration promises access to a mind, and a mind that circles a fact without naming it is either being coy on the author’s behalf, which shows the author’s hand, or repressing, which is a different and harder effect. Readers sense the difference, and they meet coyness with irritation, not curiosity. Withholding looks like suspense and is easy; making a question the reader wants answered is harder.

Remedy. Distinguish planting from withholding. Give the reader the fact and withhold its meaning, which is nearly always the stronger structure.

The exception. Beloved withholds on a large scale and succeeds, because what it withholds is unspeakable within the minds it represents, the repression is the subject and is maintained consistently, and what replaces the missing information is concentrated, disorienting specificity, not evasion. The test is whether the gap is in the character or in the author.

Throat-clearing

Symptom. The novel begins on page four. The first three pages are context, scene-setting, biography, or a character travelling somewhere.

Mechanism. The process of composition leaks into the finished text. Writers need a run-up to find the voice, and the run-up is drafting apparatus, not narrative. It is often well written, which makes it hard to see, and it feels necessary to the writer because for the writer it was.

Remedy. Delete the first three paragraphs and read what remains. If the opening improves or is unchanged, they were throat-clearing; repeat until a deletion causes a loss.

The exception. Thomas Mann’s The Magic Mountain (1924) begins with a young man’s journey up to a sanatorium in the Alps for a visit meant to last three weeks, and the slowness of the approach enacts the book’s subject, the elasticity of time for those who stay. The distinction is whether an approach does thematic work or merely postpones.
