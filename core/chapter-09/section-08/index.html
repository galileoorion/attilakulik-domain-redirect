Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a short screen reading and the household revision case follow. Each reading names the contracts the opening opens, the gambit it uses and what it leaves for later.

Pride and Prejudice: the contracts paid unequally

Austen’s first sentence, It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife (1813), asserts a law that the reader is invited to disbelieve while accepting it as the law this society lives by, and that double stance is the novel’s mode. On a mechanical audit the sentence fails three of the five contracts: it supplies no character and no want, no place and no time, and nothing is at stake in it. It survives because the voice contract is discharged with a large surplus, the irony audible in universally and in the inversion of who is in want of whom, and because a reader of 1813 recognised the marriage plot from the content alone, so that the reader’s horizon paid the genre contract. The second paragraph narrows the law to a neighbourhood with daughters, and the third arrives at a scene: “My dear Mr. Bennet,” said his lady to him one day, “have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?” The character and stakes contracts then open in a rush, with a wife who wants something, a husband who resists by teasing, and a question about the new tenant that will occupy the book.

The lesson is that contracts can be paid in unequal instalments, provided they are not all defaulted together. The aphorism defers character, which is the gambit’s usual cost, and buys authority, tone and theme at once. It sounds arch when the voice cannot sustain it, and a draft that copies the gambit without the voice has made an omission, not a choice, which is what test 1 (Contract) is for.

Everything I Never Told You: the fact given, the meaning withheld

Celeste Ng’s first novel (2014) opens: Lydia is dead. But they don’t know this yet. The first sentence gives the book’s gravest fact, the death of a sixteen-year-old, in three words. The second declares the narration’s relation to the family: it knows what they do not, and it says so. What follows is a morning, 3 May 1977, in a small town in Ohio, on which Lydia’s mother finds her bed not slept in; the family calls the police and the friends Lydia was supposed to have, and the next morning her body is recovered from the lake.

In the terms of “The Taxonomy” the opening is a plant, not a tease. It gives the reader the fact and withholds its meaning, the remedy the withholding tease lacks, and it converts what might have been suspense, whether the girl will be found, into curiosity, why she died and what this family did not tell one another. In Sternberg’s terms the backstory has become the engine. The opening is also the borrowed hook’s answer: the most dramatic event in the book is on its first page, and the next three hundred pages do resume it, because the death is the book’s own material, delivered in the voice that will tell the rest. The dramatic irony of the second sentence then does the work of the hairline crack, since an ordinary morning and an unslept bed read as dread to a reader who knows what the family does not. And the formal contract opens in the same breath: a narration that can say they don’t know this yet has declared a vantage above any one character, so its later movement among the family’s minds reads as its nature.

Lincoln in the Bardo: the form declared before the place

George Saunders’s novel (2017) begins with a stranger. Its first words, On our wedding day I was forty-six, she was eighteen, are spoken by Hans Vollman, a printer telling of his young wife and of the beam that fell on him before their marriage was consummated, and the passage is attributed to him by name, set off from the text as a citation would be. Vollman calls the box he lies in a sick-box and expects to recover. The reader infers, before he does, that he is dead, and the withheld referent is bounded: she knows which question she has.

The opening is a voice declaration, and its larger work is the formal contract. A novel that will be told by a crowd of the dead, each passage attributed, and that will intercut chapters assembled from quotations about Lincoln and the death of his son Willie, has shown its unit of division and its apparatus on its first page, so that the later chapters of citation read as the book’s nature instead of the author’s intervention. The cost is borne by the paratext. A reader with only the first page meets a man describing his marriage and has no reason to expect a president; the title supplies the frame the prose withholds, and the opening fails the zero-context test. The dependency is chosen, which is all the test asks, and the case shows how the tests are to be read: a failure that marks a decision is not a fault.

A screen reading: Inglourious Basterds and The Sopranos

Quentin Tarantino’s Inglourious Basterds (2009) begins with a title card, Chapter One: Once upon a time… in Nazi-occupied France, which opens the formal contract, chapters and a fairy-tale frame, before the first shot. The scene is the hybrid resolution at length: in 1941 a farmer chops at a stump while his daughter hangs washing, and a car approaches on the road. The action is small, in motion and comprehensible, and the car is the crack. Colonel Landa declines wine, asks for milk and praises it, and later asks to continue in English; why that matters is withheld until the camera finds the Jewish family hidden beneath the floor, who do not speak it. From then on the stakes contract is fully open, and every courtesy in the conversation is a threat. Only the daughter of the hidden family, Shosanna, escapes, and Landa lets her run.

David Chase’s pilot for The Sopranos (1999) opens in a psychiatrist’s waiting room, Tony Soprano framed against a statue of a nude woman before Dr Melfi calls him in. The scene declares the series’ terms: a crime drama whose mode of attention is a gangster’s account of himself, given to a therapist, with his recent days shown in flashback as he describes them. The genre contract is adjusted in the first minute, and the immediate want, Tony’s wish not to be there at all, is legible before anyone has been threatened.

The household revision case: the first page after the prologue

The household novel opens with a prologue focalised through the father on his last ordinary day, in which he counts the till twice, waves off a boy at the glass and pockets the good pen (The Prologue, Chapter 10). The opening pages follow it, and they belong to Nadia, four months after March. The reader arrives knowing the shop and something of the father, and nothing of his children.

Version A, the draft.

Nadia woke early, as she always did, and lay for a while listening to the traffic on the main road. It was going to be another hot day. She had lived in the flat for over a year now, ever since she had moved out of the place above the shop, which had never had proper heating and which she had shared with her brother Dov for most of her twenties. Their father had died in March. Since then things had been difficult between them. She got up, made coffee, and thought about whether she should call him.

Diagnosis. The scene sentence cannot be completed, because she is not trying to do anything (test 2: Scene). The first sixty words are waking and listening (test 3: Momentum), and the prose could belong to any of a thousand books (test 4: Voice). Every sentence carries one function, the opposite of the density problem (test 8: Density). On the contract test, character is opened partly and world thinly, voice and genre not at all, and the stakes are stated instead of enacted (test 1: Contract). It is a waking-up opening with throat-clearing in front of it, and its emotional situation is summarised, things had been difficult, instead of performed. Yet it holds every necessary fact. Its failure is arrangement and selection, not information.

Version B, the revision.

Nadia had got good at the maths of it: four months since March, seventeen since she’d left the flat over the shop for one with actual heat, and a week to the day since she’d stopped answering when Dov called to ask if she’d been by to check on the coat. She hadn’t. She told herself this was because grief had a schedule and hers ran on different maths from her brother’s, not because she couldn’t stand the smell of dust settling on wool that wasn’t going to be worn again. When her phone buzzed with his name a fourth time that morning, she did what she always did lately, which was let it ring itself out and go back to counting the register receipts nobody but her seemed to think still needed counting.

The audit of Version B is tabular, one row per contract.

Contract How Version B opens it
Voice Wry, self-auditing close third; she told herself lets the narration see past her rationalisations without leaving her head
Genre A quiet, interior register: a literary family novel, and the pace the book will keep
Character A small, resisted want: do not answer, do not go, keep counting; grief shown as avoidance
World Contemporary, domestic, straitened: heating as an achievement, a shop, a till, an unworn coat
Stakes Four unanswered calls, a coat nobody will wear, receipts nobody else thinks matter: three planted questions
Form Close third, past tense, linear, and so far one viewpoint

Every contract is opened inside one paragraph, and the rows share their material: the receipts are character, world and stakes at once. The revision made five moves. It deleted the throat-clearing, the waking, the traffic and the weather. It turned biography into inventory, so that the same facts, the old flat and the months since the death, arrive as Nadia’s own counting and the exposition characterises her. It found the hairline crack, the fourth call this morning, which answers why today? without an event. It replaced a summary of feeling with an object, a coat, dust, wool and a smell. And it opened the narration’s small gap over its character, she told herself … not because, which is the reliability protocol for the whole book. The result is the hybrid resolution: a small, comprehensible action, declining a call, with orientation delivered by voice and detail in the same breath, while the book’s engine arrives later.

What the revision has not solved. The first sentence carries three measurements of time and four facts, close to the density ceiling, and a reader may read it twice; whether that is richness or strain is a fair editorial disagreement. The register is distinctive against Version A but recognisable against its own shelf, so test 4 (Voice) is passed only locally. Four months since March never says what happened in March: Nadia knows, and the narration does not name it, which the withholding tease’s test allows only because the gap is in her, who counts instead of naming, and because the prologue has just shown the father’s last ordinary day; read without the prologue, the month is an unexplained noun. Test 7 (Inventory) finds all but one detail redeemed. The coat is paid at the ending (The Ending, Chapter 16), the counting returns in the sleeves she gathers there, Dov’s calls lead into his thread, and wool that wasn’t going to be worn again is Nadia’s belief, which the last chapter reverses when she puts the coat on, so the opening plants the ending without her knowing it; only the flat with heating stays idle, and it is small. The largest gap is in the formal contract. The page declares one viewpoint, and the book alternates between Nadia and Elena; by test 9 (Form), Elena’s first scene, the doorway exchange with Dov, should follow at the first chapter break, before the reader’s attachment to Nadia hardens into an assumption that the book is hers alone.
