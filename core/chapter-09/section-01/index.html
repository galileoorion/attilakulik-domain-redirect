Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

1. The Problem

Continue in Reference

The drawer on page one

A writer revising the household novel this book follows has heard that agents and editors read submissions under pressure, and that a first page must grip. She looks through her draft for its most gripping scene and finds it about a quarter of the way in: Elena, back in the shop with her new key, forcing the register drawer that sticks on the K, smelling fresh ink where nobody should have written, and finding her father’s ledger, whose careful hand stops at an entry of 9 March while a looser hand continues. She moves the scene to the front. The new first page is tense, specific and well made, and it does not work. (This version is a hypothetical draft, used to show the problem; it is not the novel’s opening.)

A quarter of the way into the book the scene pays because of what the reader brings to it. She has watched the father pocket the good pen on his last ordinary day, seen his coat on its hook in the flat above the shop, and heard Dov refuse to say why the locks were changed. The smell of ink is wrong for a till nobody should have used since March, and she feels the wrongness. On page one the same sentences describe a woman she has never met, in a shop she cannot picture, smelling ink that means nothing to her and finding a book whose handwriting she has no reason to care about. Every detail is a promise she cannot yet weigh. The scene has not lost its craft. It has lost its funding, and the opening has spent the book’s best discovery on a reader who could not yet value it.

There is a second, slower cost. A page that opens on a forced drawer and a secret record tells the reader what kind of book she is in: a mystery, whose question is what happened? The household novel’s question is different, closer to what do these three do with what they can reconstruct?, and its pace is set by unanswered telephone calls, register receipts and dust on a coat. A reader recruited by the drawer will reach page thirty, find herself in the quieter book, and feel that the opening was written for another one. The first cost is paid on page one and the second at page thirty, and the second is the larger, because by then the reader has invested and feels misled.

Underneath both costs lies a comparison between page 3 and page 203 of any novel. Page 203 arrives funded: by then the reader knows whose story this is, what the narrator sounds like, what the world permits and what is at risk, and a scene can spend eight hundred words on someone sitting alone with a memory and keep her, because two hundred pages of satisfaction underwrite it. Page 3 has no account to draw on. Whatever it asks of the reader it must earn in the asking, and if it fails she may never reach the page that would have justified it. This difference in kind between the opening and every later stretch of a book is the threshold problem, and it is why the opening pages need a chapter of their own instead of a paragraph in the chapter on the scene.

The claim

An opening is not a hook but a structure that carries obligations. The first pages of the main narrative must work as a scene in which someone wants something and meets resistance, and the same words must open, for a reader who has invested nothing, the obligations of voice, genre, character, world, stakes and form that the rest of the book will keep. Because all of them fall due at once and in little space, the work is done by selecting details that carry several obligations together, not by adding a sentence for each.

The claim has a consequence for the writer who moved the drawer. Her fault was not that the page was too dramatic but that it opened obligations the book would not keep and left unopened the ones it would. The opening the household novel uses, analysed in the revision case in Worked Analyses, is quieter, and it is built on an object the drawer scene would have made redundant: a coat that nobody in the family wants to touch.
