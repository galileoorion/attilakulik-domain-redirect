Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

5. The Mechanism

Continue in Reference

Multiplicity as selection

Multiplicity is the practice of making one unit of text discharge several functions at once, and in the opening it is arithmetic, not ornament. A first page of about 250 words must open six contracts, sustain a scene and give a stranger a reason to turn the page, and it cannot give each job its own sentence. Lines that do one thing, a sentence only of tone or only of fact, are affordable at page 150 and nearly unaffordable at page one. The discipline is not compression but selection: the writer is not looking for a way to say five things in one sentence but for the detail that already carries five things because it is the right one. Compression produces density; selection produces details that keep yielding. Woolf’s first sentence shows selection succeeding.

Words What they discharge
Mrs Dalloway Character (who); genre (a social register); world (a married woman known by her husband’s name)
said she would Voice (reported, at one remove); form (the book will arrive slightly after its own events)
buy the flowers Character (a want); stakes (a day with a plan in it, the party)
herself World (there are servants); character (insistence); stakes (something she must do alone)

Nine words touch every contract, and no word does a job another word was already doing. Yet the sentence does not feel efficient. It feels casual, and that is the mark of selection: the reader experiences ease while the text carries density.

Where the multiplying work lives

Revisers tend to look for multiplicity in metaphor, on the assumption that figures are where meanings stack. In practice the work is done in four plainer places. The first is the choice of noun: gymnasium, not building, carries a history that an adjective would have to state. The second is tense, mood and aspect: had once been, must have been and was to remember imply a history without narrating one, which makes tense the cheapest worldbuilding in the language. The third is what the viewpoint character declines to explain, since the unremarked thirteenth chime is a positive signal about what she considers normal. The fourth is which three things, of the thousands available, this person notices, which is characterisation, world and voice at once. Metaphor can multiply, but it is the riskiest instrument in an opening, because a figure that misses draws attention to the author, and one tied to a technology can age: the sky in William Gibson’s Neuromancer (1984), the color of television, tuned to a dead channel, meant grey static to its first readers and may mean flat blue to later ones.

The density ceiling

Multiplicity has an upper bound, the density ceiling, and a page past it shows three symptoms: no sentence at rest, so that the prose has effort and no rhythm; clauses stacked faster than the main clause can carry them; and a primary action the reader can no longer name, because she can say only what is being established. The remedy is counter-intuitive: an opening needs some sentences that do only one thing. Emphasis depends on contrast, so a page on which every detail is significant contains no significant detail. Dickens’s fog paragraph is long and low in density per sentence, repeating one word until the repetition becomes an argument; it works by hammering one function, not by stacking five. High multiplicity is a technique, and the virtue it serves is proportion.

Where to begin: the attack

Every narrative decides where in the story’s chronology the telling begins, and every such decision trades momentum against comprehension. Beginning in the middle buys momentum, an immediate question and the impression of a book already running, and risks action without context that reads as noise. Beginning with orientation buys clarity and a frame against which later disruption will register, and risks a first page on which nothing is yet at stake. The failure of the plunge is the counter-intuitive one. Action is not interesting in itself: tension depends on caring about an outcome, which depends on knowing what is at stake for whom, and a chase between two strangers whose names the reader has just learned is fast without being tense.

Most working openings resolve the trade-off in the same way, which this chapter calls the hybrid resolution: begin in the middle of a small, comprehensible action, not the book’s central conflict, which delivers voice, character and world together, and let the real disruption arrive once the reader has enough footing to feel it. The action must already be in motion, so that the reader arrives after it started; instantly comprehensible, needing no context; and characterising, so that the choice of action and the manner of it reveal the person. Clarissa Dalloway buys flowers; in Albert Camus’s The Stranger (1942) Meursault receives a telegram and asks his employer for leave; Ishiguro’s butler considers a motoring trip. The engine of each book arrives later, into a reader able to feel it.

The hairline crack

Whatever the attack, the opening must answer why today?, and the answer is rarely a catastrophe. It is the hairline crack, the smallest deviation from routine that signals that this day is not like the others: the telegram in The Stranger; the alarm clock and then the rat in the one-room flat of Richard Wright’s Native Son (1940); the breakfast that does not arrive on the morning of Josef K.’s arrest. The crack must be noticeable as a deviation, which requires the routine to be sketched clearly enough for the deviation to register, and that is why an opening that is all plunge can fail: without a baseline nothing reads as a departure from it.

Gambits and substitution

The choice between plunge and orientation describes only the relation in time. The Reference chapter sets out twelve opening gambits, recognisable strategies with characteristic costs: the small action (Mrs Dalloway), the aphorism (Pride and Prejudice), the voice declaration (Moby-Dick), the panorama (Bleak House), the frame narrator (Wuthering Heights), the glimpse of a later moment (One Hundred Years of Solitude), the document (Dracula), the arrival of a stranger (The Magic Mountain), the rupture of routine (Native Son), the catalogue (Père Goriot), the telling that refuses to begin (Tristram Shandy) and the withheld referent (Beloved). Their use is not classification but substitution testing. A draft that is not working is often the right material in the wrong gambit, and writing the same first page as a rupture, a voice declaration and a small action teaches more than three rounds of line-editing the original.
