Chapter 10 · The Prologue

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The Purposes of a Scene (Chapter 1) sets out the domains of purpose, the registers in which a unit of narrative can change something: plot, character, theme and texture, with a proposed fifth, the relational domain, which The Dialogue Exchange (Chapter 3) adopts. A prologue adapts that list. Plot, character and theme keep their meanings. World, as The Descriptive Detail (Chapter 4) uses it, replaces texture, since register and scale are what a separated opening most often offers. The relational domain seldom applies, because a prologue usually comes before the relations the book will follow, and where it does apply it is audited under character. The fifth domain is the prologue’s own: the reader contract, the deal struck with the reader before the story proper begins.

Each domain is stated here as what distance makes available, not as a job any opening might do. That is what turns the higher bar into an audit: a writer who can name the domains her prologue serves can usually name what the separation buys.

Plot: what only distance permits

A prologue serves plot when it delivers events the main narrative’s own sequence cannot reach, because they happened too early, too far away, or to someone the protagonist never meets. Four carriers do this work, and they solve different problems. The framed ending shows an outcome first and trades suspense for curiosity. The unwitnessable incident is an event the book needs and no character in the main narrative could have seen. The running clock is a threat already moving before Chapter One. Compressed antecedent is history at a scale no scene can hold, generations or centuries.

The unwitnessable incident is the plot domain’s least contestable justification. If an event matters and nobody in the main narrative witnessed it, the writer can have it reported later at second hand, invent a witness, produce a document, or give it a compartment before the story starts, and the fourth option is what a prologue is for. The prologue of Dan Brown’s The Da Vinci Code (2003) is a clean case: the curator Jacques Saunière, shot in the Louvre’s Grand Gallery by an attacker who wants a secret he guards, spends his last minutes leaving a coded message, among the paintings and on his own body, for whoever finds him. No one in the main narrative sees those minutes, and the book’s first puzzle is what he did with them. Compressed antecedent is the carrier most likely to fail, because it is nearest to pure exposition; generational history that a grandmother could tell in the novel’s twelfth chapter does not need a prologue, only that chapter.

Under-delivery is the vague portent, a figure watching and something was coming, which delivers nothing the main narrative could not deliver better. Over-delivery is a prologue holding the book’s most dramatic event, after which everything is descent.

Character: the absent vantage

A prologue serves character when it occupies a consciousness the main narrative cannot enter, because that person is dead, absent, unknown to the protagonist or barred from viewpoint. Its carriers are early access to a mind the reader will not revisit, dramatic irony (knowledge given to the reader and withheld from the cast), a formative scene the protagonist never learns of, and the retrospective self, a narrator placed after the events, as in the labelled Prologue of Ralph Ellison’s Invisible Man (1952), spoken from a cellar lit by stolen electricity after everything the novel narrates.

The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13) shows that moving a reader between minds costs something, and a prologue spends that cost in advance, on a person the reader may never occupy again. A reader who attaches in the prologue and is moved to a different protagonist can feel Chapter One as a demotion. There are two remedies. Make the prologue’s viewpoint plainly not the protagonist’s, by a voice older, remoter or more formal than the main narrative’s. Or make the loss the point: the household novel’s father is a person the reader is meant to lose, because the book is about a family losing him. The second is the stronger move, since a prologue whose viewpoint is the one the book is about not having justifies its separation without further argument. Under-delivery is the viewpoint that is a function, a nameless watcher who exists to see a battle; over-delivery is a prologue mind so vivid that the real protagonist cannot compete, which may mean the book has chosen the wrong protagonist.

World: the register shift

A prologue serves world when it speaks in a mode the main narrative cannot sustain, mythic, choric, documentary or panoramic, to set scale, rules or texture before the book narrows to one person’s view. The distinction that matters is between register and information. A register shift changes how the book speaks; an info-dump is a quantity of what it says. The substitution test separates them: rewrite the prologue’s content in Chapter One’s voice. If it survives with nothing lost but length, it was information and belongs inside. If it becomes flat or impossible, because a close third person cannot say for three hundred years the valley was empty, the register was doing the work. J. R. R. Tolkien’s “Concerning Hobbits”, the prologue to The Lord of the Rings (1954), is open exposition, hobbit customs and pipe-weed and a note on sources, and it survives the test because its antiquarian voice is itself a character whose premise the book sustains. Under-delivery is heightened language with nothing behind it; over-delivery is a register the book never returns to, so that the novel reads as a falling-off from its own first page.

Theme: the promise

A prologue serves theme when it states the book’s central image or question in distilled form before the narrative complicates it. The domain is the cheapest to serve well, a single figure costing two hundred words and paying across four hundred pages, and the most expensive to serve badly, since a thesis stated in advance turns the novel into its illustration. Two disciplines follow. State the question, not the answer: a prologue that says the book is about how families keep secrets has told the reader what to conclude, and one that shows a man deciding not to say something has posed a question. And choose an image you are willing to repeat, because the prologue’s image is the most heavily marked object in the book. Middlemarch’s Prelude does both in three paragraphs: Saint Theresa as a child setting out for martyrdom, then the question of what becomes of later Theresas who find no form for their ardour, a figure the Finale returns to.

The reader contract: tone, patience and protocol

The reader contract is the domain unique to the prologue’s position, and the one most often served alone, which is the shape readers and editors have learned to distrust. Its carriers are a declaration of tone, patience purchased, felt stakes (a dread the characters do not share) and a reading protocol (instructions about the status of what follows). Patience purchased is the most defensible and least discussed. A domestic novel whose engine takes sixty pages to start has a real problem, and one legitimate solution is to show the reader, before Chapter One, that something is coming, so that Chapter One can afford to be quiet. The household prologue works mainly in the felt-stakes mode. The opening Chorus of Henry V (1599) is pure protocol: it narrates nothing and tells the audience what imaginative work the play requires of them, which no first scene could do without breaking its fiction.

A proposed sixth: the authority domain

Some prologues resist all five domains. Dracula (1897) opens with an unsigned note saying how a set of papers was arranged and that they are contemporary records. Frankenstein opens with letters from a man who will relay a dying stranger’s story. Yann Martel’s Life of Pi (2001) opens with an Author’s Note in which a novelist, a fictional version of Martel, is told of Pi Patel in a coffee house in Pondicherry and promises to set the story down in Pi’s own voice. None of these sets much tone or advances a plot. What they do is make a claim about how the book knows what it tells.

The chapter proposes the authority domain: a prologue serves it when it establishes on whose authority the book’s account rests, by supplying a provenance, a transmitting intermediary, a documentary basis, or an account of the limits of what can be known. Two arguments support separating it. It has its own failure, a frame narrator installed and then ignored while the book narrates scenes that narrator could not know, which readers feel as a slippage no other domain names. And it requires exteriority more plainly than any other domain, since a close third-person chapter cannot pause to explain where its own account came from without becoming a different book.

The objections are serious. The domain may be world (a documentary register) and reader contract (a reading protocol) seen together; it belongs largely to a tradition running from Defoe’s editors to the Gothic and its revivals, so most contemporary novels make no such claim; and Genette’s typology of fictive and authentic prefaces already describes the apparatus more finely. The chapter adopts it for diagnosis on the ground that its failure is real and otherwise unnamed, and concedes that it is bound to certain forms in a way the other five are not. The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), whose epistemological domain concerns who in a book knows what, rules that it is not a domain of the viewpoint grant and leaves it here, as a domain of the separated unit: the authority domain claims that a vantage suffices, and the epistemological domain shows that none does. Life of Pi is the test case among the worked analyses.

How many domains?

Not all six, and the model is misused when read that way. Two or three is the normal shape of a prologue that survives revision. Some domains exclude one another in practice: installed dramatic irony forecloses a book whose pleasure is discovery shared with its protagonist. And the domains carry unequal weight for the higher bar. The unwitnessable incident and the authority domain clear it almost automatically, because their work is impossible from inside; the reader contract clears it least easily, because tone can nearly always be set in Chapter One. A prologue serving one strong domain is better placed than one serving three weak ones, and the audit’s output is a decision, keep, absorb or relocate, not a score.
