Chapter 10 · The Prologue

The Prologue

Continue in Reference

What a separated opening must prove, and how to tell whether it has

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 10. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

In brief. A prologue is a unit of narrative set apart from the main story in time, place, viewpoint or register, placed before the main story, and labelled as apart. Like any scene, it must do work the reader would miss if it were gone; unlike any scene, it must also show why that work could not be done from inside Chapter One. The chapter’s tool is that double test and its consequence: a prologue that fails only the second part is not cut but absorbed, its material folded into the main narrative where it belongs. A prologue that is kept opens a distance the book must close, and the chapter argues for closing it in small instalments rather than in one late reveal. Use the chapter on a prologue that readers call unnecessary, on one that promises a different book from the one that follows, or on a quiet book that seems to need something in front of it.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Say in one sentence why a prologue’s material must sit outside the main narrative, and distinguish a sentence that names an impossibility from one that names a preference.
  2. Decide whether to keep a prologue separate, absorb it into Chapter One, or relocate it in the main timeline.
  3. Name the axis of distance that carries a prologue, and choose how and where the book closes it.
  4. Audit the specifics a prologue plants and schedule their recurrence.
  5. Run nine tests on a draft prologue and turn their failures into a revision order.

Contents

The chapter begins by saying what a prologue is and why it costs more than an ordinary opening, then explains what it can do that the main narrative cannot and how the distance it opens is closed, and ends with material for use at the desk.

    A prologue cut, restored and relabelled, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The prologue, its length, and the units mistaken for it
  3. How It Works
    The diegetic threshold, suspense and curiosity, primacy, the dormant frame, and the age of the suspicion
  4. The Taxonomy
    Five domains stated as what distance makes possible, and a proposed sixth: the authority domain
  5. The Mechanism
    Distance and its closing, fragmentary closure, prologue types, and multiplicity as evidence
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Nine diagnostic questions and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a published work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    Frankenstein, The Book Thief, Life of Pi and Saving Private Ryan, and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    Whose test the prologue is taking

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests and the absorption exercise; the coda, which separates the craft standard from the market’s suspicion, is best read early.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds the complete chapter, The Prologue: the history of the form from the Greek prologos to the genre prologue, Genette’s three variables and Said on beginnings, fourteen prologue types with substitution testing, twelve failure patterns, eight close readings (among them Wuthering Heights, “The Custom-House”, Don Quixote, Dracula and the “Historical Notes” of The Handmaid’s Tale), prologues in short stories, series, drama and screen narrative, a concordance of concepts to works, seminar questions and the sources.

See also: The Opening Pages (Chapter 9), on the first pages of the main narrative, which a prologue precedes; Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7), which applies distance to the cutaway inside the main narrative; The Ending (Chapter 16), whose final note is the same contract kept at the far boundary; The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), on the cost of moving a reader between minds; The Reveal (Chapter 15), on the single late disclosure.
