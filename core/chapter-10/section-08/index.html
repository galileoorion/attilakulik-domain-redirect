Chapter 10 · The Prologue

8. Worked Analyses

Continue in Reference

Three close readings, a short screen reading and the household revision case. Each reading names the type, the load-bearing axis, the domains served and the mode of closing.

Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818): the frame as prologue

The novel opens with four letters from Robert Walton, an explorer bound for the Arctic, to his sister in England. His ship is caught in ice; his crew see a gigantic figure driving a dog-sledge across the floes; the next morning they take aboard a second traveller, half-dead, who has been pursuing the first. That stranger is Victor Frankenstein, and his story, which contains the creature’s, fills the book until Walton’s letters resume at the end, when Victor dies aboard and the creature comes to mourn over his body.

The type is the frame narrator’s reception, and the load-bearing axis is viewpoint: Walton is the listener, and his letters are the only position from which the whole chain of telling can be seen. The letters serve five domains. Plot: they frame the ending, since the reader meets Victor at the end of his life before learning how he reached it. Character: Walton’s ambition, his wish to go where no one has gone, mirrors Victor’s and makes him the reader’s warning in advance. Theme: knowledge pursued past its limit is posed as a question in a man who has not yet paid for it. Reader contract: the Arctic letters buy patience for Victor’s long, undramatic Genevan childhood, chapters of family, schooling and friendship that would be slow without the ice in front of them. Authority: Walton’s letters are the transmitting record, written, he says, from the stranger’s own account. The closing is total, since the frame is re-entered and completed, and it is also what turns Walton back south when his crew demand it: the one person in the book who hears the story and changes course. A prologue that buys patience for a slow opening is the usual failure of the form; this one succeeds because it does four other things as well.

Markus Zusak, The Book Thief (2005): the prologue that spoils, kept

The Book Thief is narrated by Death, and its prologue, “A Mountain Range of Rubble”, is his account of the three times he saw the girl he calls the book thief: under a white sky, beside a railway line where her younger brother died on a train; under a black sky, when an enemy pilot crashed near her town; and under a red sky, when the bombs fell on Himmel Street and she was left in the rubble of everyone she loved. The book then begins with the first of these and spends the length of the book reaching the third.

The type is the proleptic flash-forward, and the novel takes the riskier of its two forms, showing the catastrophe itself, not only its aftermath. By the chapter’s account it should fail as the prologue that spoils, since a reader who knows Himmel Street will be destroyed has lost all suspense about it. It succeeds because the book runs on curiosity and dread, not on suspense. The reader watches a girl learn to read, steal books and find a family on a street she has been told will be destroyed, and every ordinary scene is coloured by that knowledge, which is the felt-stakes carrier of the reader contract at full strength. The load-bearing axis is register: Death’s voice, wry, weary and given to stating outcomes in advance, is one no character in the book could supply, and it goes on disclosing, announcing deaths before they arrive, a habit readers argue over. The closing is total, since the narrative reaches the red sky, and it is extended by an epilogue in which Death meets Liesel again at her own death, as an old woman in Sydney, and gives back the manuscript she lost in the bombing. The prologue made one promise, that the three meetings would be told, and the book keeps it in the form in which it was made.

Yann Martel, Life of Pi (2001): the authority domain

Life of Pi opens with an Author’s Note written in the voice of a fictional version of its author. A novelist whose last book has died on him travels to India, and in a coffee house in Pondicherry an old man, Francis Adirubasamy, tells him he has a story that will make him believe in God. The novelist finds Pi Patel in Toronto and promises to tell the story in Pi’s own voice. The novel then gives Pi’s account of 227 days in a lifeboat with a Bengal tiger, broken by passages in which the novelist describes visiting the grown Pi; and it ends with a transcript of two Japanese officials interviewing Pi, who offers them a second, brutal version of the same voyage without animals, and asks which story they prefer.

The type is the frame narrator’s reception crossed with the editorial note, and the domain is authority. The Author’s Note establishes a provenance (Adirubasamy, then Pi), a transmitting intermediary (the novelist, who claims to have changed only the telling), and a promise about belief that the book will test. It sets little tone and advances no plot, and the five-domain model can file it only as reader contract, which misses what it does. The closing is recontextualisation. The ending does not return to Pondicherry; it puts a second account beside the first and leaves the reader holding the Note’s promise, that the story would make her believe, as a question about which story she has chosen. A prologue that claimed no authority would have nothing for the ending to unsettle. This is the case for the authority domain as a separate category, and it also shows the objection: the Note’s work could be described as world and reader contract combined, and only the ending shows why that description falls short.

A screen reading: Saving Private Ryan (1998)

Steven Spielberg’s film opens on an old man walking among the graves of an American war cemetery in Normandy, his family behind him. He kneels, the camera closes on his eyes, and the film cuts to the landing on Omaha Beach in 1944 and to Captain Miller. Many viewers take the old man for Miller remembering. Miller dies in the final battle, and only then does the film return to the cemetery and show that the old man is Ryan, the private Miller’s squad was sent to bring home, standing at Miller’s grave. The type is the false prologue, which depends on an error the viewer makes; the closing is total and recontextualising at once, since the return to the cemetery completes the frame and changes whose memory the film has been. It keeps the rule for the type: the misreading is rewarded without being required, because a viewer who never took the old man for Miller still has the film’s ending.

The household revision case: the prologue

The household novel’s prologue is set three days before the father stopped answering the phone, in his viewpoint, in the shop. The canon fixes what it must plant: the till counted twice, a boy at the glass waved off, the sign not flipped to CLOSED, the register drawer that sticks on the K, and the good pen put into his coat pocket instead of left by the till. The main narrative belongs to his three adult children, Elena, Nadia and Dov, in the July after his death.

Version A, the draft.

He waited until the street was empty before he pulled the blind down. Then he crossed to the register, opened the drawer that stuck — it always stuck, it had stuck for eleven years — and took out the folded papers he had put there in March, and did not read them again, because he already knew.

There had been a car on the corner for three evenings now. He had counted. A man in it who did not get out.

He carried the papers through to the back, where the sink was, and he burned them one at a time over the basin, holding each until the flame reached his fingers, and when the last of it had gone down the drain he ran the tap for a long time. Then he put the good pen in his coat pocket, where nobody would find it, and turned the sign in the window to CLOSED, and stood in the dark of his own shop, listening.

Diagnosis. It is competent prose and a structural failure. It passes the higher bar (test 1) for the wrong reason: nobody in Chapter One knows any of it, because none of it is the book’s material. The register is a thriller’s, surveillance and burned evidence, set before a quiet family novel, so test 5 (Register) finds a register the main voice would contradict, and test 9 (Genre contract) fails on pitch, pace, mode and scale. Test 6 (Closing) fails outright, since the papers are ash, the car never returns and the book contains no conspiracy. Test 7 (Plants) finds six unredeemed specifics in under two hundred words: the blind, the car, the man, the papers, the basin and the tap. By the primacy effect the reader builds a thriller from these lines, and reads the book through it: Nadia not answering her brother’s calls becomes a cover-up, Dov’s silence in the doorway complicity, the changed locks a security measure. And the draft spends the household’s material. The pen goes into the pocket where nobody would find it, which turns care into concealment and destroys the ending the book is built toward, and the sign is turned to CLOSED, when the book needs a man who did not finish his day.

Version B, the revision.

Three days before he stopped answering the phone, he stood at the register and counted the till twice, though it came out the same both times. Outside, a boy about the age his son used to be knocked on the glass, mouthing something about the sign in the window, and he waved him off without looking up. He would remember, later — if there was a later he’d know how to measure — that he’d meant to flip the sign to CLOSED and hadn’t. That the register drawer stuck on the K. That he had, for reasons he could not have explained to his wife, put the good pen in his coat pocket instead of leaving it by the till, the way he always did. In case, he would have said, if she had asked; and he could not have said in case of what.

What the revision did. It changed the type, from the victim’s last hour with the death withheld to the last ordinary day, which suits a book about aftermath. It deleted the invented plot and kept what happened on the day, which the writer knew and had avoided because it was quiet. It turned announcements into seeds: a till counted twice, a boy waved off, a sign not flipped, a drawer that sticks, a pen in a pocket. It restored the pen’s meaning, care and not concealment, and gave the last sentence to the pen and to two words of a reason the father cannot finish. In case is a fragment of the line he will say to Nadia the second time he loses the pen, in case anyone ever needs to check, which the reader hears in full only in the cutaway Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7) analyses and which closes the book in The Ending (Chapter 16). The domain audit finds plot (an unwitnessable day), character (the father’s small private dread), world (the shop’s working rhythm), theme (counting set against unmeasured time) and the reader contract (a dread carried into every domestic scene); plot, character and the reader contract require exteriority.

The closing is fragmentary. The drawer returns at about a quarter of the book, when Elena works it free on the third try and smells fresh ink, and its job is reassurance. The pen returns at about two-thirds, when Nadia finds it in the coat, and the reader knows how it got there. The line lands at the end, when the three of them read the ledger together. A single late reveal would ask the reader to recall, at page 340, a frame primed for one page three hundred pages earlier; three fragments keep it warm, and each is an object or a phrase a reader can recognise.

What the revision has not solved. It is quiet, and quietness is a submission risk: an agent reading at speed may not reach the second sentence, a cost imposed by an institution that the writer should pay knowingly. The retrospective He would remember, later grants the father a foreknowledge he cannot have, and a reader may take it as the author being ominous, the passage’s one unearned effect. In case is either the right size or a hair too large: if a reader hears the line coming in Backstory and Flashback (Chapter 7) before Nadia does, the cutaway loses some of its surprise and gains a recognition, and only a reader new to the book can say which. And the boy at the glass and the sign never flipped are planted in a privileged position and redeemed nowhere in the book as it stands; they stay open on purpose, alongside the mother’s silence, unless the ending decides otherwise, and an editor applying test 7 (Plants) will ask about both.
