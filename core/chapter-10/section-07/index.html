Chapter 10 · The Prologue

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below fails through a mechanism, not a surface feature. Each ends with a published work that uses the feature successfully because the mechanism is absent. The prologue makes this easy to show, since the form’s best-known instances break its most confident rules: Romeo and Juliet’s prologue gives away the plot, and “The Custom-House” runs to some fifty pages, opens a distance it never closes, and was republished by Hawthorne, against complaints, without the change of a word. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue has twelve patterns.

Chapter One in disguise

Symptom. The prologue shares the protagonist’s time, place, viewpoint and register, and was labelled Prologue because the writer was unsure the opening earned its place as Chapter One.

Mechanism. No distance is open, so the label creates an expectation the material does not honour, and it tells the reader to hold at arm’s length a scene that is the beginning of the book. The label is a hedge: it feels like a structural choice and is the postponement of one. In this edition’s judgement it is the commonest pattern in draft manuscripts, which is why the blanket advice to cut a prologue so often helps.

Remedy. Test 3 (Label), which is also the cure: relabel, renumber and read.

Exception. Ellison’s Invisible Man has a labelled Prologue in the narrator’s own voice and register. Only time differs, since the Prologue speaks from the cellar after everything, and that one axis carries the novel’s architecture: the label tells the reader that the voice already knows where the story goes, so the young man’s naivety in chapter 1 reads as naivety. One axis is enough when it does the work.

False advertising

Symptom. A violent, hooky or high-stakes prologue whose pitch the book never matches: a murder before a quiet domestic novel, a chase before three hundred pages of interiority.

Mechanism. The primacy effect turns every later page into a deviation from the promised book. The separation makes it worse than the borrowed hook of an ordinary opening, because the label makes the promise deliberate and the privileged position makes every detail in it load-bearing. It is the same violation as the ending’s mismatched final note, committed at the other boundary.

Remedy. Test 9 (Genre contract), run on pitch, pace, mode and scale; then revise the prologue, not the book.

Exception. Wuthering Heights opens on Lockwood, a fatuous tenant whose chapters promise a light comedy of manners and misjudged neighbours, and what follows is a novel of extreme violence and longing. The mismatch is the method: the distance between Lockwood’s register and what he blunders into is what the book is about. A mismatch the book uses is a structure; one it has not noticed is a mis-sold book.

The anonymous-victim cold open

Symptom. A prologue focalised through someone murdered at its end, usually unnamed, usually a woman, in a book about the detective who will investigate.

Mechanism. The reader is asked to occupy a mind that exists only to be destroyed. With no future it has no want beyond survival and no properties worth individuating, and readers sense a demand for feeling that has not been earned. The type’s long use is a second mechanism, since a label that predicts its contents has stopped working as a device, and a writer choosing it should know that it has made a woman’s terror a standard opening unit in a great deal of fiction.

Remedy. Individuate the victim, with a want unrelated to her death and a specific that returns later; or substitute the last ordinary day, which gives dread without a death.

Exception. The Da Vinci Code follows its victim to his death and succeeds because Saunière is not a camera position: he acts, choosing in his last minutes how to pass on what he knows, and his acts become the book’s first clues, so the investment the prologue asks for is repaid by the whole plot.

The prologue that spoils

Symptom. The prologue reveals the outcome: who dies, who is guilty, how it ends.

Mechanism. Disclosure converts suspense into curiosity, which destroys a book whose value lies in outcomes and serves one whose value lies in causes. The failure is conditional on what the book is for; there is no general prohibition on disclosure.

Remedy. Ask which engine the book runs on. If curiosity, disclose freely; if suspense, disclose an aftermath instead, which promises a shape without answering the question.

Exception. The sonnet before Romeo and Juliet gives away the feud, the deaths and the reconciliation in fourteen lines, and the play’s pathos depends on it: every hopeful line is heard by an audience that knows. Markus Zusak’s The Book Thief, among the worked analyses, does the same in a novel. A prologue may spoil anything the book is not for.

The unresolved distance

Symptom. The prologue opens a real gap, a time jump, a viewpoint, a frame, and the book never returns to it.

Mechanism. A contextual frame is built, primed briefly and suspended for good, and the reader holds it at a cost, expecting a return that never comes. Nothing feels wrong while reading; the failure arrives at the end, as a sense that the book was longer at the front than it needed to be, which readers report as “the prologue was unnecessary”. Writers commit it because they know the connection and cannot see that it was never written down.

Remedy. Test 6 (Closing), as a literal inventory with page numbers; then build the closing, preferably in fragments, or absorb the material.

Exception. “The Custom-House” opens a distance on all four axes, two centuries, another building, a narrator absent from the romance and an essayist’s register, and closes none of it. It works, so far as critics agree that it does, because the essay is about a man who cannot enter the past he holds in his hands, and returning to him would assert a continuity the book denies. Whether it is design or indulgence is still argued, which is the honest status of the exception.
