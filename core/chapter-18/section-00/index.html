Chapter 18 · The Structural Edit

The Structural Edit

Continue in Reference

In what order to revise a whole book, and when to stop

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 18. Part IV: Beyond the Book and Into Practice.

In brief. A structural edit revises a book’s architecture before its sentences. A structural edit is difficult because its repairs must be made in the right order: every decision in a novel rests on other decisions, so a repair made in the wrong order is undone by the repair that follows it. The chapter’s claim is that a book should be revised in the order in which its decisions depend on one another, not in the order of its pages. That order begins with what the book promises and asks; then come whose book it is and how its times and threads are arranged, then the shape of the whole, then the joins, and last the scenes and their sentences. Diagnosis runs the other way, from the place where a reader felt a problem up to the highest level at which its cause can lie. The chapter has two tools. The book map is a table with one row per scene, and the other chapters’ instruments can be read off it; the revision ladder puts those instruments in order. Use the chapter when you have a full draft and a pile of notes, when each revision seems to undo the last, or when you cannot tell whether the book is finished.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. Build a book map of a full draft, and read the other chapters’ charts and tables off it.
  2. Sort a set of readers’ notes onto the revision ladder by where each problem was felt and where its cause lies.
  3. Revise in the order of dependence, recording for each repair the tests it reopens.
  4. Diagnose five failures of revision (a page-order edit, premature polish, a local repair, a cascade and an edit by addition) and correct each one.
  5. Decide when a structural edit is finished, and defend the failures you keep.

Contents

The chapter begins by saying what a structural edit is and why the order of repairs matters, then sets out the ladder and the map that serve that order, and ends with material for use at the desk with a draft and its notes.

  1. The Problem
    A household edit made in page order, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The structural edit and its neighbours, and what other chapters keep
  3. How It Works
    How experienced writers revise, what editors do first, what a map should record and how to read a reader’s note
  4. The Taxonomy
    The five rungs of the revision ladder, and the ruling on formal closure
  5. The Mechanism
    The book map, the order of dependence, the edit list and the stopping rule
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Seven questions about the edit itself, and a table from symptom to rung
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures of revision, each paired with a work in which the same habit succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    The Great Gatsby, Never Let Me Go, Make Me, Annie Hall and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the ladder assumes, and what no edit can decide

A writer with a draft and notes in hand can start at the mechanism, build the map, and then use the diagnostic tests. If a writer has revised several times and the book is getting no better, the failure patterns are the place to start, since the order of the work may be part of the problem.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds a longer version of this chapter. The longer version sets out the theory at greater length and places every chapter’s instruments on the ladder. It covers the kinds of structural edit, the ruling on formal closure in full, further failure patterns and the procedures for each test. It adds further analyses (the Harry Potter series, The Martian, A Little Life and the remix of Arrested Development), the household book map and edit list in full, a glossary of the chapter’s terms and the sources.
