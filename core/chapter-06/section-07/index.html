Chapter 6 · The Chapter Break

7. Failure Patterns

Continue in Reference

Each pattern below is described by its mechanism and ends with a published work that commits it and succeeds, because the pattern fails through the mechanism, not the surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the feature is harmless. The Reference chapter’s full catalogue adds seven more: the arbitrary cut, the anticlimactic soft landing, the uniform chapter, the chapter that is really two, the recap opening, the epigraph doing the chapter’s work, and the undifferentiated sequence.

Cliffhanger fatigue

Symptom. Every chapter, or nearly every one, ends on unresolved danger.

Mechanism. The price of an exit is relative, so where every exit costs the most the reader recalibrates and the device stops carrying information. She also learns that this book always ends chapters this way, and starts to read the ending as a formal habit rather than a fact about the situation, so that she stops registering danger. The fault persists because it is what good advice produces when followed literally: each chapter ends compellingly, and the fault is invisible from inside any one of them.

Remedy. Run test 7 (Variety) on eleven consecutive chapters, then install soft landings at the ends of movements, so that the cliffhangers regain their contrast.

The exception. The Count of Monte Cristo ends its chapters on unresolved turns with a regularity a modern editor would query, and has been read compulsively for nearly two centuries. It was published as a newspaper feuilleton in three runs with gaps, and its newspaper instalments did not consistently end where its book chapters do. The effect of repeated turns depends on how the text was delivered and read; a fixed daily dose and an absence of fatigue cannot be inferred from the format.

The padded chapter

Symptom. The chapter goes on past its own ending, with a further conversation, a second place or a stretch of reflection that does not serve its proposition.

Mechanism. The writer is filling a container to a habitual size, and the break falls after the boundary the reader has already made: she closed the event when the chapter’s question was answered and reads the rest as residue. This is the usual mechanism behind the report that a chapter sags at the end, and it explains why the sag is seldom where the writer looks for it.

Remedy. Test 6 (Proportion), then delete the last page and read what remains. If nothing is lost, the chapter ended a page earlier. Repeat.

The exception. By any proportion test the cetological chapters of Moby-Dick (1851), on the classification of whales, the whiteness of the whale and the whale in pictures, are padding, and they are why the novel is what it is. Melville makes the digression the book’s proposition and gives each excursus its own chapter and title, so the reader never doubts that it is deliberate. Padding pretends to be story; these chapters do not pretend.

The buried pivot

Symptom. A reveal or turn arrives mid-chapter, and the chapter continues.

Mechanism. The final position confers emphasis for nothing; a turn placed mid-chapter forfeits it and must generate its own, which costs words and usually fails. The pages after the reveal are also read at reduced attention, while the reader is still absorbing the turn. The fault persists because drafting follows chronology, and moving a reveal means reordering, which feels like more work than it is.

Remedy. For each chapter containing a turn, ask whether it could be the last thing in the chapter; if the pages after it hold the reaction, the reaction may be the next chapter’s opening (placement 2).

The exception. Ford Madox Ford’s The Good Soldier (1915) buries its revelations, deaths, betrayals and the central adultery, mid-paragraph and in subordinate clauses. The burial is the characterisation: Dowell cannot order his material because he cannot face it. The condition is that the misplacement be attributable to the narrator; where only the book can be seen to have buried a turn, it is a fault.

The false cliffhanger

Symptom. A chapter ends on apparent danger that the first paragraph of the next dispels: the noise was the cat, the figure a friend.

Mechanism. The reader invested attention in a threat the book knew was not real. A reader may become less responsive after repeated false alarms, so that a later genuine cliffhanger has less force. It appears at the points where the story has nothing to offer, because it is the cheapest way to look strong there.

Remedy. Test 4 (Stopped reader) across the boundary: read the ending and the next page together and ask whether three days of the reader’s anxiety would have been repaid. If not, replace it with a type that promises nothing, an echo or a question close.

The exception. In Don Quixote, Part I (1605; translated by Edith Grossman, 2003), chapter 8 breaks off in the middle of a duel, swords raised, because the narrator’s source stops there; chapter 9 recounts finding the continuation in an Arabic manuscript in the market at Toledo. The deflation is the joke, and the joke is the book’s method. The condition is that the trivial resolution be more interesting than the danger would have been.

The sleep ending

Symptom. The chapter ends with the viewpoint character falling asleep, going to bed or otherwise ceasing to be conscious.

Mechanism. Closural allusion fires with nothing behind it. Sleep and darkness are among the strongest terminal signals in the language, so the chapter feels finished while resolving nothing, and the exit is made as cheap as possible at the moment the writer stopped thinking about it. A book that runs on daily chronology will do it at every break unless the writer intervenes.

Remedy. Move the break earlier, to the last moment at which the character wanted something. Sleep is seldom the chapter’s real boundary; it is the point past the boundary where the writer stopped typing. If the sleep must stay, make the last thought before it do work the sleep cannot.

The exception. Kate Atkinson’s Life After Life (2013) ends life after life of Ursula Todd, born in snow on 11 February 1910, with her death, marked again and again by the phrase darkness fell, after which the next life begins. The closural allusion is the book’s subject, death as a boundary crossed and recrossed, and its repetition is the form. A closural allusion is a fault when it is borrowed and a technique when it is thematic; the same holds for chapters ending on departures, nightfall or a door closing.
