Chapter 6 · The Chapter Break

The Chapter Break

Continue in Reference

Where to cut, and what the reader holds if she stops there

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition) — Chapter 6. Part II: The Joins.

In brief. A novel’s chapters are not found in its story; they are placed in it, and every chapter break is a point at which the reader may close the book. The last sentence before the break sets what stopping costs her: an ending that leaves a danger, a question or an incompletion active makes stopping expensive, and an ending that completes its movement makes it free. Neither price is right in the abstract, because prices are relative. A book that charges the most at every break can lose the contrast that makes an expensive exit effective, so the right price for one break depends on the breaks around it. The tools are a statement of what each chapter proposes and what its ending does with it, six ending types with known costs, chapter length read as tempo, twelve positions for the cut, and ten diagnostic questions. Use them on a draft that readers call relentless, slow or shapeless when no single scene is at fault.

What you can do after this chapter

  1. State what a chapter proposes, and name what its ending does with the proposal.
  2. Choose among six ending types by the price each sets on stopping, not by habit.
  3. Move a break one beat earlier and one beat later, and say what each position buys.
  4. Audit the lengths and endings of every chapter in a manuscript, and read the pattern as tempo.
  5. Decide where a book’s larger divisions fall and what its headings promise.

Key terms

Chapter break; segmentation and the segmentation problem; the offered exit, its price and the permission to stop; capitulum and articulated time; intertitle, epigraph, part-issue, number plan and feuilleton; closural allusion; event boundary; the Zeigarnik effect; proposition and payoff, with the five operations (resolve, escalate, hold open, recast, exchange) and abandonment; cliffhanger, soft landing, pivot, echo close, question close and the proposed recasting close; the principle of proportion; placement strategy and the substitution test; chapter architecture; the chapter-length audit. Each is defined at first use and collected in the consolidated glossary.

Contents

The first files say what a chapter break is and why the reader feels it; the middle files set out the ending types and the mechanism that chooses among them; the last are for use at the desk.

  1. The Problem
    A gripping ending in the wrong place, and the chapter’s claim
  2. Definitions
    The chapter break against the scene and the section break, the scales of division, adjacent terms
  3. How It Works
    The chapter as an inherited technology, the serial, the heading, closure at small scale, event segmentation, the Zeigarnik effect, and the dates of the convention
  4. The Taxonomy
    Five ending types, and a proposed sixth: the recasting close
  5. The Mechanism
    Proposition and payoff, the price of the exit, length as tempo, twelve placements, and the architecture of Parts, titles and epigraphs
  6. Diagnostic Tests
    Ten questions on one page, and how to read their failures together
  7. Failure Patterns
    Five recurrent failures, each with a published work that commits it and succeeds
  8. Worked Analyses
    Great Expectations, Vanity Fair, The Underground Railroad, Game of Thrones and Severance, and the household revision case
  9. Checklist and Exercises
    The revision checklist and five exercises for your own draft
  10. Coda
    What the price model assumes about readers

A writer in revision can start at the diagnostic tests and the chapter-length audit in the exercises; the short coda is best read early.

The full treatment

The Reference layer holds the complete chapter, The Chapter Break: the history at length, each ending type’s textual carriers, a table of chapter lengths across fourteen novels, twelve failure patterns, close readings of Bleak House, The Count of Monte Cristo, Middlemarch, Don Quixote, Moby-Dick, Catch-22 and Tristram Shandy, the break in the short story, the series, the serial, screen, children’s fiction, audio and translation, seminar questions and an assessment rubric, and the sources.

See also: Scene and Sequel (Chapter 2), for the six beats by which the placements are named; The Transition (Chapter 5), on how a gap is crossed once it is placed; The Viewpoint Shift (Chapter 13), on who is on the other side of it; The Ending (Chapter 16), which inherits proposition and payoff at the scale of the book.
