Chapter 6 · The Chapter Break

4. The Taxonomy

Continue in Reference

The taxonomy names six ways of closing a chapter, so that end the chapter well becomes a choice among options with known costs, and the commonest fault at the break, the same close every time, becomes visible as a choice. The types describe last sentences, not chapters: the same chapter can take any of the six by a change to its final paragraph. They are not ranked, since the cliffhanger is more expensive than the soft landing and not more sophisticated. They compound. And they answer which one, here, never how many across the book, which is a question of distribution that The Mechanism and the diagnostic tests handle.

The cliffhanger

The cliffhanger ends on a danger, a crisis or a physical situation left visibly unresolved, so that the reader stops, if she stops, holding an open threat. It is the most expensive exit available, because it can turn a break into an urgent prompt to continue, and it has the shortest life under repetition. Its carriers are the interrupted action (a hand on the handle), the revealed threat named and left standing, the withheld object of a verb (she turned, and saw what was in the doorway), a short final sentence after a long one, and the unanswered address. It exploits investment and cannot create it, which is why well-built novels save it for late in the book. Under-delivered, it is the false cliffhanger; over-delivered, it cuts so early (and then she saw—) that the reader does not know what to fear. Many chapters of Dumas’s The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46; translated by Robin Buss, 1996) end on turns. The newspaper instalments had their own boundaries, sometimes within a chapter, and publication paused between the novel’s three runs; the book-chapter endings cannot be equated with daily newspaper cuts. The threat at the end of chapter 38 of Great Expectations (1860–61) is figurative rather than an event in the scene: Pip compares what is coming to a heavy slab prepared over a long time and finally dropped on his stronghold. The next chapter reveals the event behind the metaphor.

The soft landing

The soft landing ends on a settled beat: the chapter’s movement completes, its tension discharges, and stopping is free. It is the type that makes the others work, since a book with no free exits has no expensive ones. Its carriers are the completed change, a return to stillness, narratorial comment, closural allusion, and a final sentence that falls rather than lifts. A resolved chapter is not an inert one: it can close a domestic argument completely and leave the novel’s question untouched and growing, so that the reader returns tomorrow because she wants to. And where free exits fall tells the reader about the book’s shape. Under-delivered, it is the anticlimax that releases pressure the book needed; over-delivered, it is the valedictory close that makes every chapter sound like the last. Austen’s chapters in Pride and Prejudice (1813) close on settled social facts, a visit concluded or an opinion formed, releasing the reader at the level of event while the novel’s question, a judgement, carries on. The chapters of the first part of Things Fall Apart (1958) close on communal movements completing, giving the book the rhythm of a repeating year, which is the precondition for the third part’s rupture.

The pivot

The pivot ends on a reveal or a turn placed at the close, sending the reader forward with new urgency. It uses the leverage of the final position: Peter Rabinowitz’s rules of notice (The Opening Pages, Chapter 9) apply at chapter scale, so material placed last is read as significant without the writer spending a word to make it so, and a reveal placed mid-chapter forfeits that emphasis. It is therefore often spoiled by placement rather than execution, because writers set the turn down where it occurred to them in drafting. Its carriers are the withheld fact released last, the detail that recontextualises the scene, an arrival, and an unmasking. Under-delivered, it is the buried pivot; over-delivered, a reveal at every close, so that nothing stays unknown long enough to be wondered about. Esther’s narrative in chapter 59 of Bleak House (1852–53) ends at the burial-ground gate on And it was my mother, cold and dead: eight words, seven of them monosyllables, after a chapter of frantic movement, resolving the pursuit and reframing all of it.

The echo close

The echo close ends on an image, a line or a gesture that carries feeling or theme rather than plot, so that what pulls the reader on, if anything, is curiosity or feeling rather than threat. It is the means by which a book’s argument becomes audible at its joints. Because the final position confers significance for nothing, there are no incidental images at a chapter’s close, which is the type’s resource and its hazard. Its carriers are the object held last, a returning image, a gesture left without gloss, a widened frame and a line of dialogue left standing. Under-delivered, it is atmosphere with no referent; over-delivered, an object so plainly loaded that the reader sees the author placing it. The first chapter of The Great Gatsby (1925) ends on Gatsby stretching his arms towards the water and a single green light, minute and far away, which the novel spends two hundred pages making mean something. Chapter 47 of Bleak House, the death of Jo the crossing-sweeper, closes by turning from the scene to address the Queen, the lords and gentlemen and the reader (Dead, your Majesty), an echo whose charge is moral.

The question close

The question close ends on a question, stated or implied, without a physical danger: the engine is what does this mean? rather than is she safe? Meir Sternberg’s distinction between suspense, uncertainty about what will happen, and curiosity, uncertainty about what has happened, explains why it lasts longer than the cliffhanger: curiosity accumulates where suspense must escalate. It works only if the reader can state the question in a sentence; vague unease is not a question. Under-delivered, it is the vague close; over-delivered, the italicised question addressed to nobody (But who had put it there?), which turns a hidden engine into a visible one. Kafka’s chapters in The Trial (1925; translated by Breon Mitchell, 1998) close on procedural questions that never mature into answers, and the accumulation is the novel’s subject. The day-sections of Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day (1989) end on a question Stevens has declined to ask himself, which is the type used for character.

A proposed sixth: the recasting close

The five types assume that an ending’s business is with what comes next. Some endings in good novels look backwards instead. The recasting close is an ending whose final sentence changes the meaning of the chapter just finished, so that the reader’s first movement is to reconsider rather than to turn the page; what it alters is not the situation but the interpretation of what she has read. One question separates it from the pivot: does the new information change what happens next, or what has already happened? Its practical consequence is that it leaves the writer free at the next break, because a pivot obliges the following chapter to engage the new situation and a recasting obliges nothing; it is therefore the instrument for the points where a book needs to change thread, jump time or hand over viewpoint. Its carriers are the motive supplied last, a narrator’s self-description, a re-naming, an admission about the narration, and a move into a wider time frame (It was the last time she saw him). Chapter 3 of The Great Gatsby ends on Nick’s claim to be one of the few honest people he has ever known, which reaches back across a chapter of gossip and elisions and puts his reliability in question. Chapter 7 of Things Fall Apart narrates the killing of Ikemefuna and gives Okonkwo’s fear of appearing weak within the chapter; its close turns to Nwoye, who remembers hearing abandoned twins in the forest. That delayed reaction can recast the killing as part of Nwoye’s estrangement. The test is to cover the last sentence and say what the chapter was about, then uncover it and say again; if the answers match, the sentence does not recast. Under-delivered, it confirms what the chapter showed; over-delivered, it voids the chapter it closes.

Three objections stand. It may be a pivot facing backwards, and the pivot’s definition can already accommodate sending the reader back. Readers may not experience the two as different. And the criterion is unstable: Achebe’s closing turn to Nwoye can both reinterpret the killing and point toward its consequences, which suggests a continuum rather than a boundary. This chapter adopts the recasting close for diagnosis, because it names a decision the five types cannot express, ending a chapter with no forward obligation so that the next is free, and concedes the third objection as unanswered. The Ending (Chapter 16) adopts or rejects it at the scale of a book’s last line, and The Reveal (Chapter 15) meets it from the other side. The Ending (Chapter 16) rules that at a book’s last line the category no longer discriminates, since nothing comes next and every close looks back, and keeps it here as a type of chapter ending; the question of how far a close may recast a whole book passes to The Reveal (Chapter 15). The Reveal (Chapter 15) answers it with the teller rule: a close may falsify only what a teller in the book could have shaped.

Must an ending be only one type?

No. Compounding is normal: chapter 32 of Vanity Fair is a pivot, an echo close and a recasting at once (worked analyses). Some compounds contradict each other, as when a chapter resolves and reopens in one blended paragraph; the reliable form closes in the penultimate paragraph and opens in the last. The table summarises the six by carrier, price and characteristic faults.

Type Chief carriers Price of stopping Under-delivered Over-delivered
Cliffhanger Interrupted action; revealed threat; short final sentence Highest A false danger, dispelled next page An unnamed threat
Soft landing Completed change; stillness; closural allusion Free Anticlimax Every chapter sounding like the last
Pivot Withheld fact released last; arrival High, informational The buried pivot A reveal at every close
Echo close Object held last; returning image; unanswered line Low Mood without referent The visibly significant object
Question close Anomaly stated flatly; withheld reaction Moderate No statable question The italicised question
Recasting close (proposed) Motive supplied last; self-description; admission Ambiguous: nothing pends, but the reader is unfinished Confirmation The chapter voided

Read down the price column and the table becomes a scale from highest to free, along which a book’s endings should move; read across and each row says what to look for when a type has been reached for by habit.
