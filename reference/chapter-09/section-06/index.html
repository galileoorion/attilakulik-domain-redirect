Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

6. The Attack: In Medias Res, Orientation, and the Repertoire of Gambits

Continue in Core

6.1 The oldest problem in the field

Every narrative must decide where in the story’s chronology the telling begins — the dramaturgical attack — and every such decision trades momentum against comprehension.

The terms are inherited from classical rhetoric. Horace, in the Ars Poetica (c. 19 BCE), praised Homer for plunging his audience in medias res — “into the middle of things” — rather than beginning ab ovo, from the egg, with Leda and the swan. Two features of the classical case are usually lost in modern craft citation:

  1. Horace was describing epic, a form with a known story. Homer’s audience already knew the Trojan War; the plunge cost them nothing, because their orientation was cultural rather than textual. A novelist writing an invented world has no such reserve, which is why unmodified in medias res is riskier now than the classical precedent suggests.
  2. The epic compensated structurally. Homer plunges, then recovers the antecedents through embedded retrospection. In medias res was never a licence to omit exposition; it was a rule about sequence, not about inclusion.

6.2 Sternberg’s expositional modes

The most rigorous modern treatment is Meir Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978), which reframes the whole question. Sternberg’s insight is that exposition is not a place in a novel but a distribution problem: every narrative must convey its antecedents, and the only variables are when and in what order.

Mode Description Effect
Preliminary (concentrated) Antecedents delivered up front, before the action Maximum clarity, minimum suspense; the nineteenth-century default
Delayed Action first; antecedents supplied later, often piecemeal Creates curiosity about the past as well as suspense about the future
Distributed Antecedents dispersed throughout, never gathered Sustains ambiguity; characteristic of modernist and detective fiction

Sternberg’s crucial distinction is between suspense (uncertainty about the future) and curiosity (uncertainty about the past). Delayed exposition converts backstory from an obligation into an engine: the reader who does not know why Josef K. was arrested, or what happened at 124, is being pulled forward by a question about what already happened. This reframes the info-dump problem entirely. The issue with front-loaded backstory is not that backstory is boring but that delivering it early spends an asset.

Teaching formulation. Backstory withheld is a resource. Backstory delivered is an expense. The opening pages should ask what they can afford to spend, not how much they can fit.

6.3 The trade-off in plain terms

In medias res Orientation
Buys Momentum; immediate question; the impression of a book already running Clarity; investment; a stable frame for later disruption
Costs Risk that action without context reads as noise rather than tension Risk of a first page in which nothing is yet at stake
Fails when The reader cannot tell who to care about or what the action means The disruption arrives after the reader has left
Suits Thriller, crime, action-led commercial fiction; any book whose premise is legible instantly Literary fiction, historical fiction, systems-heavy speculative fiction; any book whose premise requires a baseline

The item in that table that most needs attention is the failure mode of in medias res, because it is counter-intuitive. Action is not intrinsically interesting. Tension is a function of caring about an outcome, and caring requires knowing what is at stake for whom. A chase scene involving two strangers whose names the reader has just met is not tense; it is only fast. This is the precise mechanism by which a “gripping” opening can be boring.

6.4 The hybrid resolution

Most working openings do not choose. They adopt a resolution so common that it deserves its own formulation:

Open in medias res on a small, comprehensible action — not the book’s central conflict — that delivers voice, character and world in the same breath. Let the real disruption arrive once the reader has enough footing to feel its weight.

The action must satisfy three conditions:

  1. Already in motion — the reader arrives after it started, which supplies momentum.
  2. Instantly comprehensible — buying flowers, walking to a bridge, avoiding a telephone. No context required.
  3. Characterising — the choice of action and the manner of performing it reveal the person.

Woolf, Dostoevsky and Carroll (§4.3) are all instances. So is Camus: Meursault receives a telegram and arranges leave. So is Ishiguro: a butler considers a motoring trip. The engine of the book — murder, arrest, war, the party’s revelations — arrives later, into a reader who is now able to feel it.

6.5 A taxonomy of opening gambits

The binary of in medias res versus orientation is too coarse for practical use, since it describes only the temporal relation. What follows is a working repertoire of gambits — recognisable strategies with characteristic costs. They are not mutually exclusive, and strong openings often braid two.

# Gambit Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 The Small Action A minor, legible task already under way Mrs Dalloway Momentum + character at once Can read as slight if the action never escalates
2 The Aphorism A general proposition, then descent to the particular Pride and Prejudice; Anna Karenina Authority; instant tone and theme Defers character; sounds arch if the voice can’t sustain it
3 The Voice Declaration A narrator announcing themselves Moby-Dick; Invisible Man; Huckleberry Finn Immediate intimacy and reliability protocol Nothing is happening; the voice must be singular
4 The Panorama A place or system described before any person Bleak House; A Passage to India Scale, atmosphere, thematic frame Slow; fails without a thesis behind the description
5 The Frame A narrator receiving or transmitting the story Frankenstein; Wuthering Heights; Season of Migration to the North Reliability questions; distance; a listener Two openings to earn instead of one
6 The Proleptic Flash The end, or a later crisis, glimpsed first One Hundred Years of Solitude Enormous pull-forward Can neutralise suspense; risks the borrowed hook
7 The Document Letter, report, transcript, dossier Dracula; The Color Purple; Pamela Authenticity; formal contract declared Voice must be plausible as the document type
8 The Arrival A stranger enters an established place The Trial; Pedro Páramo; The Magic Mountain Orientation through action, as the newcomer learns The oldest structure in the form; needs freshness elsewhere
9 The Rupture Routine broken on the first page Native Son; The Stranger “Why now” answered immediately Requires the routine to be sketched even as it breaks
10 The Catalogue An inventory of things, people, rules Père Goriot; Dune’s epigraphs Density of world; material argument Modern readers’ patience is short for it
11 The Metafictional Refusal The telling declines to begin properly Tristram Shandy; Don Quixote; Brás Cubas Formal contract declared at maximum volume Only works if the book sustains the joke
12 The Withheld Referent An unexplained noun or pronoun in subject position Beloved Immediate cognitive engagement One step from unreadable; see §8.6

How to use the taxonomy

Its purpose is not classification but substitution testing. A draft that is not working is often the right material in the wrong gambit. The productive exercise is to ask: what would this opening be if it were a Rupture instead of a Panorama? Rewriting the same first page in three different gambits reliably teaches more than three rounds of line-editing the original.

6.6 The “why now” question and the hairline crack

Whichever gambit is chosen, the reader tacitly assumes the book begins at a moment of significance. Openings that depict an entirely ordinary day violate that assumption silently, and the resulting flatness is often misdiagnosed as a prose problem.

The remedy is rarely a catastrophe. It is the hairline crack — the smallest possible deviation from routine that signals today is not like the others:

Exemplar The crack
The Stranger The telegram; the ambiguity of the date
Native Son The alarm; the rat
The Trial The breakfast that does not come
Alice in Wonderland A rabbit with a watch
Things Fall Apart Reputation stated as already made: the height from which the fall will be measured

Note that four of the five are small. The crack’s function is not to be dramatic but to be noticeable as a deviation, which requires that the routine be sketched clearly enough for the deviation to register. This is the deep reason a purely in medias res opening can fail: without a baseline, nothing can read as a departure from it.

6.7 Historical drift, and a caution against universalising the present

The conventions governing openings have moved substantially, and a student should be able to historicise the advice in this chapter rather than receive it as timeless.

Period Dominant expositional habit
18th century Frame, document, editorial apparatus; the fiction of found text (Robinson Crusoe, Pamela, Tom Jones)
19th century Preliminary exposition; panoramic or genealogical opening; the narrator as social analyst (Père Goriot, Middlemarch, Bleak House)
Modernist Plunge into consciousness; orientation withheld or distributed (Mrs Dalloway, The Sound and the Fury, Ulysses)
Mid-century to present Fast attack, early voice signature, exposition distributed; the “cold open” borrowed from screen conventions
Contemporary commercial The first-page hook treated as a submission requirement in advice to submitting writers (Lukeman 2000); exposition deferred

Two inferences follow, and they pull in different directions, which is the point.

  • The advice in §§4–8 is calibrated to contemporary conventions, and contemporary conventions are themselves shaped by the institutional pressures described in §3.6. That is a reason to know them, and a reason not to mistake them for laws of narrative.
  • The historical openings that violate them are not merely tolerated exceptions. Bleak House is not a great novel despite its opening; the opening is one of the reasons it is great. A pedagogy that can only say “this would be rejected today” has explained the market and not the book.

The properly critical position is therefore double: learn the current conventions well enough to break them deliberately, and read historically well enough to know that they are conventions.
