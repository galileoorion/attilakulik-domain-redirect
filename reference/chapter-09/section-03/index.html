Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

3. Why Beginnings Carry Disproportionate Weight

Continue in Core

The threshold problem is usually asserted in craft literature and rarely argued. It can be argued, and from several independent directions — cognitive, rhetorical, institutional. This section assembles the case, because a writer who understands why the opening is load-bearing revises it more intelligently than one who has merely been told that it is.

3.1 Primacy: early information disproportionately shapes later interpretation

The foundational finding is not literary but social-psychological. Solomon Asch’s 1946 experiments on impression formation demonstrated that when participants received identical lists of personality traits in different orders, the earlier traits governed the interpretation of the later ones. An “intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious” person was judged more favourably than an “envious, stubborn, critical, impulsive, industrious, intelligent” one — same words, reversed order, different person.

Menakhem Perry’s “Literary Dynamics” (1979) transposed this into literary theory as the primacy effect in reading: the reader constructs a hypothesis from initial material and then assimilates subsequent material to it, resisting revision until the counter-evidence becomes overwhelming. Perry’s analysis of Faulkner’s “A Rose for Emily” showed a text deliberately exploiting the effect — building a frame the ending demolishes.

For the writer, two operational consequences follow:

  • An accidental early impression is expensive to correct. If page 2 makes the protagonist read as passive, pages 30–80 spent demonstrating agency will be read as inconsistency rather than as correction.
  • The effect is a resource, not only a hazard. Every reversal, unreliable narrator and twist ending in the canon is built on primacy. The opening that plants a confident wrong frame is doing skilled work — provided the demolition is designed.

Two canonical exploitations of the effect. Austen opens Emma (1815): Emma Woodhouse, handsome, clever, and rich, with a comfortable home and happy disposition, seemed to unite some of the best blessings of existence. The list is flattering, the verdict admiring — and the single word “seemed”, buried mid-sentence where a hurried reader will glide over it, is the hairline crack through which the novel’s entire ironic correction will eventually pour. The primacy effect ensures most readers adopt the flattering frame; the word ensures that when the correction comes, it can be shown to have been signalled.

Pride and Prejudice (1813) performs the same manoeuvre at a higher volume: It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife. The sentence asserts a universal law that the reader is invited to disbelieve while accepting it as the operative law of this society — a double stance which is the novel’s mode. Note also what this opening does not do: it supplies no character, no want, no scene, no place, and no time. It would fail three of the five contracts on a mechanical audit. It survives because the narratorial voice is itself so distinctive that the voice contract is discharged with overwhelming surplus, and because a reader in 1813 recognised the marriage-plot genre from the sentence’s content alone. Contracts can be paid in unequal instalments; what cannot happen is a default across the board.

3.2 Rules of notice: beginnings are structurally privileged

Peter J. Rabinowitz’s Before Reading (1987) argues that readers operate with tacit rules of notice — conventions determining which parts of a text are treated as significant. Among the strongest are the rules of privileged position: titles, epigraphs, first and last sentences, and the openings and closes of chapters are read as load-bearing, and details placed there are automatically assigned interpretive weight.

This has a consequence that is easy to state and hard to internalise:

In the opening pages, there is no neutral detail. Everything mentioned is read as mentioned for a reason. A writer who names the weather on page one has, whether or not they intended to, told the reader that weather matters in this book.

The corollary explains a great deal of opening-page failure. Detail that would pass unnoticed on page 150 becomes, on page 1, a promise — and unredeemed promises accumulate as a sense that the book is unfocused. The discipline is not to write fewer details but to choose details one is willing to be held to.

The principle is the generalised form of Chekhov’s much-quoted rifle. Herbert’s Dune (1965) illustrates its positive use: the gom jabbar, the poisoned needle that the “old crone” of the first paragraph, the Reverend Mother Gaius Helen Mohiam, holds at Paul’s neck in the first chapter, is named there as an instrument of a test, and at the novel’s climax it is the weapon with which Alia kills Baron Harkonnen. The first chapter gave the needle its weight, so the climax did not need to explain it.

Conversely, the rule explains why naming is expensive in openings. A character introduced by name on page one is thereby promised to matter. Openings that introduce six named characters in two pages are not being generous with information; they are issuing six promissory notes, and readers who cannot tell which will be honoured stop tracking any of them.

It also clarifies the formal symmetry between openings and endings. Both occupy privileged positions; but the ending is read against everything preceding it, and the opening against nothing. Barbara Herrnstein Smith’s Poetic Closure (1968) and Frank Kermode’s The Sense of an Ending (1967) describe the ending’s work as the retrospective conferral of shape; the opening’s work is the prospective creation of the conditions under which shape can later be conferred.

3.3 Situation models: what the reader is actually building

Cognitive research on discourse comprehension holds that readers do not store sentences; they build a situation model — a mental representation of the state of affairs the text describes. Rolf Zwaan and Gabriel Radvansky’s influential synthesis (1998) proposes that readers track a small number of dimensions and register a processing cost whenever one shifts:

Dimension Reader’s implicit question Contract it serves (§4)
Protagonist Whose experience am I inside? Character
Space Where is this? World
Time When is this, and how fast is it moving? Formal / World
Causality What is making things happen? Stakes
Intentionality What does someone want? Character / Stakes

The correspondence with the contract model is close enough to state plainly: the five contracts are, in large part, a craft-side description of the dimensions a reader must populate to build a situation model at all. Catherine Emmott’s Narrative Comprehension (1997) makes a related argument with her notion of contextual frames — the reader’s running record of who is present in a scene and where — and shows that texts which fail to bind characters to frames early produce measurable disorientation.

This reframes “orientation” from a matter of politeness to a matter of processing. An opening that withholds all five dimensions is not being bracingly mysterious; it is asking the reader to hold unbound variables, which is cognitively expensive and quickly abandoned.

The practical inference: an opening may withhold some dimensions to create productive mystery, but it should supply enough that the withheld ones read as absences the reader can feel, not as noise. A named character in an unnamed place is intriguing. An unnamed pronoun in an unnamed place doing an unspecified thing is static.

3.4 Transportation and absorption: the state the opening must induce

Richard Gerrig’s Experiencing Narrative Worlds (1993) describes reading as being transported — a traveller carried some distance from the world of origin and returned somewhat changed. Melanie Green and Timothy Brock (2000) operationalised transportation as a measurable state combining attention, imagery and affect, and found it predicts belief change and enjoyment; later work (Kuijpers et al., 2014) refined this into a multi-component account of literary absorption.

The relevance is that transportation is a threshold phenomenon with a startup cost. It requires an initial investment of attention before it becomes self-sustaining, which is the investment the opening pages must solicit before they have anything to offer in exchange. John Gardner’s older, less empirical formulation in The Art of Fiction (1983) — fiction as a “vivid and continuous dream” that any awkwardness interrupts — captures the same phenomenon from the writer’s side, and adds the important detail that the dream is easiest to break before it has properly begun.

3.5 Horizon of expectations: the reader arrives pre-loaded

Hans Robert Jauss’s horizon of expectations (Toward an Aesthetic of Reception, 1982) holds that readers meet a text with a formed set of generic, formal and thematic expectations derived from prior reading. The opening pages are where the text negotiates with that horizon — confirming it, adjusting it, or deliberately violating it.

This is the theoretical foundation of the genre contract (§4.2). It also explains why the genre contract is discharged so cheaply when it is discharged well: the reader is doing most of the work, and a competent opening needs only to supply the cue that activates the right schema. One correctly chosen noun can orient a reader who has read forty books in the genre.

Two genre-specific refinements carry over:

  • Darko Suvin’s novum (Metamorphoses of Science Fiction, 1979) — the single validated novelty that distinguishes the science-fictional world from ours, and around which cognitive estrangement organises itself. Openings in speculative modes are typically where the novum is first felt, often before it is named.
  • Samuel R. Delany’s account of the science-fictional sentence (“About 5,750 Words”, 1977) — the argument that speculative genres alter the subjunctivity of ordinary sentences, so that “her world exploded” must be read literally until proven otherwise. Delany’s insight generalises: the opening pages teach the reader at what level to take the book’s sentences literally, and that instruction is itself a contract.

3.6 The institutional frame: how openings are actually encountered

A complete account must acknowledge that openings are read under material conditions, not in a vacuum.

  • Acquisition. Agents and commissioning editors read submissions under severe time pressure and in volume; the sample requested is conventionally the first three chapters, and, by agents’ own accounts (Lukeman’s among them), rejection decisions are frequently made in the first page or two. Noah Lukeman’s The First Five Pages (2000) is the canonical statement of this from inside the industry, and it should be read as a description of a filtering institution rather than an aesthetic principle.
  • Retail. Bookshop browsing and online “look inside” previews both sample the beginning. The opening pages are, in commercial terms, the product’s demonstration unit.
  • Consequence, and caution. These pressures are real, and they have shaped contemporary opening conventions — faster attacks, earlier voice signature, less front-loaded exposition than the nineteenth-century norm. But they are historically local. Openings that would fail a modern slush-pile screen include some of the most durable in the language. A student should be able to hold both facts at once: the market rewards certain opening behaviours, and the market is not the same thing as the art.

3.7 The zero-context problem, restated — and qualified

The chapter’s first principle can now be stated.

The zero-context problem. The opening pages must establish voice, genre, character, world and stakes using only their own resources, at a point when the reader has no accumulated investment to draw on, no established frame to assimilate new information to, and no evidence that further reading will be rewarded.

And immediately qualify it, because the qualification is where the more interesting teaching lies.

Readers do not, in fact, arrive at page one with zero context. They arrive with a title, a cover, jacket copy, a genre shelf, a price, a recommendation, an author’s reputation, possibly a prize sticker. Gérard Genette’s Paratexts (1997) is the systematic treatment of this apparatus — the “thresholds of interpretation” surrounding a text and conditioning its reception. A thriller cover does a substantial amount of the genre contract’s work before a word is read.

Why, then, teach a zero-context test at all?

Because the paratext is not the author’s to control, not permanent, and not always present. Covers change between editions and territories; jacket copy is written by marketing; the book is excerpted in a magazine, quoted in a review, assigned in a seminar as a photocopy, sampled as unlabelled text in a workshop. An opening that depends on its packaging to make sense has outsourced a structural obligation to a temporary artefact.

The test therefore survives as a deliberately austere idealisation — not a description of how books are read, but a stress test that isolates what the prose is doing on its own. Its correct use is diagnostic, not evaluative: an opening that fails it is not thereby bad, but it has a dependency the writer should have chosen consciously.
