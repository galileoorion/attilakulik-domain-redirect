Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Seven close readings and one revision case. The analytical apparatus is the same throughout: identify the gambit (§6.5), audit the contracts (§4), and assess the multiplicity (§5).

9.1 Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813) — the aphorism

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Gambit: Aphorism (2). Attack: before the action, at the level of general law.

Contract Status Carrier
Voice Fully discharged The ironic universal; the mock-legal it is a truth acknowledged; the balanced syntax
Genre Fully discharged Marriage plot, social comedy, English provincial
Character Deferred None present
World Partially A society in which fortune and marriage are systematically linked
Stakes Implied If the law is true, someone in this book will be married; if false, someone will be disappointed
Form Partially Third-person narrator with authority over generalisation

The lesson. The opening runs a two-page deficit on character and covers it with an enormous surplus in voice. It is a demonstration that the contracts are not a checklist to be ticked but a budget to be balanced — and that the narrator’s voice can itself be a character sufficient to carry a reader.

Observe also the mechanism of the irony: the sentence’s claim is false as stated (single men in possession of good fortunes generally do not want wives) and true as social description (the neighbourhood behaves as though it were true). The reader who catches this has been taught, in one sentence, that the novel’s narration must be read at two levels at once — the essential skill for everything that follows.

9.2 Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — the panorama with a thesis

London. Michaelmas Term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln’s Inn Hall. Implacable November weather.

Gambit: Panorama (4). Attack: the condition, prior to any event.

The passage that follows piles fog upon fog for several hundred words — up the river, down the river, in the eyes and throats of pensioners, and at last “at the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery.”

Contract Status Notes
Voice Overwhelming Verbless present-tense fragments; incantatory repetition; a narrator with the whole city in view
Genre Fully Social novel, satire, systemic critique
Character Absent by design The system is the protagonist of this chapter
World Fully Named institutions, seasons, streets, legal calendar
Stakes Thematic rather than personal Something is deeply wrong and enormous
Form Declared The present-tense panoramic narrator alternates with Esther’s retrospective first person — a formal contract Dickens declares by contrast at Chapter III

The lesson. Every prohibition in §8.1–8.2 is violated and the opening is magnificent. The distinguishing feature is that the description is an argument in the form of an image: fog is what Chancery does to people. A student attempting this gambit should be asked one question — what is your fog an argument about? — and, if there is no answer, redirected.

A complication. Bleak House is a double-narrator novel, and its formal contract is delayed: the reader meets the panoramic present-tense narrator first and Esther’s retrospective I only in Chapter III. By the argument of §4.6 this ought to fail. It does not, because the two narrations are so strongly differentiated that the switch reads as design rather than as slippage — the very criterion §4.6 sets out. The case shows the criterion doing real work rather than merely licensing convention.

9.3 Kafka, The Trial (1925) — maximum multiplicity

Someone must have been slandering Josef K., for one morning, without having done anything wrong, he was arrested.

Gambit: Rupture (9) fused with Arrival (8) — though here the institution arrives at the man.

Element Functions
Someone must have been slandering Voice (inference, not report); Stakes (an enigma); Character (K.’s need to explain)
Josef K. Character (named, but truncated); World (bureaucratic, documentary); Genre (the case file)
one morning Formal (ordinary time); World (this happens on ordinary mornings)
without having done anything wrong Stakes (injustice); Voice (whose assessment?); Genre (the moral universe is in question)
he was arrested Stakes; World (an apparatus exists); Genre (nightmare-logic realism)

Every contract, one sentence, and no strain — the sentence reads as plain reportage. This is the chapter’s clearest evidence for the claim in §5.1 that multiplicity is selection rather than compression.

On the translations. Muir’s classic version (“Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K.”) differs from Mitchell’s and from Parry’s in ways that matter for the reliability question: telling lies is more colloquial and less juridical than slandering, and slightly weakens the legal register that the German verleumdet carries. The structural analysis above survives all three; the tonal analysis does not. This is the translation caution from the front matter in practice.

9.4 Camus, The Stranger (1942) — a comparative reading in translation

French: Aujourd’hui, maman est morte. Ou peut-être hier, je ne sais pas.

Three published English versions:

Translator Rendering
Stuart Gilbert (1946) Mother died today. Or, maybe, yesterday; I can’t be sure.
Matthew Ward (1988) Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don’t know.
Sandra Smith (2012) My mother died today. Or maybe yesterday, I don’t know.

Why this is the best available teaching case on narrative distance. The three versions establish three different narrators from the same French.

  • Gilbert’s “Mother” is formal and slightly cold, and his added semicolon and commas impose a considered, literary syntax. This narrator sounds like a man in control of his prose — which nudges the reader toward reading Meursault as deliberately affectless.
  • Ward’s “Maman” retains the French word because no English equivalent carries both intimacy and childishness; his flat, unpunctuated I don’t know produces a narrator who is not performing anything. This narrator sounds like a man reporting.
  • Smith’s “My mother” splits the difference and adds a possessive that Gilbert’s version lacks, restoring a relationship the more clinical rendering suppresses.

The entire critical debate about whether Meursault is cold or merely literal is prefigured in this choice, and the anglophone reader’s answer is substantially determined by which paperback they bought.

Contract analysis (structural, translation-independent):

Contract Carrier
Voice Two short declaratives; no subordination; no emotional vocabulary; the uncertainty stated without distress
Genre Existential; the book will be about a man whose relation to his own experience is unlike ours
Character A want is absent, and the absence is characterising — the first of many
World Institutional: a telegram, an old people’s home, a bureaucracy that dates deaths
Stakes Small and enormous: what kind of person does not know?
Form First person, present-adjacent past, the notation of a man keeping a record

Note the deliberate contract failure. Test 3 (momentum) fails: nobody wants anything on the first page. The failure is the point, and it demonstrates the principle from §7.0 — a failed test is a question, and here the answer is that the missing want is the novel’s subject.

9.5 Morrison, Beloved (1987) — the withheld referent

124 was spiteful. Full of a baby’s venom. The women in the house knew it and so did the children.

Gambit: Withheld Referent (12).

What is supplied, immediately: an entity with a number for a name; a moral condition; a baby; women; children; collective knowledge; and the flat, communal certainty of knew it. What is withheld: what 124 is, who the baby is, why it is venomous, and where and when this is.

The discrimination the chapter has been building toward. Compare §8.6. Morrison withholds more than any tease, and produces curiosity rather than irritation. Three reasons:

  1. The withheld material is unspeakable within the represented consciousness — the novel is about what cannot be said, so the gap is thematic rather than tactical.
  2. The supplied material is dense and confident. A narrator who supplies six facts in three sentences does not read as evasive.
  3. The withholding is bounded. The reader knows which questions they have. Unbounded withholding — where the reader cannot tell what they do not know — produces noise (§3.3).

On the number. A house identified by its street number, in a novel about people who were owned and counted, is the most efficient thematic gesture in any opening discussed here. The address is doing what a name would do, and the substitution is the argument.

9.6 Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — voice as concealment

It seems increasingly likely that I really will undertake the expedition that has been preoccupying my imagination now for some days.

Gambit: Voice Declaration (3), in its most self-effacing possible form.

The sentence contains no event, no other person, no place, and no visible stake. By tests 2 and 3 it fails outright. It is nonetheless one of the most complete openings in postwar English fiction, because the diction is the plot in miniature.

Feature What it establishes
It seems increasingly likely A man who reports his own decisions as external probabilities
I really will Faint self-astonishment; a life in which small autonomies are remarkable
expedition Ludicrous inflation of a motoring holiday; a life without proportion because it has had no scope
preoccupying my imagination The imagination as an embarrassing organ, mentioned with care
now for some days A man who measures deliberation in days, and finds that quick

The lesson. In a novel whose subject is self-deception, the opening must be written so that the reader can see what the narrator cannot. This is Booth’s unreliability delivered without a single unreliable statement: every word is true, and the arrangement is a diagnosis. It is also the strongest available counterexample to the momentum test, and a useful corrective to any student who has concluded that openings must move.

9.7 Wright, Native Son (1940) — the rupture

Brrrrriiiiiinnng! An alarm clock clanged in the dark and silent room.

Gambit: Rupture (9), executed inside the forbidden waking-up frame (§8.1).

The onomatopoeia does something further: the book begins with a sound before a narrator, so that the reader is startled a fraction of a second before being told what startled them — the reader’s experience and the family’s are briefly identical. What follows within two pages: one room, four people, the enforced ritual of not looking at each other while dressing, a rat, a fight with the rat, and a mother’s contempt. Want, obstacle, world, class, race, and the novel’s governing image, all inside a frame the workshop prohibits.

The lesson. Restated from §8: the prohibition is on a mechanism, never on a surface feature. Waking fails when the waking is a runway. It succeeds when the waking is a rupture, or when the conditions of waking are the subject.

9.8 A revision case: from draft to opening

The following demonstrates the diagnostic protocol applied to an invented draft: the opening of the household novel, a contemporary literary novel about three siblings after their father’s death, which follows the prologue treated in Chapter 10.

Version A (draft)

Nadia woke early, as she always did, and lay for a while listening to the traffic on the main road. It was going to be another hot day. She had lived in the flat for over a year now, ever since she had moved out of the place above the shop, which had never had proper heating and which she had shared with her brother Dov for most of her twenties. Their father had died in March. Since then things had been difficult between them. She got up, made coffee, and thought about whether she should call him.

Diagnosis:

Test Result
1 — Contracts Character partially; world thinly; voice absent; genre absent; stakes stated not enacted
2 — Scene Cannot complete the sentence. She is not trying to do anything.
3 — Momentum Fails. First 60 words are waking and listening.
4 — Voice Fails. Interchangeable.
8 — Density Under-selected; every sentence carries one function
— Patterns present: waking-up (§8.1), throat-clearing (§8.7), and stated rather than embodied stakes

Note that Version A contains all the necessary information. Its failure is entirely one of arrangement and selection: the biography is delivered as biography, and the emotional situation is summarised (“things had been difficult”) rather than performed.

Version B (revision)

Nadia had got good at the maths of it: four months since March, seventeen since she’d left the flat over the shop for one with actual heat, and a week to the day since she’d stopped answering when Dov called to ask if she’d been by to check on the coat. She hadn’t. She told herself this was because grief had a schedule and hers ran on different maths from her brother’s, not because she couldn’t stand the smell of dust settling on wool that wasn’t going to be worn again. When her phone buzzed with his name a fourth time that morning, she did what she always did lately, which was let it ring itself out and go back to counting the register receipts nobody but her seemed to think still needed counting.

Contract audit:

Contract How it is established
Voice Precise, wry, self-auditing close third (“the maths of it”, “different maths from her brother’s”); the crucial move is she told herself, which signals that the narration can see past the character’s rationalisations without leaving her head
Genre Interiority-led, quiet register; literary family drama, not a plot-forward book — and the pace the rest of the novel is licensed to keep
Character An immediate, small, resisted want: do not answer, do not go, keep counting. Grief is revealed through avoidance rather than stated
World Contemporary, domestic, straitened: a flat with heat as an achievement, a shop, a till, an unworn coat
Stakes Four unanswered calls; an untended coat; receipts nobody else thinks matter — three planted questions, none coy
Form Close third, past tense, linear; one viewpoint so far, which the book must soon show is not its only one (see below)

What the revision did, procedurally:

  1. Deleted the throat-clearing — waking, traffic, weather (§8.7).
  2. Converted biography into inventory. The same facts (the old flat, the father’s death, the months since) now arrive as the character’s own counting, which makes exposition characterising. Of the five moves, this one transfers most readily to other openings, because every draft has biography that a character could be made to count.
  3. Located the hairline crack (§6.6): the fourth call this morning. “Why now” is answered without an event.
  4. Replaced a summary of feeling with an object. “Things had been difficult” becomes a coat, dust, wool, and a smell. Barthes’s reality effect (§4.4) doing structural work.
  5. Added the narratorial gap. She told herself… not because… — establishing that the book knows more than Nadia admits, which is the reliability protocol for the whole novel.

Applying §6.4: this is the hybrid resolution. The opening is in medias res on a small, comprehensible action — declining a phone call — while orientation happens in the same breath through voice and specific detail. The novel’s actual engine (whatever the siblings must finally do about the coat, the shop, and each other) arrives later, into a reader who can now feel it.

What Version B has not solved. It runs close to the density ceiling: the first sentence carries three time-measurements and four facts, and a reader may need to re-read it. Whether that is richness or strain is a legitimate editorial disagreement, and a useful one to stage in a workshop. Version B also fails test 4 in one respect: the wry-precise close third is a recognisable contemporary literary register, distinctive against Version A but not against its own genre’s shelf.

Two further gaps follow from the canon of the book. Four months since March never says what happened in March: Nadia knows, and the narration declines to name it, which by the test in §8.6 is defensible only because the gap is in the character, who counts rather than names, and because the prologue has just shown the father’s last ordinary day. Read without the prologue (test 5), the month is an unexplained proper noun. And the opening declares one viewpoint, while the book alternates between Nadia and Elena: by test 9 Elena’s first scene, the doorway, should follow at the first chapter break, before the reader’s attachment to Nadia hardens into an assumption of continuity.
