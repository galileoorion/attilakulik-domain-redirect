Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

4. The Contracts

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The contract model treats the opening pages as opening a set of obligations to the reader, each of which the rest of the book must honour. The metaphor is borrowed from the older critical language of the “narrative contract” or “generic contract” and from the reader-response tradition’s interest in the expectations a text licenses.

Its value is diagnostic. It converts the vague instruction make your opening better into a set of separable questions with separable remedies. Its danger is mechanical application, which produces the overstuffed first page (§8.5): an opening visibly discharging obligations rather than telling a story.

Five contracts are set out below, followed in §4.6 by a proposed sixth. For each: what it is, why it matters, how it is realised at sentence level, what under- and over-delivery look like, and where it connects to theory.

4.1 The Voice Contract

What the reader learns: what it will be like, sentence by sentence, to be inside this book.

Components

Component The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
Narrative distance How close am I to this mind? Free indirect style, pronoun and tense choice, whether judgements are the character’s or the narrator’s
Diction and rhythm What texture will I be living inside? Sentence length variation, register, Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon vocabulary, punctuation habits
Reliability Can I take this at face value? Overprecision, defensiveness, gaps, self-justification, inconsistency between report and implication

Discussion

Narrative distance is the most technically consequential and the least often taught explicitly. Gérard Genette’s Narrative Discourse (1980) separates who speaks (voice) from who perceives (focalisation); Dorrit Cohn’s Transparent Minds (1978) maps the modes of rendering consciousness; James Wood’s How Fiction Works (2008) offers the most accessible modern account of free indirect style, in which the narrator’s language is contaminated by the character’s. The opening pages set the default position on this scale, and readers detect violations of it long before they can name them.

A practical demonstration — the same event at four distances:

Distance Rendering
Far The woman crossed the yard.
Middle She crossed the yard, keeping to the gravel.
Close (free indirect) She kept to the gravel. The grass would be soaked, and she had one pair of dry shoes left.
Interior monologue Gravel. Not the grass, not today, not with one dry pair left.

Each is competent. But the opening pages choose, and the choice is binding: a book that opens at the far position and drifts to interior monologue by chapter three has not deepened, it has wobbled — unless the drift is patterned, in which case it is a technique.

Reliability is Wayne Booth’s territory (The Rhetoric of Fiction, 1961/1983). What the opening must signal is not whether the narrator is lying but the reading protocol: face value, or with suspicion. The signal is usually structural rather than declared — a narrator who protests too much, or knows too much too neatly, or explains what nobody asked.

Illustrations

Four openings, four positions on the reliability scale, each established in a single sentence.

1. Melville, Moby-Dick (1851)

Call me Ishmael.

Three words that have been over-praised as a hook and under-analysed as a contract. Note what the imperative actually does. It is not My name is Ishmael but an instruction about what to call him — a name offered for convenience, its relation to the truth unspecified, and borrowed from a biblical outcast. The reader is placed in conversation with a narrator who is managing his own presentation. The sentence also fixes distance (first person, retrospective, oral), diction (plain, monosyllabic, Anglo-Saxon) and reliability (provisional) in a single stroke, and the enormous encyclopaedic sprawl that follows is licensed by this: a narrator who has claimed the right to tell it his way.

2. Ford Madox Ford, The Good Soldier (1915)

This is the saddest story I have ever heard.

The reliability signal is grammatical. Heard concedes that the narrator was not a witness to his own life’s central events — an astonishing admission from a man who was present throughout, and the first symptom of the wilful obtuseness the novel anatomises. Dowell’s superlative is also unearned at the moment of utterance: the reader has been given no story yet, only a rating of one. An opening that begins by telling you how to feel before giving you anything to feel it about is an opening about a man who cannot see what happened to him.

3. Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884)

You don’t know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain’t no matter.

Vernacular grammar establishes class, region, education and age; the direct address to you establishes an oral register; the offhand dismissal of the prerequisite establishes a narrator with his own priorities. The sentence immediately following (“There was things which he stretched, but mainly he told the truth”) makes truth-telling an explicit topic on page one, and thereby hands the reader the interpretive instrument the whole novel requires.

4. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989)

It seems increasingly likely that I really will undertake the expedition that has been preoccupying my imagination now for some days.

Here reliability is signalled by syntax alone. A man about to take a short motoring holiday describes it as an “expedition” that has been “preoccupying” his “imagination”; he approaches the fact obliquely (“it seems increasingly likely”), as though his own intention were an external weather system he is observing. Nothing is stated about repression. Everything is enacted by the sentence’s refusal to say I have decided to go. This is the voice contract doing work no summary of character could do — and it is the reason the novel’s late revelations land as confirmations rather than surprises.

A note on distance across the four. Melville and Twain are close and oral; Ford is close but retrospectively distant from himself; Stevens in Ishiguro is close in access and infinitely distant in self-knowledge. Narrative distance, properly understood, is not one dial but two: distance from the character’s perception, and the character’s distance from their own truth.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: interchangeable prose. Grammatically clean, rhythmically neutral, attributable to any of ten thousand books. This is the failure the voice test (§7, test 4) is designed to catch.
  • Over: voice as performance. Style so insistent that it obstructs the situation — extravagant metaphor before the reader knows who is speaking or why. Voice is best established while doing something else; the character contract and the voice contract are the natural pair.

4.2 The Genre Contract

What the reader learns: what kind of book this is, and therefore what to expect, hope for and fear.

Components

Component The reader’s implicit question Typical carrier
Scale Is this about a marriage or an empire? The size of the problem the opening treats as important
Tone Comic, grim, wondrous, plain? Diction, irony, the narrator’s stance toward suffering
The fantastic, or its absence Do the world’s rules match mine? An unexplained noun; a character’s unremarked assumption
Mode of attention What am I meant to watch for? Whether the prose lingers on clues, on feeling, on landscape, on ideas

Discussion

The genre contract is the most efficiently discharged of the five, because — per Jauss (§3.5) — the reader supplies the schema. The cost of getting it wrong is correspondingly high, and asymmetric:

  • Promising more than the book delivers is the graver error. A reader promised a thriller who receives a domestic novel feels defrauded, however good the domestic novel is.
  • Promising less is recoverable but wasteful: readers who would have loved the book put it down before it declares itself.

Signalling the fantastic deserves separate treatment because it is where speculative fiction most often falters in draft. The mechanism that works is normalisation: the fantastic element is delivered inside a sentence about something else, treated by the viewpoint character as unremarkable, and thereby established as a fact of the world rather than an announcement to the reader. The four illustrations below are all instances of it.

Scale is more often botched than tone. An opening that treats a small problem with enormous rhetorical weight promises an epic and delivers a domestic drama; an opening that treats catastrophe with a shrug promises a comedy of nihilism. The rule is not that stakes must be large but that rhetorical weight must be proportionate to the weight the book will actually maintain.

Illustrations: four ways to declare a world’s rules

1. Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) — the novum inside the cliché

It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen.

Few genre signals in English fiction are as efficient, and the efficiency depends on the cliché the sentence inhabits. The first clause is the weather report Elmore Leonard famously prohibited (§8.2) — deliberately, banally ordinary. The second clause detonates inside it. Thirteen is not explained, not marvelled at, and not grammatically emphasised; it is delivered in the same flat tone as the temperature. The reader learns in fourteen words that the world is not theirs, that its strangeness is administrative rather than magical, and that the narration will not flag such strangeness in future. Orwell has also taught a reading protocol: check the details, because they are load-bearing.

2. Atwood, The Handmaid’s Tale (1985) — the past perfect as worldbuilding

We slept in what had once been the gymnasium.

Nine words containing an entire political history. The tense — had once been — establishes a before, and therefore a rupture, and therefore a regime that repurposes institutions. The pronoun we establishes collectivity and, implicitly, confinement. That the building is a gymnasium — a school, a civic space, somewhere for children — makes the repurposing obscene without a syllable of commentary. Compare the version that explains: After the coup, the new government converted schools into detention centres for fertile women. Same information; no world.

3. Gibson, Neuromancer (1984) — genre by simile

The sky above the port was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel.

The genre contract is discharged not by content but by the terms of comparison. Nature is described in terms of a consumer appliance, and a malfunctioning one; the reader is told that in this book the technological is the primary reality against which the natural is measured. The sentence also demonstrates a hazard: the image has aged, because a dead analogue channel was grey static and a dead digital channel is flat blue. The referent shifted under the sentence. Genre signals anchored to contemporary technology have a shelf life; those anchored to social relations do not.

4. Rowling, Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone (1997) — the fantastic signalled by its denial

Mr and Mrs Dursley, of number four, Privet Drive, were proud to say that they were perfectly normal, thank you very much.

The fantastic is promised by the vehemence of the disavowal. “Thank you very much” imports a spoken defensiveness into the narration — free indirect style used comically — and a reader who has read any fiction at all knows that a novel opening with an insistence on normality is a novel about to be invaded by the abnormal. The sentence also fixes tone (arch, comic, aligned against the Dursleys), scale (domestic, suburban, English) and the moral geography of the series, before any magic appears.

The common mechanism. In all four, the world’s rules arrive as subordinate information inside a sentence about something else — the weather, the sleeping arrangements, the sky’s colour, the neighbours’ self-regard. This is the technique the chapter calls normalisation, and of the techniques in this section it transfers to the most speculative openings, because it needs nothing but a sentence about something else.

Contrast the announcement mode: In this world, magic was real, and every household maintained protective wards. It discharges the genre contract, damages the voice contract, and forfeits the character and world contracts entirely — the reader is being addressed by an author, not accompanied by a person.

4.3 The Character Contract

What the reader learns: whose eyes they have borrowed, and why they should care what those eyes see next.

Components

Component The reader’s implicit question Note
Whose story Whom am I following? Ambiguity here is the most costly single defect in an opening
A want What does this person want right now? The immediate want, not the story-goal
A distinct interiority How does this person see? Selection of detail is characterisation

Discussion

The immediate want is the engine, and it is routinely confused with the story-goal. The story-goal — dismantle the conspiracy, survive the winter, win her back — usually cannot be present on page one, because the reader does not yet have the context to feel its weight. What page one needs is far smaller: get out of this conversation; finish counting the till; avoid the phone; leave the coat on its hook. Dwight Swain’s Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965) formalised the scene as goal–conflict–disaster, and the goal in that formula can be trivially small so long as it is legible and resisted.

The test is mechanical: can you complete the sentence “In this scene, X is trying to , and is in the way”? If not, the character contract is open regardless of how vivid the prose is.

Interiority as selection is the subtler half. A character reveals themselves less by what they feel than by what they notice and how they name it. Two characters in the same kitchen: one registers the smell of scorched milk and the unpaid bill under the fruit bowl; the other registers the light on the tiles. This is characterisation performed by the world contract’s own materials — the clearest everyday instance of multiplicity (§5).

Illustrations: the immediate want

1. Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925)

Mrs Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.

The most instructive opening in the language on this point, because the want is trivial and the sentence is nonetheless doing five things. The want — buy the flowers — is small, concrete, immediate and already in motion. “Herself” implies servants, and therefore class, and therefore a world. “Mrs Dalloway” rather than Clarissa places the reader at the social surface, from which the novel will spend the day descending. The reporting construction — said she would — means the sentence is not narration but quotation of a decision already taken, so the reader arrives a beat late, catching up, which is the temporal relation the whole book maintains with its heroine’s interior life. And a woman insisting on doing a small thing herself is, in miniature, the novel’s entire subject.

2. Carroll, Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

Alice was beginning to get very tired of sitting by her sister on the bank, and of having nothing to do.

Boredom is a want. It is the want for something to happen, and it is one of the most efficient openings available in children’s and fantastic fiction because it aligns the protagonist’s desire with the reader’s. Alice wants an adventure; so does the reader; the White Rabbit satisfies both at once.

3. Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment (1866)

On an exceptionally hot evening early in July a young man came out of the garret in which he lodged in S— Place and walked slowly, as though in hesitation, towards K— bridge. (trans. Garnett)

The want is not stated; it is inferred from hesitation. A man walking slowly as though undecided, towards a specific place, on a specific errand he will not name, generates the pull-forward question without a word of interiority. Note also the anonymising initials — a documentary convention, borrowed from the crime report and the case history, that establishes the novel’s clinical relation to its own atrocity.

Sympathy is not the requirement

Craft lore often demands a “likeable” protagonist by page one. The better formulation is that the reader needs investment, of which likeability is only one route. Competence, wit, acute perception, evident danger, an interesting error in progress, and sheer specificity of attention all generate it. Nabokov’s Lolita (1955) opens in the voice of a man the reader should despise and secures attention through sheer verbal seduction — which is not incidental to the book’s ethics but identical with them: the reader is made to experience being charmed by Humbert before being asked to judge him. The opening pages recruit; they need not ingratiate.

4.4 The World Contract

What the reader learns: that there is a real place here, with rules, and that the book knows them.

Components

Component The reader’s implicit question Note
Concrete specificity Is this a place or a stage set? Precision matters more than quantity
Implicit rules What is normal here? Conveyed through unremarked assumption, not explanation
Social texture Who has power here, and how does it show? Often the most underused resource in opening pages

Discussion

Ian Watt’s The Rise of the Novel (1957) identified circumstantial particularity — named places, exact times, specific objects — as constitutive of the novel’s formal realism. Roland Barthes’s “The Reality Effect” (1968) pressed further: certain details signify nothing except reality itself, and their apparent uselessness is what authenticates the world. This is why the specific detail outperforms the abundant one. Three exact things beat a paragraph of general scenery, because exactness is the signal.

Implicit rules is the contract’s harder half, and it is where the info-dump opening (§8.3) originates. The mechanism that works is the same normalisation described in §4.2: the world’s rules are revealed by what the viewpoint character fails to find remarkable. A character who steps around the offering bowl at the threshold without comment has established a religion, a custom and a household in eleven words. A narrator who explains that in this culture offering bowls are placed at thresholds has delivered an info-dump.

Two principles follow:

  1. Rules are shown by their operation, not their statement. The reader infers systems from instances and enjoys doing it.
  2. The character’s indifference is the instrument. What the viewpoint character does not bother to explain marks the boundary of the normal, and that boundary is the world.

Social texture — who defers to whom, who is allowed to be late, whose name gets used in full — is the most efficient available shortcut because it discharges world, character and stakes simultaneously. It is underused because it is not decorative and therefore does not feel like description.

Illustrations: three modes of world-establishment

1. Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — the world as a moral atmosphere

London. Michaelmas Term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln’s Inn Hall. Implacable November weather.

Verbless fragments; a place-name alone; a legal term of art; weather given a moral adjective. This opening violates almost every rule taught in contemporary workshops — no character, no dialogue, no want, and then several hundred words of fog. It is also one of the most successful openings ever written, and the reason is that the fog is the argument. Chancery obscures; the fog obscures; the novel is about a legal system in which nothing can be seen clearly, and the reader is placed inside the condition before being introduced to anyone suffering from it. The lesson is not description can work but rather: description works when the described thing is the book’s thesis rather than its backdrop.

2. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958) — the world as a system of value

Okonkwo was well known throughout the nine villages and even beyond.

The opening does not describe Umuofia; it describes what Umuofia esteems, and thereby establishes the social system more economically than any physical description could. The reader learns that reputation travels, that villages are counted in nines, that fame is measurable and public. The register is that of a communal teller addressing people who already know the world — an orientation strategy that also enacts the novel’s politics, since a colonial framing would have had to explain what the community takes for granted.

3. Balzac, Père Goriot (1835) — the world as material determinism

Balzac’s long inventory of the Maison Vauquer — its smells, its stained furniture, its precise rent — is the limit case of Watt’s circumstantial particularity, and it is unfashionable in a way that can be defended. Balzac’s boarding house is not scene-setting; it is causal. The novel argues that the lodgers are what the rent makes them, so the rent must be specified before the people can be understood. Where a contemporary editor sees delay, the nineteenth-century realist sees the premise being laid.

The synthesis. These three, plus Atwood’s gymnasium in §4.2, exhaust the practical repertoire: a world can be established as atmosphere (what it feels like), as value system (what it esteems), as material condition (what it costs), or as rupture (what it used to be). Most strong openings choose one and imply the others.

4.5 The Stakes Contract

What the reader learns: why the book began here, and what question will pull them forward.

Components

Component The reader’s implicit question Note
Why this moment Why now, and not a year ago? The attack; often signalled by a break in routine
The pull-forward question What do I want to know? The engine of continued reading
Cost What can be lost? Danger is not the only currency: dignity, hope, and a self-image all qualify

Discussion

“Why now” is the question a surprising number of drafts cannot answer. The reader tacitly assumes the book begins at a moment of significance; if the opening depicts an ordinary day indistinguishable from the ten thousand before it, that assumption is quietly violated. The remedy is rarely to add an explosion. It is to locate the hairline crack — the small deviation from routine that signals the day is not like the others. A phone call not answered for the fourth time; a letter that arrives on the wrong day; a habit performed once too carefully.

The pull-forward question is Barthes’s hermeneutic code (S/Z, 1970) in operational dress: the mechanism by which a narrative poses an enigma and delays its resolution, with the delay itself structured by snares, partial answers and equivocations. The opening’s job is to place at least one enigma the reader wants resolved.

The distinction that matters in revision is between withholding and planting:

  • Planting creates a question the reader knows they have. Why won’t she answer her brother’s calls?
  • Withholding conceals information the reader does not know is missing, or knows is being deliberately kept. Coyness — the narrator obviously declining to name a thing the viewpoint character knows perfectly well — is the most reliably irritating move available in an opening, because it breaks the voice contract to serve the stakes contract.

Cost is often left implicit, and revision should make it explicit. A want with no discernible cost of failure is a preference, not a stake. The cost need not be physical or even large; it needs only to be real to this character.

Illustrations: three shapes of the pull-forward question

1. Morrison, Beloved (1987) — the enigma as syntax

124 was spiteful. Full of a baby’s venom.

Most of the eight words are ordinary; the arrangement is not. A number occupies the subject position where a name belongs, and is given a moral quality. The reader must infer that 124 is a house, that houses can be spiteful here, and that a baby is involved in a way not yet stated. Morrison famously described wanting the reader to be “snatched” into the book as her characters were snatched into slavery — the disorientation is not decorative but structurally mimetic. Note how much is supplied: an entity, a condition, an agent, an emotional charge. The withheld information is bounded, which is what separates this from mere obscurity (§8.6).

2. Kafka, The Trial (1925) — the enigma as premise

Someone must have been slandering Josef K., for one morning, without having done anything wrong, he was arrested.

The sentence installs a question that the novel will refuse to answer, and announces that refusal in its own grammar. “Must have been” is the narrator inferring — adopting Josef K.’s hypothesis rather than reporting a fact — so that the one apparently solid causal claim in the sentence is already contaminated by the protagonist’s need for an explanation. “Without having done anything wrong” is likewise K.’s assessment, not the narrator’s guarantee. The reader is thus handed both the enigma and a warning that the guide is unreliable about it.

3. Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925) — the enigma as retrospective promise

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

The stakes here are declared formally rather than dramatically. Nothing is at risk in the sentence; but the retrospective frame (“ever since”) promises that something happened which reorganised a life, and the reader continues in order to find out what. This is a distinct and underused mechanism: the promise of significance, made by a narrator’s posture rather than by a situation. Its cost is that the promise must be honoured — a retrospective narrator who turns out to have witnessed nothing worth the retrospection is the borrowed hook (§8.4) in slow motion.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Formal Contract

The five-contract model is inherited from a broadly realist account of fiction, in which the reader’s questions are about the represented world. But readers of opening pages are also learning something about the artefact: how the book is built, and what reading procedure it requires.

The formal contract. The opening pages teach the reader the book’s structural conventions — its person and tense, its handling of time, its unit of division, its use of typographic and paratextual devices, and whether the narration is single or plural.

Components

Component What the reader learns Cost of a late change
Person and tense First or third; past or present Very high; feels like error
Temporal architecture Linear, retrospective, braided, fragmented High; a first analepsis on page 90 in a strictly linear book reads as a lapse
Unit of division Chapters, sections, white-space breaks, documents Moderate
Multiplicity of narration Single viewpoint or several Very high — see below
Apparatus Footnotes, epigraphs, letters, transcripts, images High; a device introduced late reads as a gimmick

Why it matters

The severest case is multi-viewpoint structure. A book that spends its first forty pages in one consciousness and then switches has, in effect, mis-stated its terms: readers form attachment on the assumption of continuity, and the second viewpoint is met with resistance proportional to the strength of the first attachment. Books using several viewpoints typically solve this by declaring the pattern early — a viewpoint change at the first chapter break, or a titling convention that announces the structure before it operates.

The same logic governs apparatus. Footnotes, interpolated documents and epistolary inserts are all far cheaper to introduce in the first pages than in the second act, because early introduction reads as the book’s nature and late introduction reads as the author’s intervention.

Illustrations: the formal contract declared and broken

1. García Márquez, One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967) — the whole temporal architecture in one sentence

Many years later, as he faced the firing squad, Colonel Aureliano Buendía was to remember that distant afternoon when his father took him to discover ice. (trans. Gregory Rabassa)

Three time-planes in twenty-six words: a future execution, a present of remembering, and a remembered childhood afternoon. The sentence does not merely contain prolepsis and analepsis; it teaches the reader the book’s temporal grammar, so that the subsequent hundred years of loops, repetitions and prophecies arrive as fulfilment of a rule already stated. Note too the collision of registers — a firing squad and the discovery of ice — which declares the magical-realist proposition that the marvellous and the atrocious occupy the same plane of reality.

2. Machado de Assis, The Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas (1881) — apparatus as premise

The novel opens with a dedication (here paraphrased, since translations differ) to the worm that gnawed the author’s corpse, and a narrator who announces he is writing from beyond the grave. The formal contract — deceased narrator, digressive chapters, direct address, a chapter consisting of ellipses — is declared before the story begins, which is why the later formal outrages read as consistent rather than arbitrary.

3. Sterne, Tristram Shandy (1759–67) — the opening that satirises the attack

Tristram begins at his own conception and is not born for several volumes. The joke is a genuine argument about openings: any beginning is arbitrary, since every event has antecedents, and the convention of “starting somewhere” conceals a choice. Students who find §6 too confident about where to begin should read Sterne as the counter-case.

4. Flaubert, Madame Bovary (1857) — the contract quietly abandoned

The novel opens in the first person plural: We were in class when the headmaster came in (this chapter’s rendering). A schoolboy narrator, one of Charles Bovary’s classmates, observes the new boy — and then vanishes, never to return, as the book slides into the impersonal free indirect narration for which it is celebrated. It remains an unresolved formal crux, and it is arguable whether it is a flaw, a residue of an abandoned draft, or a deliberate gesture that establishes Charles as someone seen from outside before the novel moves inside Emma. It is set as seminar question 3 (§12.2) because reasonable critics disagree.

Deliberate violation

The formal contract can be broken to great effect — a book that is about the instability of its own telling will break it deliberately, and there is a whole modernist and postmodern tradition of doing so. Saramago’s Blindness (1995) declares its refusal of quotation marks and paragraph breaks on its first page, so that the typographic strangeness reads as the book’s condition rather than as an error; Faulkner’s The Sound and the Fury (1929) opens in a consciousness that cannot mark time, and the reader’s disorientation is the represented experience.

The distinction the chapter maintains throughout applies with special force here: the violation must be legible as a violation. A reader who thinks the book has slipped is having a different experience from one who knows the book has turned. Flaubert’s vanishing we is instructive because it sits on the boundary — which is why it is still argued about.

Should there be six contracts?

Reasonable disagreement is possible, and students should be invited into it. Two positions:

  • Five is enough. The formal contract is really a component of voice (person, tense, distance) and genre (apparatus conventions), and adding it dilutes a model whose virtue is compactness.
  • Six is more accurate. Formal features are not perceived as voice or genre by readers, they have distinct failure modes and distinct remedies, and multi-viewpoint mis-declaration is a common structural failure the five-contract model cannot name.

This chapter adopts the sixth for diagnostic purposes while noting that the original five capture the majority of revision problems. Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class.

4.8 Must all contracts be discharged at once?

No — and the model is misused when read that way. Three qualifications:

  1. Opening is not closing. A contract may be opened on page one with a single cue and honoured over the first chapter. The stakes contract in particular is usually opened by a small question and honoured by the arrival of the real problem some pages later.
  2. Deferral is legitimate when it is a choice. Some novels deliberately withhold the character contract for pages (the panoramic opening, §6.6), or the world contract almost entirely (the chamber opening). The diagnostic question in §7 is not is anything absent? but is the absence deliberate, and is the reader given a reason to tolerate it?
  3. Proportion varies by mode. A literary novel may weight voice and character heavily and defer stakes; a novel that opens on its central danger reverses the weighting. The contracts are constant; the allocation is a genre-inflected choice.
