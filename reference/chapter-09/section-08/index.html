Chapter 9 · The Opening Pages

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern below is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what actually goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and — importantly — its exception: a published opening that commits the “error” and succeeds.

The exceptions are not decorative. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults and not writers who can make choices. In every case, the pattern fails because of a mechanism, not because of a surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless.

8.1 The Waking-Up Opening

Symptom. The novel begins with a character waking, rising, washing, dressing, considering the day.

Mechanism. Waking is a transition into consciousness rather than an action within it. The character has no want yet (they have not been awake long enough to form one), no obstacle, and no relation to anyone. All five contracts are therefore deferred behind a sequence of physical events that are identical for every human being on earth. The problem is not that the character wakes; it is that nothing can be at stake in a body becoming conscious.

Persistence. It is the chronological start of the day, and drafts gravitate to chronological starts. It also gives the writer a comfortable runway — which is the throat-clearing described in §8.7.

Remedy. Begin at the first moment the character wants something. This is frequently three or four paragraphs later; the deleted material is usually the writer’s warm-up rather than the reader’s requirement.

The exception. Wright’s Native Son (1940) opens:

Brrrrriiiiiinnng! An alarm clock clanged in the dark and silent room.

This is a waking-up opening that succeeds absolutely, and the reason is instructive. The alarm is not a transition; it is an intrusion, and what follows immediately is not washing and dressing but a one-room apartment in which a family of four must not look at each other while dressing, and then a rat. Within a page the reader has a want (kill the rat), an obstacle, a world (poverty, Chicago, the colour line) and a metaphor the whole novel will pursue. Wright uses the waking frame to make the conditions of waking the subject.

Kafka’s The Metamorphosis (1915) does the same by a different route: the waking is the catastrophe. Where waking is the disruption, the pattern inverts and becomes one of the strongest available openings.

8.2 The Weather Report

Symptom. The novel opens with atmospheric or landscape description; no person, no want, no voice yet in evidence.

Mechanism. Weather is the default subject when a writer wants to begin but has not decided what the book is about. Elmore Leonard’s often-quoted prohibition (“Never open a book with weather”) is a rule of thumb against decorative scene-setting: description that establishes mood without establishing anything else, thereby serving one contract thinly and none of the others.

Persistence. Description is the easiest prose to write and feels like craft. It also postpones the harder decision of whose consciousness the book inhabits.

Remedy. Two options, and the second is usually better. Either give the weather to someone — filter it through a viewpoint character whose relation to it is characterising (a farmer, a mourner, and an arsonist notice different rain) — or make the weather thematic, in which case it stops being weather.

The exceptions are so distinguished that the rule needs restating. Bleak House opens with several hundred words of fog, and Nineteen Eighty-Four opens with a bright cold day in April. Both work, for the same reason: the weather is not the setting but the proposition. Dickens’s fog is the epistemological condition of Chancery; Orwell’s clock is the world’s rules declared inside a banality. Compare a draft in which grey clouds gather over the moors because the writer has not yet decided who is standing under them, and the difference is not one of skill but of function.

The generalised rule. Open with weather when the weather is an argument. Do not open with weather as an establishing shot, because prose has no establishing shots — every clause is paid for.

8.3 The Info-Dump Opening

Symptom. Worldbuilding, history or backstory delivered before any character or action: the political situation, the magic system, the family’s four generations.

Mechanism. Two distinct failures, usually simultaneous. First, per Sternberg (§6.2), delivering exposition early spends an asset that would otherwise generate curiosity. Second, and more fundamental: information is not memorable without a container. A reader given the political history of a nation before meeting anyone who lives in it has nowhere to store it, and will retain approximately none of it — which means the info-dump does not even achieve its own stated purpose.

Persistence. The writer knows the world intimately and fears the reader will be lost without it. This fear is real; the remedy is misdirected. The reader does not need to know the world early; they need to believe the author knows it, which is achieved by confident specificity rather than by explanation.

Remedy. Convert exposition into implication (§4.4). Ask of each expository sentence: which character would say this, to whom, and why? If no one would, the reader does not need it yet.

The exception. Dune (1965) opens with an epigraph from a fictional history and proceeds to a scene thick with unexplained terminology — Bene Gesserit, gom jabbar, Arrakis, the spice — almost none of it defined. It succeeds because the vocabulary is delivered inside a tense scene between two people, and because unexplained terms function as withheld information rather than delivered information: they generate curiosity in Sternberg’s exact sense. This is the crucial discrimination for speculative writers: an unexplained proper noun is an asset; an explained one is an expense.

8.4 The Borrowed Hook

Symptom. A gripping opening — a chase, a murder, a battle, a scream — that turns out to be a dream, an unconnected flash-forward, a scene from a genre the book does not belong to, or an event the following three hundred pages never resume.

Mechanism. The opening writes a cheque the book does not honour. Because of primacy (§3.1) the reader forms their model of the novel from these pages, and every subsequent page is read as a deviation from the promised book. Note the asymmetry: readers forgive a book that becomes more than it promised, and do not forgive one that becomes less.

Persistence. Submission pressure (§3.6). Writers are told to hook; the fastest hook available is an event more exciting than the book’s actual material; the mismatch is invisible to the writer, who knows what is coming.

Remedy. Test 6. If the opening and pages 30–40 read as different books, change the opening.

The near-exception, and why it is not one. One Hundred Years of Solitude opens with a firing squad that does not occur for hundreds of pages. This is not a borrowed hook, because the proleptic flash is the book’s own material, delivered in the book’s own voice, and it establishes the temporal architecture the novel actually uses (§4.6). The distinction is exact: a flash-forward that belongs to the book is a structure; one that belongs to a more exciting book is a fraud.

The dream-opening deserves its own sentence. A dream sequence presented as reality asks the reader to invest and then informs them the investment was void — the one manoeuvre guaranteed to teach a reader that this narrator’s assertions need not be believed, at the exact moment the narrator most needs to be believed.

8.5 The Overstuffed First Page

Symptom. Every sentence is doing four jobs; the prose is relentlessly significant; no clause is idle.

Mechanism. Multiplicity past the density ceiling (§5.4). Emphasis is relational — a detail lands as important because surrounding details are not — so a page on which everything is important contains nothing important. The reader experiences this as strain rather than as richness.

Persistence. It is the failure mode of good advice, and it is generated by revision rather than by drafting. A writer told that opening pages must do five things at once will attempt to do five things in every sentence. In this chapter’s estimate, from drafts rather than a count, it is commoner in heavily revised openings, which is why it often afflicts conscientious students.

Remedy. Restore rests. Identify the two or three load-bearing details and let the rest of the page be ordinary around them. Read aloud (test 8).

The exception. There is no exception, but there is a legitimate neighbour: the deliberately overwhelming opening — Faulkner’s Benjy section, the first page of Gravity’s Rainbow — where saturation is the represented experience. The difference is that saturation there is thematic and sustained, not a symptom of anxiety on page one that relaxes by page ten.

8.6 The Withholding Tease

Symptom. The narration conspicuously declines to name something the viewpoint character knows perfectly well: the thing she had done that summer; what was in the box; a protagonist referred to only as he for six pages.

Mechanism. A breach of the voice contract in the service of the stakes contract. Close narration promises access to a consciousness; a consciousness that circles a fact without naming it is either being coy on the author’s behalf — which reveals the author’s hand — or is repressing, which is a different and much harder effect requiring far more care. Readers detect the difference immediately, and the emotional register of the response is irritation rather than curiosity.

Persistence. It looks like suspense, and it is easy. Genuine curiosity requires constructing a question the reader wants answered; withholding merely notes that an answer exists.

Remedy. Distinguish planting from withholding (§4.5). If the reader knows a question exists and wants the answer, that is a plant. If the reader knows the narrator is refusing to tell them, that is a tease. Give the reader the fact and withhold its meaning — nearly always the stronger structure.

The exception. Beloved withholds massively and succeeds. Why? Because Morrison’s withheld material is unspeakable within the represented consciousness — the repression is the subject, thematically motivated and consistently maintained — and because what is supplied in place of the missing information is not evasion but concentrated, disorienting specificity. The test to apply: is the gap in the character, or in the author?

8.7 Throat-Clearing

Symptom. The actual novel begins on page four. The first three pages are context, scene-setting, biography, or the character travelling somewhere.

Mechanism. Composition process leaking into the finished text. Writers need a run-up to find the voice; the run-up is drafting apparatus, not narrative.

Persistence. The material is often perfectly well written, which makes it hard to see, and it feels necessary to the writer because for the writer it was necessary.

Remedy. The most mechanically reliable revision in this chapter: delete the first three paragraphs and read what remains. If the opening improves or is unchanged, they were throat-clearing. Repeat until deletion causes loss. As an estimate, not a count, a sizeable minority of student openings improve on the first application.

The exception. Novels whose opening pages are a deliberate approach — Proust’s overture, the slow entry of The Magic Mountain — where the delay enacts the book’s relation to time. The distinction is whether the approach is doing thematic work or merely postponing.

8.8 The Unanchored Opening

Symptom. The novel opens with dialogue, or with action, between speakers who have no bodies, in a place with no properties, at an unspecified time.

Mechanism. A failure at Emmott’s level of the contextual frame (§3.3). Readers bind speech to persons and persons to places; unbound speech cannot be stored, and by the fifth exchange the reader is holding voices without owners. The result is the paradox that a fast opening reads as slow, because comprehension has stalled while the words continue.

Persistence. Dialogue is vivid to write and creates the sensation of momentum. Advice to “start with dialogue” is common and, unqualified, harmful.

Remedy. Anchor within the first three exchanges: one body, one place, one time. Not a paragraph of description — a clause.

The exception. Openings where non-orientation is the represented state: Beckett; the opening of The Sound and the Fury, where an unbound perception is the point.

8.9 The Cast Parade

Symptom. Six named characters in two pages.

Mechanism. Per the rules of notice (§3.2), a name in a privileged position is a promise. Six promises exceed working memory and, worse, are indistinguishable in weight, so the reader cannot tell whom to attach to. Openings fail here not from confusion alone but from the reader’s inability to allocate investment.

Persistence. Ensemble novels and family sagas do have many characters, and the writer wishes to be fair to them.

Remedy. Name one, at most two. Others may be present as roles (her brother, the man from the bank) and acquire names when they acquire significance. Ensemble structures typically solve this by sequential introduction — a chapter each — rather than by simultaneous introduction.

The exception. Nineteenth-century multi-plot novels frequently open with a survey of a family or a society and rely on the reader’s patience and on chapter architecture to sort them out. Yet Middlemarch opens with one woman, and Anna Karenina, despite its enormous cast, opens with a single household in a single crisis.

8.10 Paratext Dependency

Symptom. The opening makes sense only to a reader who has read the jacket copy — a story that assumes the reader already knows there will be a murder, a dystopia, or a ghost.

Mechanism. The obligation has been outsourced to an artefact the author does not control and that does not travel with the text (§3.7).

Persistence. The writer has read their own blurb, and cannot unread it. The knowledge is invisible from inside.

Remedy. Test 5, run with an uninformed reader.

The exception. Series instalments, which legitimately assume prior reading — though see §10.4, which argues they assume it far less safely than authors believe.
