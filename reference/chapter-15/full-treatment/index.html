Chapter 15 · The Reveal

The Reveal

Continue in Core

Disclosure, Withholding and the Second Reading: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 15. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

This chapter is new in the second edition, so its Reference layer is this single file rather than a corrected original. It extends the reader’s version in Core, The Reveal, and can be read without it: the definitions it relies on are restated in section 1. Illustrative passages are quoted briefly for the purposes of criticism and study, and full citations appear in section 10. Where a work is read in translation, the translator is named. The analyses disclose the endings of the works they discuss.

1. Scope and working definitions

A reveal is a disclosure of a fact the reader lacked that changes the reader’s understanding of what she has already read. The first half of the definition separates it from new information, which changes only what will happen; the second makes it retrospective. Every passage that bears on a reveal is read twice: the first reading is the one the reader made at the time, and the second reading the one the reveal makes. The movement between them is the double reading, and a reveal is prepared when the passages before it support both.

A recognition, Aristotle’s anagnorisis, is a character’s change from ignorance to knowledge, the reveal received on the page. The two often coincide and need not: a reader can learn alone, and a character can learn what the reader already knew.

The governing claim is that a reveal is earned when the pages before it bear two readings and when something inside the book, a character who does not know or a teller with a reason not to say, explains why the truth came when it did; and that it may reinterpret whatever the double reading reaches and falsify only what a teller in the book could have shaped. The claim is about preparation and authority, not about how surprising a reveal should be. A reveal the reader half expects can satisfy it, and so can a reveal disclosed in the first quarter.

The chapter does not treat the mechanics of narrating the past once a reveal has made it necessary, which belong to Chapter 7; the arrangement of timelines that withholds by structure, which belongs to Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture; the prologue that discloses its ending in advance (Chapter 10); the placing of a midpoint in the book’s shape (Chapter 11); the character’s change of rule, as distinct from the reader’s change of understanding (Chapter 12); the allocation of minds, which decides who can know what (Chapter 13); the ending’s other duties (Chapter 16); or the reveal held across a series and the genres that require one (Chapter 17, Series and Genre).

2. The theory at length

2.1 Aristotle’s recognitions

The Poetics (S. H. Butcher’s translation) defines recognition in chapter 11 as “a change from ignorance to knowledge, producing love or hate between the persons destined by the poet for good or bad fortune”, and calls the finest recognition one attended by a reversal, as in the Oedipus. Chapter 16 ranks the kinds. Lowest are recognitions by signs, birthmarks, scars, necklaces, the “least artistic” because they need no plot. Next are recognitions invented at will by the poet, as when Orestes simply declares himself. Third is recognition by memory, when a sight or a song awakens a feeling, and fourth by reasoning, as in the Choephori: “Some one resembling me has come: no one resembles me but Orestes: therefore Orestes has come.” A fifth, which Aristotle calls composite, turns on a false inference by one of the characters. Best of all is the recognition that “arises from the incidents themselves, where the startling discovery is made by natural means”.

The ranking anticipates two of this chapter’s tests. A recognition by token or by the poet’s fiat is a reveal from outside (test 8), because nothing in the chain of events produces it; the recognition from the incidents is the reveal whose source is the book’s own causal system. And the pairing of recognition with reversal is the earliest statement of consequence (test 6): the discovery is finest when it changes the discoverer’s fortune in the same movement.

2.2 Sternberg’s three interests and Barthes’s hermeneutic code

Meir Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978), and his later essays in Poetics Today under the title “Telling in Time”, derive narrative interest from the relation between the order of events and the order of telling. Suspense is uncertainty about the future of the story; curiosity is uncertainty about a past the reader knows to be missing; surprise is the discovery of a gap the reader did not know existed. The three are produced by arrangement, and a single fact can generate any of them depending on when the reader learns that she lacks it. A reveal answers curiosity or produces surprise, which gives the chapter its curiosity reveal and surprise reveal. The distinction matters in revision because the two are prepared differently. A curiosity reveal is prepared by keeping the question alive, and readers are expected to guess at it; a surprise reveal is prepared by double readings that do not announce themselves, and a guess spoils it.

Roland Barthes’s S/Z (1970; translated by Richard Miller, 1974), a reading of Balzac’s novella Sarrasine, names the code by which a text poses enigmas and delays their answers the hermeneutic code, and lists its devices: the thematisation and proposal of the enigma, its formulation, the promise of an answer, the snare (a deliberate evasion of the truth), the equivocation (a mixture of truth and snare), jamming (an acknowledgement that the enigma is insoluble), the suspended answer, the partial answer, and the disclosure. The list is a repertoire for the space between a question and its answer, and two of its items, the snare and the equivocation, are what section 5 calls the decoy reading and the equivocation. Barthes’s jamming is the declined reveal seen from inside a sentence.

2.3 Hitchcock’s bomb

In his interviews with François Truffaut (Hitchcock, 1966; English edition 1967) Alfred Hitchcock distinguished surprise from suspense with a bomb under a table. Unannounced, its explosion gives the audience fifteen seconds of surprise; shown in advance, with a clock set for one o’clock, it turns an ordinary conversation into fifteen minutes of suspense. The lesson concerns alignment, whether the audience learns a fact with a character, ahead, or after. Hitchcock applied it to Vertigo (1958), whose deception is shown to the audience two-thirds through, before the hero learns it; he told Truffaut that nearly everyone around him had been against the change. The trade is not always made in suspense’s favour: Hitchcock himself made Psycho (1960) on surprise. The principle is that the writer chooses it.

2.4 The fair-play codes

The golden-age detective story made explicit what a reveal owes the reader. S. S. Van Dine’s “Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories” (The American Magazine, 1928) and Ronald Knox’s “Decalogue”, the preface to The Best Detective Stories of 1928–29 (1929), require among other things that every clue be presented to the reader, that the culprit be introduced early, and, in Knox’s words, that the criminal not be “anyone whose thoughts the reader has been allowed to follow”, and that the Watson “not conceal any thoughts which pass through his mind”. Agatha Christie’s The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), narrated by its murderer, was attacked for breaking such rules and defended, then and since, on the ground that its narrator states nothing untrue (section 6.1).

The codes are a period convention for one genre’s contract, and they are not applied literally outside it. Their principle survives in two parts: the reader must have been able in principle to reach the second reading, which is the double reading; and no mind the reader shares may hide a thought without a reason the book can show, which is the withholder of section 5.

2.5 The unreliable narrator and paralipsis

Wayne C. Booth’s The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961) called a narrator unreliable “when he does not speak for or act in accordance with the norms of the work”, and reliable when he does; the reader of an unreliable narrator reads past the narration to an implied author’s norms. Gérard Genette’s Narrative Discourse (1972; translated by Jane E. Lewin, 1980) adds two terms for departures from a narration’s chosen focalisation: paralipsis, giving less information than the focalisation would supply, and paralepsis, giving more. A withheld fact in a narration that follows the mind which knows it is a paralipsis, and the fair-play codes forbid it unless it is motivated.

The two theories together give the teller rule of section 3.6. Booth’s unreliability belongs to a teller, a narrator with a character and norms of his own, and a reveal can expose it. A narration that has declared no teller cannot be unreliable in Booth’s sense, because there is nobody whose norms differ from the work’s; a reveal that requires it to have stated an untruth makes the work, not a teller, the liar.

2.6 The evidence

The evidence is experimental and drawn from short texts. Hans Hoeken and Mario van Vliet (Poetics, 2000) varied the discourse structure of one story and found that a version containing an unexpected event was rated higher and recalled better than one without, and attributed the difference to readers reassessing preceding events, which is the double reading measured. They also found suspense where readers knew the outcome, which supports Hitchcock’s trade. Jonathan Leavitt and Nicholas Christenfeld (Psychological Science, 2011) gave readers short stories, among them ironic-twist stories and mysteries, with and without a paragraph disclosing the outcome, and found the spoiled versions rated more enjoyable. Benjamin Johnson and Judith Rosenbaum (Communication Research, 2015), with longer stories, found the reverse: unspoiled readers reported more suspense and more enjoyment. Thomas Daniel and Jeffrey Katz (Psychological Reports, 2019) found spoilers reduced the enjoyment of television episodes but not of short stories. The results disagree on whether knowing an outcome reduces pleasure, and agree that it changes the kind of interest a reader takes. A conjecture consistent with all four is that a reveal’s value lies less in surprise than in the rereading it prompts, which is the claim of this chapter on other grounds.

3. The extended catalogue of reveals

3.1 The four objects, and the reveal of world

Core classifies reveals by what they change. The reveal of event discloses what happened (Rebecca, 1938, where Maxim’s confession recasts every silence the narrator read as grief). The reveal of identity discloses who someone is (Magwitch in Great Expectations; the dead psychologist in The Sixth Sense). The reveal of motive discloses why an act the reader watched was done (Okonkwo’s fear at chapter scale, in Chapter 6; the father’s illness in the household novel). The reveal of telling discloses something about the text itself: its author, its purpose, or its untruth (The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, Gone Girl, Atonement).

A fifth object, which Core folds into the others, is the reveal of world: a disclosure about the rules of the story’s world, which recasts the meaning of every ordinary scene at once. Kazuo Ishiguro’s Never Let Me Go (2005) discloses, gradually and then plainly, that its students were created to provide organs, and every scene of school life becomes a scene in an institution with a purpose. It differs from the others in range, recasting the world rather than particular passages.

3.2 The gradual reveal

Not every reveal has a moment. In the gradual reveal the truth is disclosed in increments, each confirming what the reader has begun to infer, so that the double reading is performed progressively rather than at once. Never Let Me Go is the model: Miss Lucy’s statement to the students is a disclosure the reader has half anticipated, and the book’s interest moves from what is true to how the characters live with it. A gradual reveal fails if one increment is so much larger than the others that it reads as a twist the earlier increments gave away; it succeeds when the reader’s inference and the characters’ acknowledgement advance together.

3.3 The false reveal

A false reveal is a disclosure presented as the truth and later shown to be wrong, so that the book contains two reveals, the second correcting the first. Its legitimate form gives the false disclosure a source the book can blame, a lying witness or a character’s mistaken inference, and lets the true one reinterpret it rather than void it. Its illegitimate form is the narration announcing a solution and then retracting it, which teaches the reader to believe nothing the book states. Knives Out (section 6.3) contains a false reveal of the legitimate kind.

3.4 The serial reveal

A serial reveal is a chain of disclosures, each recasting the last. The chain works when each reveal reinterprets the previous one and the reader can hold all of them at the end; it fails, as the serial reveal of section 4.3, when each voids the previous one, since the reader stops investing in any reading at all.

3.5 The declined reveal: a ruling

Chapter 13 proposed the epistemological domain, a grant of viewpoint made to show that no single vantage can know what happened, and adopted it for diagnosis while conceding three objections: that it may be contrast plus unreliability; that its examples cluster in modernist fiction; and that it names what a structure means rather than what a grant buys. It left this chapter to adopt or reject it at the scale of a book.

This chapter adopts it at the scale of a book, as the plural-vantage form of the declined reveal: a book that holds a question of fact open to its end and shows the reader that it is doing so. The ruling meets the objections in turn. The first is answered at this scale because the scale supplies the test that Chapter 13 lacked: contrast plus unreliability ends in a reveal that tells the reader which account to discount, while the epistemological domain ends without one, so that a reader who adopted any single account would believe something the book will not confirm. The third lapses, since what a structure means is a reveal’s own subject; a domain that names meaning is out of place among the purposes of a grant and in place among the outcomes of a book. The second stands in part. The declined reveal is commoner in literary fiction, though The Affair’s first season keeps its lovers’ memories unreconciled on screen, and Yann Martel’s Life of Pi (2001) ends by offering one teller’s two accounts and asking which the listener prefers, the same refusal made by a single voice.

The declined reveal is wider than the epistemological domain. It includes the single teller’s refusal (Life of Pi) and the plain silence, a fact the book does not give and no account disputes, which is the household’s mother. Its condition is visibility: the question must be kept before the reader and the book’s other questions closed around it, as Chapter 16 required of the mother’s reason for changing the locks. A declined reveal the reader can no longer see is an abandoned thread. Barthes’s jamming is its signature in the sentence: an acknowledgement, in the text, that the answer will not come.

3.6 The teller rule and range

Chapter 6 proposed the recasting close, a chapter ending that changes the meaning of the chapter it closes, and noted that over-delivered it voids the chapter. Chapter 16 ruled that the category stops discriminating at a book’s last line and passed on the question it leaves, how far a close may recast a whole book, from the parallel that adds a layer to the retrospective reframe that changes what the book was.

The answer distinguishes reinterpretation from falsification. A closing reveal reinterprets a passage when it gives it a second meaning while its first remains a true description of what the reader was shown; it falsifies a passage when it declares that what the reader was shown did not happen. The range of a reveal is the set of passages it recasts in either way; the term is distinct from Chapter 7’s reach, Genette’s measure of how far an anachrony leaps from the present. Reinterpretation may extend as far as the double reading holds. Falsification is governed by the teller rule: a reveal may falsify only passages that a teller in the book could have shaped, and only if the reveal identifies the teller and the book gives the teller a reason. Atonement falsifies its Balham reunion within Briony’s authorship and gives her motive; Gone Girl falsifies Amy’s diary within Amy’s authorship and gives hers; The Usual Suspects falsifies its flashbacks within Verbal Kint’s telling. The Murder of Roger Ackroyd and The Sixth Sense falsify nothing and recast their whole length by reinterpretation alone, which is the safest way to a range of the whole book.

A close that falsifies passages the narration vouched for on its own authority, the dream ending, the simulation nobody in the book was running, voids the book as the over-delivered recasting close voids its chapter. The recasting close therefore needs no separate category at book scale: a close may recast a whole book when every passage it recasts survives the double reading or lies within a teller’s account. The teller rule is a reader’s contract rather than a law, and section 8 notes the books it does not fit.

3.7 Summary table

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

Core gives five: the cheat, the giveaway, the reported reveal, the idle reveal and the reveal from outside, defined in section 9. The patterns below extend the catalogue.

4.2 The coy narrator

Symptom. The narration keeps announcing that it is withholding: if she had known then, it would be years before he understood, had she looked more closely. Readers grow impatient, and the reveal, when it comes, has been anticipated so often that it lands as a relief rather than a surprise.

Mechanism. The coy narrator turns curiosity into irritation by advertising a gap without feeding it. Each announcement is a promise the narration does not keep until the end, and promises without increments read as manipulation. It is a form of the giveaway, since it tells the reader there is something to give away.

Remedy. Cut every announcement but one, and make that one carry information: a clue that bears both readings, not a statement that a clue exists.

Exception. Markus Zusak’s The Book Thief (2005), whose narrator, Death, announces deaths in advance as a matter of style. It succeeds because the announcements disclose rather than withhold: the reader is told what will happen and left wondering how, so the coyness is converted into dread, as Chapter 10 discusses for its prologue.

4.3 The serial reveal that voids

Symptom. The last act stacks twist on twist, each overturning the last, and readers end unsure what was true and uninterested in deciding.

Mechanism. Each reveal in the chain falsifies its predecessor rather than reinterpreting it, so that no reading survives. The reader, taught that every disclosure will be retracted, stops investing in any. It fails the teller rule repeatedly, or passes it only by naming a new teller at every step.

Remedy. Order the chain so that each reveal recasts the last without voiding it, and so that ranges shrink as the chain goes. Cut any reveal that exists only to overturn the one before.

Exception. The Usual Suspects, which stacks reveals in its last minutes and succeeds because they all collapse into one, the identity of the teller, so that the chain resolves into a single double reading of the whole film.

4.4 The red herring that eats the book

Symptom. A false trail occupies so much of the book that, when it is abandoned, a large part of what the reader invested in turns out to have been for nothing.

Mechanism. A red herring is a passage built to support a false second reading. When it grows into a thread, with its own scenes and characters, its abandonment voids the thread, and the reader experiences the reveal as a loss of the book she was reading. It fails test 6 in reverse: the reveal’s consequence is to make much of the book idle.

Remedy. Keep red herrings at the scale of a passage. If a false trail must be large, give it a second job, a character’s arc or a thematic variation, so that it is reinterpreted rather than voided when it is abandoned.

Exception. Wilkie Collins’s The Moonstone (1868), in which suspicion of Rosanna Spearman and of Rachel Verinder occupies much of the book and is abandoned, and the book succeeds because both trails are reinterpreted rather than voided: Rosanna’s actions were real and had a motive, love for the man who took the diamond, and Rachel’s silence protected him.

4.5 The withholding viewpoint

Symptom. A viewpoint character turns out to have known the truth all along, and readers who have spent chapters in her mind feel that her thoughts were edited.

Mechanism. Paralipsis without a reason: the narration follows a mind that holds the fact, and the fact does not appear. Knox’s rule against hiding the Watson’s thoughts describes it. In close third person it is harder to excuse than in first, because the first-person narrator has a motive the reveal can disclose, while the close-third narration has no teller to blame.

Remedy. Either show where the character’s attention is, so that the fact is plausibly not in her thoughts in the scenes the reader shares; or give the narration a teller, a retrospective frame, so that the withholding belongs to someone; or move the viewpoint away from the knowing character in the scenes where the fact would surface.

Exception. The household novel’s prologue (Chapter 10), which follows the father on a day when he knows he is ill and does not name the illness. It holds, for most readers, because his attention is shown to be elsewhere, on the till, the boy, the pen, and because the one sentence that touches his knowledge, he could not have said in case of what, is an equivocation (section 7.2). It is the book’s nearest approach to the pattern.

5. The diagnostic procedures

The tests use two documents. The double-reading table lists every passage bearing on one reveal, in book order, with its position as a percentage, its first reading and its second. The disclosure map has a row for each withheld fact and a column for the reader and for each major character, with the position at which each learns it; a cell may say never. Draft both before revising.

Test 1, double reading. Read down the table and mark rows with one reading. A row with only a first reading is a candidate cheat; check it against test 2. A row with only a second is a giveaway; give it a first reading or cut it. Then look at the spread: more than half the rows in the last fifth of the book is an approach, and a reader will feel the reveal coming.

Test 2, teller. For each passage the reveal falsifies, write who could have shaped it. If the answer is the narration, and the book has no declared narrator with a character of his own, the passage must be rewritten or reassigned.

Test 3, withholder. Name the withholder of each reveal: a vantage that does not know, a teller with a motive, or an arrangement. For a vantage, write the reason for its ignorance and the place the reader sees it; for a teller, the motive and the place it is disclosed; for an arrangement, the convention that makes it acceptable. A reveal with no name in this column is the withholding viewpoint or the cheat.

Test 4, alignment. For each row of the disclosure map, compare the reader’s column with the characters’. Where the reader is ahead, check that the gap is used as suspense; where she is level, check that the recognition is staged; where she is behind a character she follows, apply test 3.

Test 5, successor. For each reveal before the last fifth, write the question it answers and the question it leaves, and compare them. If the second is weaker, move the reveal or find the decision it forces.

Test 6, consequence. For each reveal, list the characters who act differently because of it and what the ending means because of it. An empty list is the idle reveal.

Test 7, recognition. Find the scene in which each reveal is received. Mark whether the truth is found or told, and whether the finder does something on the page.

Test 8, source. Trace each disclosure to its source and the source to its first appearance. A source first seen within a chapter of the reveal fails unless the genre’s contract allows it.

6. Further analyses

6.1 The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926): the range of the whole book without a falsehood

Dr James Sheppard, a village doctor, narrates the investigation of the murder of Roger Ackroyd, conducted by Hercule Poirot, who has retired next door to grow vegetable marrows. Sheppard acts as Poirot’s companion and records the case. In the last chapters Poirot shows that Sheppard is the murderer, and the final chapter, “Apologia”, is Sheppard’s confession, written as he prepares to take his own life. He explains that he had begun the manuscript as an account of Poirot’s failure.

The reveal is of identity and of telling at once, and its range is the whole book by reinterpretation alone. Sheppard’s narration omits but does not state an untruth. At the point where he removed the dictaphone and moved the chair after the murder he writes that he did what little had to be done, which is true under both readings, an equivocation in the sense of Core’s mechanism. The withholder is a teller with a motive, invisible until the end, which is why the book fails test 3 at the moment of reading and passes it in retrospect; it is Core’s exception to that test. The fair-play objection, that a narrator the reader follows hid his thoughts, is answered by the teller rule: Sheppard is a teller, his manuscript has a purpose the last chapter discloses, and the concealment belongs to him. A close-third version of the same plot, with no manuscript and no teller, would have been the withholding viewpoint of section 4.5.

6.2 The Silent Patient (2019): withholding by arrangement

Alex Michaelides’s thriller is narrated by Theo Faber, a psychotherapist who takes a post at a secure unit in order to treat Alicia Berenson, a painter who shot her husband, Gabriel, and has not spoken since. Theo’s account of the treatment is interleaved with his account of discovering his wife Kathy’s affair, and with Alicia’s diary from before the killing. The reader takes the two strands of Theo’s life to run together. The reveal is that they do not: the affair belongs to years earlier, Kathy’s lover was Gabriel, and Theo, following him, became the masked man who held Alicia and Gabriel captive on the night of the shooting. Alicia’s diary, read in the last pages, tells the police so.

The reveal is of telling, and its withholder is an arrangement inside a teller’s account. Theo never dates his two strands as simultaneous; the reader assumes it, because a narrator’s alternating strands usually run together. The book therefore passes the teller rule: nothing is falsified, and the reinterpretation reaches every scene of Theo’s marriage. It is also a case for Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture, since the withholding is a timeline placed beside another without a stamp. Readers have divided over whether the arrangement plays fair. The double-reading table explains the division: the passages bear both readings, but few of them bear the second reading strongly enough for a reader to point to afterwards, which is the third property of a sound table in Core’s mechanism.

6.3 Knives Out (2019): a false reveal and a turn of alignment

Rian Johnson’s film opens on the death of Harlan Thrombey, a crime novelist, at his birthday party. Early in its first act it shows the viewer what the detective, Benoit Blanc, does not know: Harlan’s nurse, Marta Cabrera, believes she gave him an overdose of morphine by mistaking his vials, and Harlan, to protect her, cut his own throat after coaching her in an alibi. Marta vomits when she lies, a trait the film establishes before it matters. From this point the viewer is ahead of the detective, and the whodunit has become a story of suspense about whether Marta will be caught. In the last act Blanc discloses that Marta made no mistake: Harlan’s grandson Ransom had swapped the vials’ contents, and Marta, by her nurse’s instinct, gave the right dose from the wrong labels. Ransom confesses when Marta tells him a witness has survived.

The early disclosure is a false reveal of the legitimate kind (section 3.3). It was true as Marta perceived it, and the second reveal reinterprets rather than voids it: the flashback showed what she did and what the labels said, and never what the vials held. The film thus keeps the teller rule with a camera, since its images belong to Marta’s understanding of the night, an equivocation in pictures. The alignment turns twice, from level to ahead at the first disclosure and from ahead to level at the second, when the viewer learns the truth with Marta. The successor question at the turn, whether Marta will be found out, is stronger than the one it answered, and the closing reveal has consequence, since it frees Marta and gives her the house.

7. The household reveal in full

7.1 The draft

In the draft convergence (Core, “The Problem”) Dov says that he took the ledger home, read it, and knows their father was ill and had seen a doctor since the autumn; these details are draft-only. It answered a question about plausibility by spending the reveal: the recognition becomes a report (test 7), a character learns first offstage (test 4), and the ending’s He had known. He had not told them repeats Dov.

7.4 The revision

The revision deletes Dov’s four sentences of report, leaving “It’s all there. From March, it’s mine.”, and adds two sentences to his chapter of the evening of 9 March, after he writes the date and the count:

He did not turn back the page, then or in any of the months after. In eleven years he had never read a page of it; the ledger was for writing in, and reading it back had been his father’s business, and he did not start now.

The sentences supply the reason for Dov’s ignorance that the case in Chapter 13 lacked. The reason is in character: Dov thinks of the dead man as his father, has just decided that the ledger has a way of being written in, and keeps the form of his father’s honesty without its content, as Chapter 14 proposes. The reason is visible before the reveal, so the withholder passes test 3, and it gives the convergence line its double reading. It also holds a thematic irony the book need not state: the father wrote in case anyone ever needs to check, and the one person who held the book for four months never checked.

A rejected alternative placed the evidence outside Dov’s mind: Elena, at the convergence, would see a till receipt she had used to mark her place still standing out of the pages where she left it. It would have shown, from a sister’s viewpoint, that nobody had read on. It was rejected because it adds a plant to a scene that Chapter 12 and Chapter 14 already found crowded, and because it proves only that nobody moved a receipt.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

It settles the withholder of the book’s largest reveal and the alignment of the ending: the illness reaches the reader and all three siblings at the reading, and the reader arrives at it half a step ahead, as a curiosity reveal rather than a surprise. It confirms the mother’s silence as a declined reveal, kept visible. It does not change the text of any earlier chapter; the two sentences are proposed for Dov’s chapter, for the author to confirm.

Five things remain unsolved. The sentences state a motive rather than dramatise it. They use the narration’s retrospective gap a second time in a short chapter, which may read as underlining. Beside the doctor’s name they make the illness inferable at 78 per cent, which costs surprise. The prologue’s equivocation depends on one verb. And the convergence remains crowded for the sisters, so its sequence must let Elena receive the hand before Nadia arrives for the reading.

8. The limits of the model

The model assumes a reader who reads forwards once and then looks back, and a book whose facts are stable enough to be disclosed. It fits the novel of secrets, the mystery and the thriller well, and the novel of consciousness less well, where what is withheld may be a feeling the character has not yet had rather than a fact anyone holds. It assumes too that a narration without a declared teller is authoritative, and the teller rule rests on that assumption. Some books unsettle it deliberately: metafiction that admits the author into the text, as John Fowles’s The French Lieutenant’s Woman (1969) does with its alternative endings, and the fantastic in which no frame decides between dream and event. In such books the teller rule describes what the reader expects in order to show what the book declines, and its failures are the book’s subject rather than its faults.

The model also says little about the rereader, who reads every reveal ahead of the characters; section 2.6’s evidence is consistent with, but does not establish, the view that the double reading rather than the surprise is what a reveal contributes over time.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

The definitions below are for the consolidated glossary.

Alignment. Whether the reader learns a withheld fact with a character, ahead of one, or after.

Closing reveal. A reveal in the last part of a book, with no successor question inside it.

Consequence. The property of a reveal that changes what a character does or what the ending means.

Coy narrator. A failure pattern: a narration that announces its withholding without feeding it.

Curiosity reveal. A reveal that answers a question the reader knew she had (after Sternberg’s curiosity).

Decoy reading. A first reading supplied by a character with a reason to believe it, so that the reader’s error is attributable.

Declined reveal. A book’s refusal, kept visible to its end, to disclose a fact or to adjudicate between accounts; at book scale, the form of Chapter 13’s epistemological domain.

Disclosure map. The chapter’s tool for timing: a row for each withheld fact and a column for the reader and each character, recording when each learns it.

Double reading. The reading of a passage twice, under the understanding before and after a reveal.

Double-reading table. The chapter’s tool for preparation: every passage bearing on a reveal, with its first and second readings.

Early reveal. A reveal in the first third of a book, which turns curiosity into consequence.

Equivocation. A sentence true under both the first and the second reading.

False reveal. A disclosure presented as true and later corrected by another.

First reading; second reading. The understanding of a passage before a reveal, and after it.

Giveaway. A failure pattern: preparation that supports only the second reading, or too densely supports both.

Gradual reveal. A truth disclosed in increments, each confirming the reader’s inference.

Idle reveal. A failure pattern: a reveal that changes nothing a character does or the ending means.

Range. The set of passages a reveal recasts, by reinterpretation or falsification; distinct from Chapter 7’s reach.

Recognition. A character’s change from ignorance to knowledge (Aristotle’s anagnorisis); the reveal received on the page.

Reported reveal. A failure pattern: the truth delivered as information by a character or the narration, rather than found.

Reveal. A disclosure of a fact the reader lacked that changes her understanding of what she has already read.

Reveal from outside. A failure pattern: a disclosure whose source lies outside the book’s causal system.

Objects of a reveal. Event, identity, motive, telling and world: what happened, who someone is, why an act was done, what the text itself is, and the rules of the story’s world.

Serial reveal. A chain of reveals, each recasting the last.

Successor question. The question a reveal leaves the reader holding in place of the one it answers.

Surprise reveal. A reveal of a gap the reader did not know existed (after Sternberg’s surprise).

Teller rule. A reveal may falsify only passages a teller in the book could have shaped, and only if it names the teller and gives a reason.

Cheat. A failure pattern: a passage before a reveal that supports only the first reading and is stated on the narration’s own authority.

Turn reveal. A reveal near the middle that changes the book’s engine.

Withholder. What keeps a truth from the reader until its disclosure: a vantage that does not know, a teller with a motive, or an arrangement.

Withholding viewpoint. A failure pattern: paralipsis without a reason in a narration that follows a mind which knows the fact.
