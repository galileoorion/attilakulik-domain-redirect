Chapter 16 · The Ending

6. Earned and Unearned

Continue in Core

6.0 What this section covers

“Earned” is the central term in craft discussion of endings and the least analysed. It is used as though its meaning were obvious, and it is not: writers apply it confidently to endings they like and cannot say what test they have run. This section takes the term apart.

It proceeds in seven steps. §6.1 states what the earned/unearned distinction actually claims, which turns out to be two claims rather than one. §6.2 separates the claim from the thing it is most often confused with, predictability, and argues that the separation is the whole art. §6.3 grounds both in Aristotle and in the plant economy this textbook has been building since Chapter 6. §6.4 supplies a taxonomy of sixteen closural strategies. §6.5 shows how to use a taxonomy of strategies in revision, which is not by classification. §6.6 addresses the proportion problem of climax and aftermath. §6.7 historicises the whole apparatus and warns against universalising the present.

6.1 What “earned” claims

The working distinction is the standard one:

Earned — every element the ending relies on was planted or built earlier in the book, so that the resolution feels, in hindsight, like a culmination the reader could have anticipated even if they did not predict it.

Unearned — the ending depends on something introduced at the last moment: new information, a sudden capability, an outside rescue, a fact withheld not for effect but for convenience.

This is correct and incomplete. Unpacked, it contains two distinct claims, which have different evidence, different failure modes and different remedies, and which are conflated at some cost.

Claim 1 is about preparation, and it is a fact about the text. Every element the ending uses as load-bearing must exist in the book before the ending. This is checkable, mechanically, by anyone with a copy: it is the plant audit (§7.1, test 2). Its failure mode is the unearned rescue (§8.1). Its remedy is to plant the missing element earlier, which is nearly always a small edit — a sentence in chapter four — rather than a structural repair.

Claim 2 is about retrospective inevitability, and it is a fact about the reader. The ending must produce, in hindsight, the sense that this was what the book was going towards. This is not checkable. It is a felt property, it can fail in books where every element is dutifully planted, and its remedy is not planting but pressure — the sense that the material was building towards something rather than merely accumulating.

The distinction matters because the two failures look identical from inside a draft and require opposite responses.

Claim 1 fails Claim 2 fails
Symptom The reader says where did that come from? The reader says so what?
Diagnosis An element is doing load-bearing work and was not deposited Every element was deposited and none of them was under pressure
What is missing A plant A want, running through the book, that the ending answers
Remedy Insert the plant, early and unremarked Strengthen the mid-book, not the ending
Cost of the wrong remedy Adding pressure to an unplanted ending produces melodrama Adding plants to an unfelt ending produces a well-documented shrug

The second failure is far more common in competent drafts and far less often correctly named. A writer who has been told to plant things will plant them; the resulting ending will be defensible, traceable, and inert. Claim 1 is a necessary condition and not a sufficient one, and a chapter that taught only the plant audit would produce endings that pass an audit and land on nothing.

The full statement the chapter uses from here on:

An ending is earned when everything it relies on was already in the book, and when what it does with that material answers a pressure the book has been building. The first is a matter of deposit; the second of interest accrued on it.

A note on why this is the answer to Miller. §3.9 left the chapter with a concession: every ending is a loss, and the question is what the reader gets in exchange. Claim 2 is the answer. An ending that merely closes the narratable has taken something and given nothing; an ending that closes the narratable by answering a pressure the narratable itself generated has converted instability into shape. The exchange is real, and it is what “earned” names. Miller’s thin endings are thin because they satisfy Claim 1 and ignore Claim 2 — they dispose of the disequilibrium administratively. His diagnosis is right about the endings he examines, and the remedy is not to abandon closure but to stop treating it as disposal.

6.2 Inevitability is not predictability

A damaging misunderstanding in this area, because it produces two opposite errors, is the belief that an earned ending is one the reader can see coming. It is not, and the confusion produces two opposite errors: writers who fear predictability and therefore withhold preparation, and writers who fear the accusation of cheating and therefore telegraph.

The distinction:

Predictability is a property of the reader’s state before the ending: they can say what will happen.

Inevitability is a property of the reader’s state after it: they cannot imagine it having happened otherwise.

These are independent. All four combinations exist:

Felt inevitable afterwards Not felt inevitable
Predicted beforehand The classical satisfaction. The reader sees the shape coming and is pleased to be right — the mode of tragedy, where the audience knows the myth, and of much romance and crime The tedious ending: predicted, arrived, meant nothing
Not predicted The target. Unforeseen and, in retrospect, the only possible outcome. This is the whole art The cheat: unforeseen and arbitrary — §8.1

The top-left quadrant deserves emphasis, because contemporary craft discussion tends to treat predictability as a defect tout court. It is not. Greek tragedy is entirely predictable to its original audience; the pleasure is in watching the known outcome arrive by the particular route this dramatist has chosen. So is the marriage plot: nobody reading Pride and Prejudice is uncertain that Elizabeth will marry Darcy, and the novel is not damaged by it. Surprise is one source of satisfaction at an ending and not the principal one.

What produces inevitability without predictability is a specific and teachable structure, and it needs naming because most drafts get it backwards:

Plant the materials in plain sight; withhold the combination. The reader should be able, afterwards, to point at every component and say they saw it. What they could not do is assemble them, because the assembling required a piece of judgement — a decision by a character, a shift of vantage, a recognition — that only the ending supplies.

This is Aristotle’s anagnorisis doing structural work. The recognition is not new information arriving; it is existing information reorganised. And it is why the strongest twist endings are also the most heavily planted: the elements were all present, in the open, doing other apparent jobs.

Two practical consequences.

(1) Plants should not look like plants. An element inserted so that the reader will notice it has been half-spent already. Genette’s term for the unemphatic advance notice is the amorce — a seed that passes as ordinary and becomes significant only retrospectively (Chapter 10 §6.2 uses the same term for the prologue’s fragments). The good pen going into the coat pocket in the household narrative’s prologue is an amorce: at the time it is a small oddity about a man’s habits.

(2) The test for a plant is not “did the reader notice?” but “can the reader find it?” These are different questions with different answers, and the second is the one the earned/unearned distinction actually depends on. A reader who reaches the last page and cannot locate the preparation feels cheated. A reader who located it in advance feels clever. A reader who could not have located it in advance but can locate it now is having the intended experience.

6.3 Aristotle’s necessity or probability, and the plant economy

The ancient form of the earned test is the requirement in the Poetics that the incidents of a plot follow one another by necessity or probability. Aristotle is precise about the alternative he is rejecting: a sequence in which events follow one another merely after rather than because. The distinction is the same one Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) makes with the causal chain — the test of whether each beat “had to happen next” — and applying it at the scale of the whole book gives the earned test its logical form.

Two refinements from the Poetics are worth carrying into revision.

“Or probability” is doing a great deal of work. Aristotle does not require that endings be necessary; probable is enough. This is a lower bar than modern craft discussion usually assumes, and it licenses a good deal. An ending need not be the only possible outcome; it needs to be one that the established characters, in the established world, would plausibly produce. Chapter 14’s Levin-and-Anna problem is the standard case: nothing necessitates Levin’s arrival at faith, and the novel’s claim is only that a man like that, in that situation, might.

The plot’s own structure is the source. Aristotle’s stipulation that the unravelling arise from the plot itself rather than from a contrivance is the ancestor of the whole modern prohibition on the deus ex machina, and it contains a discrimination that the modern version has lost. What Aristotle objects to is not implausibility but externality: an outcome produced by something outside the causal system the play has built. A wildly improbable event generated by the plot is acceptable; a probable one imported from outside is not. This is why coincidence at an ending is so much more damaging than coincidence at a beginning — a point worth teaching as a rule, since it explains a large asymmetry that puzzles students:

Coincidence may start a story and may not end one. A chance meeting on page one is a premise; the same meeting on page four hundred is a contrivance. The reason is causal position: an opening coincidence generates the causal chain, and a closing one substitutes for it.

The plant economy, formalised. This book has built the machinery for this in the chapters before this one and it can now be stated as a single system.

Stage Chapter The operation
Deposit Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) An object, phrase or gesture is established with a denotative job, so that it is present without being flagged
Maintenance Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) Proposition and payoff: the element recurs at intervals, accruing connotation, so that the reader’s frame stays warm
Instalment Chapter 10 (The Prologue) Closing the distance: partial closures are scheduled so the reader is periodically reassured the material is load-bearing
Discharge This chapter The element is used, in the ending, to do work it could not have done at deposit

The whole system has one purpose: to make the fourth stage possible without the reader detecting the first three as preparation. And it produces the chapter’s most testable single instruction:

Nothing new and load-bearing arrives at the ending. Everything the ending uses was deposited, maintained, and instalment-paid; what the ending contributes is not material but combination.

That instruction has a limit, and the limit should be stated because students over-apply the rule. It prohibits new load-bearing elements, not new elements. An ending may perfectly well contain a room the reader has not seen, a minor character who has not appeared, a sentence about a bus going past. What it may not do is rest on them. The test is substitution: if the element were removed, would the ending still work? If yes, it is furniture and is welcome. If no, it must have been planted.

6.4 A taxonomy of sixteen closural strategies

The strategies below are adapted from Torgovnick’s typology of closural relations (§3.5) and Herrnstein Smith’s account of closural devices (§3.2), with additions from practice. They are not mutually exclusive — most strong endings braid two or three — and they are not ranked. Each is a shape the ending can take, with characteristic purchases and characteristic costs.

# Strategy Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 Circularity The close returns to the beginning’s language, image or situation Wuthering Heights (Lockwood on the moor, twice); One Hundred Years of Solitude Formal closure at maximum; the shape becomes visible Can imply nothing has changed; risks reading as a trick
2 Parallelism A situation from earlier recurs with its terms altered Great Expectations, revised (the ruined garden); Pride and Prejudice (the second proposal) Character arc made visible without statement; uses paid-for material Requires the earlier instance to be memorable; fails silently if it is not
3 Tangent The close swerves to something apparently unrelated Madame Bovary (Homais’s medal); Nineteen Eighty-Four (the Appendix) Verdict delivered without comment; escapes the protagonist’s special pleading Reads as evasion or as authorial whim if the relation is not inferable
4 Incompletion The pattern is deliberately left unfinished The Handmaid’s Tale; Huckleberry Finn (lighting out) Honesty about what cannot be settled; preserves the narratable (§3.9) Now the default of a genre (§6.7); must work against the suspicion of default
5 The frame closed A narrator or vantage established at the start reappears Frankenstein (Walton resumes); Heart of Darkness Perspective; the story is shown having been received Two endings to write; the outer one is often flatter than the inner
6 The coda or finale A short final movement in a changed key, after the structure is complete Middlemarch (“Finale”); War and Peace’s epilogues Room for the character and theme domains after a plot-heavy climax The commonest site of §8.3 (endless aftermath) and §8.5 (tidiness)
7 The epilogue in another voice The close is delivered by a different speaker, register or document The Handmaid’s Tale (“Historical Notes”); Dracula (Harker’s note) Reframing power; the story is placed in a world larger than itself The reader has invested in the first voice and must be persuaded to accept a second
8 The proleptic close The future beyond the action is narrated Middlemarch; Jane Eyre’s final chapter Consequence made visible; the settlement’s classical instrument Forecloses the reader’s imagination; the most conventional strategy on the list
9 The terminal image The book ends on a picture, without commentary Blindness; Wuthering Heights; The Grapes of Wrath Maximum after-image; leaves interpretation with the reader Carries no information; fails if the image has not accrued meaning
10 The aphoristic close A generalisation, maxim or unqualified assertion Middlemarch (unvisited tombs); The Great Gatsby (boats against the current) Herrnstein Smith’s strongest formal device; portable, memorable Requires a narrator licensed to generalise; one step from §8.6
11 The deliberate anticlimax The close is smaller than the expectation it follows Vanity Fair (the puppets put back in the box); Madame Bovary Refuses false elevation; can be devastating Indistinguishable from failure unless the deflation is legible as chosen
12 The arrested ending The book stops mid-action, before the outcome The Handmaid’s Tale (the step into the van); The Grapes of Wrath Intensity; the reader completes the gesture The reader may experience it as the printer’s error; needs a strong formal signal
13 The retrospective reframe A late disclosure changes what the whole book was Atonement; The Murder of Roger Ackroyd The retroactive lens weaponised; enormous force Spends the reader’s trust; a second reading must be better, not merely different
14 The appendix or apparatus Non-narrative matter follows the narrative Nineteen Eighty-Four; Pale Fire Formal closure (§4.6); can reverse the narrative’s own verdict Only available to books whose formal contract admits apparatus (Chapter 9 §4.6)
15 The double ending Two endings are given, or the choice between them is exhibited Great Expectations (two published states); The French Lieutenant’s Woman Makes the arbitrariness of closure visible without abandoning it Almost always reads as metafiction; rarely available to a realist book
16 The refused ending The text declines to conclude, and marks the refusal Villette; A Passage to India; The Castle Integrity where the material admits no settlement Cannot be distinguished from evasion by inspection; §8.9

Four clusters behave alike in revision.

  • Strategies 1, 2 and 5 are return strategies. They close by revisiting. They are cheap (the material is paid for), reliable, and require nothing new. They are also the strategies most likely to be available in a draft that already has an ending problem, and the first place to look.
  • Strategies 6, 7, 8 and 14 are displacement strategies. They close by moving — in time, voice or register — away from the narrative. They buy perspective and cost immediacy, and every one of them opens the question Chapter 10 asks of prologues: does this material require its exteriority?
  • Strategies 9, 10, 11 and 12 are terminal strategies. They are decisions about the last page rather than the last chapter, and they can be substituted for one another almost freely without touching the structure. This makes them the cheapest thing to experiment with and the least likely to fix a structural problem.
  • Strategies 3, 4, 13, 15 and 16 are disruptive. They close by refusing the expected relation between ending and book. They have the largest effects, the highest failure rate, and — this is the significant point — they are almost never available as a repair. A book either was always going to end this way or cannot be made to.

6.5 How to use the taxonomy: substitution testing

The taxonomy’s purpose is not classification. Knowing that a draft ends with a proleptic close is worth nothing by itself. Its purpose is substitution testing, as with Chapter 9 §6.5’s opening gambits and Chapter 10 §6.4’s prologue types.

The procedure:

  1. Name the strategy the draft currently uses. Most drafts use one without having chosen it, and the commonest unchosen default in contemporary literary fiction, as far as this book can judge without a count, is 9 (the terminal image) and in commercial fiction 8 (the proleptic close).
  2. Write the same ending in two other strategies, chosen from a different cluster. Not sketches — actual prose, at least a page each.
  3. Do not judge until all three exist. The finished draft always feels correct; that is a property of finish, not of fit.
  4. Ask what each version makes the book about. This is the diagnostic question, and it is more informative than asking which is best. An ending in strategy 6 makes a book about consequences; the same material in strategy 12 makes it about a moment; in strategy 3 about a world that continues regardless.

The exercise is set formally at §12.1, exercise 2. Its reliability is high enough to be worth a claim: in a workshop setting, by this book’s estimate rather than any measurement, roughly a third of endings that “aren’t working” are the right material in the wrong strategy, and the substitution locates the problem faster than any amount of line-editing.

A specific and common finding is worth flagging. Drafts that feel too tidy are frequently strategy 6 or 8 material that wants to be 2 or 9 — a novel that has written a finale when it needed a parallel scene. And drafts that feel abrupt are frequently strategy 12 that wants to be 6: the writer, correctly wary of the endless aftermath, has stopped at the climax and left four domains unserved.

6.6 Climax and aftermath: the proportion problem

The most consequential structural decision at an ending is not which strategy but how much aftermath. Both errors are severe and they are opposite (§8.2, §8.3), which means the correction for one produces the other, and drafts commonly oscillate.

The framing that resolves the oscillation is Aristotle’s (§3.3): the aftermath is not an appendage to the structure but the lusis, the second of two movements. The question is therefore not how much can I get away with? but what does the unravelling have to do?

What only the aftermath can do:

Task Why the climax cannot do it
Show the consequences of the climax Consequences are, by definition, subsequent
Complete a second arc Two arcs cannot peak in the same scene without one subordinating the other (Chapter 14’s kernel/satellite distinction)
Let the reader register the value shift A shift occurring at maximum intensity is felt but not understood; understanding requires a beat of lower pressure
Deliver catharsis Release requires the pressure to have stopped
Provide descent from transportation (§3.6) The reader must be returned
Deliver the final image The image after the event outlasts the image of the event

What the aftermath must not do:

Prohibition Failure it produces
Introduce a new question The book restarts and then stops
Resolve threads in order of appearance rather than of weight §8.5
Continue past its own last decision §8.3
Explain what the climax meant §8.6

The operational rule the chapter offers on proportion:

The aftermath ends at the last decision, not at the last consequence. Consequences are infinite; decisions are countable. Find the final decision any character takes — however small, and it may be as small as putting on a coat — and end within a page of it.

This rule is Chapter 2’s (Scene and Sequel) sequel structure applied to the whole book (§2.3.1): reaction, dilemma, decision. A book’s aftermath that stops at reaction feels indulgent; one that reaches a decision feels complete; one that continues past the decision into further consequence feels like a failure to notice that it had finished.

How much do the traditions permit? The following is descriptive and approximate, derived from the works discussed in this chapter. It is offered as a corrective to the assumption that “short aftermath” is a rule rather than a period preference.

Tradition Typical aftermath as % of the book Characteristic form
Greek tragedy 10–20% The messenger speech and the chorus’s summation; the lusis is structurally substantial
18th-century novel 5–10% The concluding chapter; narrator’s direct address; distribution of fortunes
Victorian, serialised 5–15% The wind-up chapter, sometimes a full number; every named character accounted for
Tolstoyan Up to 12% An entire final part after the climax; a second arc completed (Anna Karenina, Part Eight)
Modernist 0–3% The terminal image; frequently no aftermath at all
Contemporary literary 1–5% A short final scene or a single image; the coda is out of fashion
Contemporary commercial 3–8% A short resolution chapter plus, frequently, a forward-looking beat
Genre series 3–10% Resolution of the volume plus a series hook (§8.11)

Two things follow. First, the range is enormous, which means no absolute prescription can be right. Second, the contemporary literary norm is at the extreme low end of the historical distribution — which is worth knowing before concluding that a draft’s forty-page final movement is self-indulgent. It may be Victorian.

6.7 Historical drift, and a caution against universalising the present

§3.7 laid out the history. This section states its consequence for the advice in this chapter, in the same terms Chapter 9 §6.7 and Chapter 10 use, because it is this book’s standing intellectual obligation.

Almost every prescription in §§7 and 8 is calibrated to Anglo-American literary and commercial practice since roughly 1960. That is a real tradition with real standards, and a writer working inside it needs to know them. It is not narrative.

Specifically:

Prescription in this chapter Describes narrative Describes a local culture
Nothing new and load-bearing at the close (§6.3) ✔ (Aristotle; nearly universal)
An ending must be felt as well as resolved (§2.3.2) ✔
Aftermath should be short ✔ (post-1920; the Victorian and Tolstoyan norms are three to five times longer)
A character should not state the theme (§8.6) ✔ (post-1900; the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century narrator does this as a matter of course)
Ambiguity is a legitimate close (§4.8) ✔ (post-1900, and now itself a default — §3.7)
Every named character need not be accounted for (§8.5) ✔ (the Victorian reader expected the opposite, and was not naive to expect it)
Coincidence may not resolve (§6.3) ✔ (Aristotle) but the tolerance varies hugely by period and genre
The series hook is a defect (§8.11) ✔ (a judgement of the standalone literary novel; serial forms have always done it)

The properly critical position is the same double one this book has maintained throughout: learn the current conventions well enough to break them deliberately, and read historically well enough to know they are conventions. A pedagogy that can only say “a modern editor would cut this” has explained the market and not the book — and at the ending, more than anywhere else, the market’s preferences have hardened into an aesthetic without anyone deciding that they should.

One final observation, which §16 returns to. The contemporary preference for the unresolved close is frequently defended on the grounds that life does not resolve. This is true and is not an argument, for the reason Kermode gives (§3.1): novels are not like life in any other respect either, and the demand for shape is not a demand for accuracy. A novel that refuses to conclude because life does not conclude has confused mimesis with form. The good reasons for an open ending are formal and thematic — that this book’s material admits no settlement, that the refusal is the argument — and they are available. The mimetic reason is not one of them.
