Chapter 16 · The Ending

4. The Domains of Purpose

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The domain model treats an ending as owing the reader work in several distinct registers at once. It is the same analytical move Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) makes for the scene, Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) for the detail and Chapter 10 (The Prologue) for the separated unit: a unit is anatomised by asking what kinds of work it can do, so that a vague dissatisfaction can be converted into a specific absence.

Its value is diagnostic. “This ending isn’t working” is not actionable. “This ending delivers plot and catharsis, gestures at theme, and does nothing for character” is.

Its danger is the one that the whole of §5 exists to counter: treating the domains as a sequence to be worked through. A book that stages an action climax and then appends a wrap-up chapter handling character, then theme, then feeling, has read the taxonomy as a schedule. The domains are registers in which an ending is heard, not tasks to be discharged in order, and the strongest endings sound several of them in a single image.

Five domains are set out below, followed at §4.6 by a proposed sixth. For each: what it is, why it matters, the textual carriers through which it is realised, what under- and over-delivery look like, and illustrations from published fiction.

One preliminary. The domains are not equally obligatory, and the inequality is a function of genre rather than of quality. A detective novel that fails plot resolution has failed; a lyric novel of consciousness may resolve nothing and be complete. §4.8 handles this properly; it is flagged here so that the tables below are not read as a bill.

4.1 Plot Resolution

What the reader receives: answers to the dramatic questions the book raised — or a legible, deliberate refusal to answer them, which is a different thing from having forgotten that they were asked.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives
The answered question Did she find out who kept writing in the ledger? The scene in which the information changes hands, or the moment a character acts on it
The consequence enacted What follows from the answer? A decision, a departure, a transaction, a refusal
The declined question, marked Is this being left open, or dropped? An explicit registration that the question remains — a character noting it, a narrator declining it
The reallocated question Was I asking the wrong thing? A late redirection: the book answers a question the reader did not know was primary (§8.7’s failure mode, used well)
The irreversible act Can this be undone? Signature, death, sale, departure, confession, burning

Discussion

Plot resolution is the domain craft literature over-weights and theory under-weights, and both distortions produce errors.

The over-weighting produces the belief that an ending’s job is to tie off. It is not. Its job is to reach a state in which the questions have stopped being live — which can be achieved by answering them, by making them unanswerable, or by making them no longer matter. Barthes’s hermeneutic code (S/Z, 1970), invoked in Chapter 9 §4.5 as the mechanism by which an opening plants an enigma, closes here; and Barthes’s own account of the code includes not only the answer but the snare, the partial answer and the jammed enigma. A hermeneutic sequence can terminate without disclosure and still terminate.

The under-weighting, more common in literary pedagogy, treats plot resolution as the vulgar domain — the one serious fiction rises above. This is a category error. A book that raised a question and then discarded it has not transcended plot; it has broken a contract, and readers feel it as carelessness rather than sophistication. The distinction the domain actually requires is between a question answered, a question declined on the page, and a question dropped. Only the third is a defect.

The discrimination is worth a rule, because the rest of the domain depends on it:

A declined question must be visibly declined. Somewhere in the ending, the text must register that the question exists and is not going to be answered. A character asks and is not told; a narrator notes the absence; a document is found to be incomplete. The registration costs a sentence and converts an oversight into a choice.

The Handmaid’s Tale is the model. Offred’s fate is unresolved — but the Historical Notes stage an entire scholarly apparatus failing to determine it, which converts the gap from an omission into the novel’s subject. The reader is not left wondering whether Atwood forgot; they are shown people trying to find out and failing.

A note on the plot question’s proper size. Chapter 14 (The Subplot) supplies the attention tax: every thread charges the reader page space and the novel owes a return proportionate to what it charged. The ending is where the bill arrives. A subplot that took eighty pages and receives one sentence of resolution has under-delivered; a subplot that took eight pages and receives a chapter has over-delivered, and the over-delivery is more damaging than it looks because it misrepresents the book’s own hierarchy. §7.1’s test 6 is this audit.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the dropped thread. A character, a question or an object of evident significance is simply not mentioned again. Note that under-delivery is much more often caused by forgetting than by choice, and that the forgetting is invisible to the writer, who remembers everything.
  • Over: the comprehensive settlement. Every named entity accounted for, in proportion to nothing. This is §8.5, and its mechanism is that uniform resolution destroys hierarchy as uniform density destroys emphasis (Chapter 9 §5.4).

Illustrations

1. Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — resolution as system. The novel’s plot resolution is famously total: Jarndyce and Jarndyce is consumed by its own costs, Lady Dedlock’s identity and death are settled, Esther’s parentage and marriage are settled, Tulkinghorn’s murder is solved, Krook’s shop and its papers are disposed of, and roughly two dozen minor figures receive a final position. It is worth teaching because it is the maximum case and it works — and the reason it works is that the resolutions are not distributed evenly but causally: nearly every thread terminates through Chancery, so the settlement is also the novel’s argument. Comprehensive resolution is safe when the comprehensiveness is itself thematic.

2. Flaubert, Madame Bovary (1857) — resolution by the wrong person. Emma dies; Charles dies; and Flaubert gives the novel’s final position not to either of them but to Homais, the pharmacist, who prospers and receives the cross of the Legion of Honour. The plot resolves and the resolution is an insult. This is Torgovnick’s tangent (§3.5) used with maximum precision: the ending swerves to a minor character, and the swerve is the verdict, because the survival of Homais is what the book has been arguing all along.

3. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — resolution the character cannot register. The plot’s central question — whether Stevens will recognise what he gave up — is answered on the pier at Weymouth, where he does, briefly and devastatingly, and then turns immediately to the technical problem of bantering. The resolution is complete and the character walks away from it. This is a demonstration that plot resolution does not require a character to acknowledge the resolution, only that the reader be able to.

4.2 Character-Arc Completion

What the reader receives: the final state of the change — or of the refusal to change — that the book has been tracking.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Note
The act only this changed person could perform Would they have done that in chapter three? The strongest carrier available, and the most underused
The repeated situation, answered differently What do they do the second time? Torgovnick’s parallelism; the most reliable device for making change visible, since the reader makes the comparison
The refused act What do they now decline to do? Change shown by omission; usually stronger than a performed act
A change in what they notice Do they see the same things? Chapter 4’s territory: selection of detail as characterisation, used as an arc marker
A change in address What do they call people now? Names, pronouns, forms of address; very cheap and very legible
The unchanged element What survived? An arc requires a constant against which the change is measured

Discussion

The governing principle is that arcs are completed by behaviour, not by statement. A character who says they have changed has not demonstrated it; a character who does something they could not have done earlier has. This is the ending’s version of the oldest instruction in the craft literature, and it fails in drafts for a specific and identifiable reason: at the end of a book, the writer knows what the change was and is therefore able to name it, and naming is easier than staging.

The strongest available device is the repeated situation. Put the character back in a circumstance the book has already shown, and let the difference in their behaviour carry the arc. It is efficient (it uses material already paid for), legible (the reader has the earlier instance to compare against), and unsentimental (nobody has to describe a feeling). It also produces closure by Torgovnick’s parallelism, so it discharges the character domain and a formal one at once.

Two refinements matter in revision.

First, the arc may complete as a refusal. Not every protagonist changes, and a book that tracks a failure to change owes the same completion. Stevens does not change; the ending shows him not changing, in full view of the opportunity. The novel is complete and the arc is complete, and it would be a poorer book if he had.

Second, an arc requires a constant. Change is only perceptible against something stable. If everything about a character is different at the end, the reader registers replacement rather than development. The practical form: identify the one thing that has not changed and make sure it is visible in the final pages. Nadia in §9.10 puts on her father’s coat — a change — and gathers the sleeves so they do not drag, which is the same managing, measuring precision she has had since Chapter 9’s Version B. The constant is what makes the change legible.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the unmarked arc. The character has changed and the ending gives the reader no occasion to see it. Frequently caused by an ending that is all plot: the protagonist is too busy resolving things to be observed.
  • Over: the announced transformation. A character explaining what they have learned, or a narrator explaining it for them. See §8.6; it is the commonest single failure in student endings, and it is generated by anxiety that the reader will miss the point.

Illustrations

1. Austen, Emma (1815) — the arc completed in an apology. Emma’s arc is a movement from confident misjudgement to accurate self-knowledge, and it completes not at her engagement but at Box Hill and afterwards, in her response to Mr Knightley’s rebuke. The novel’s ending confirms rather than performs the change. Worth noticing: the marriage is the plot resolution; the apology is the character resolution; they are separate events, several chapters apart, and the book is careful not to let one stand in for the other.

2. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958) — the arc completed by a refusal, and then annihilated. Okonkwo’s arc completes in his final act, which is the act his whole character has been driving towards and which his society can no longer contain. What Achebe then does is more radical: the last page removes the arc from the reader’s hands entirely and gives it to the District Commissioner, who is deciding how many paragraphs the matter deserves. The character domain is completed and then shown being erased, which is the novel’s argument about who writes endings.

3. Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878) — two arcs, completed at different distances. Anna’s arc completes in Part Seven; Levin’s completes in Part Eight, after her death, in a domain the novel has been building in parallel. The structural point, developed at §9.4: a novel with two arcs cannot complete both at the climax, and the aftermath exists to complete the second.

4.3 Thematic Closure

What the reader receives: the book’s final position — or its considered refusal of one — on the question it has been testing since its opening pages.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Note
The recurring phrase, returned Where have I heard that? The most economical carrier available; see §5.3
The recurring object, reused What is it for now? Chapter 4’s motif economy, cashed
The situation that answers the opening’s Is this where we started? Circularity; §6.4, no. 1
The narrator’s generalisation What does the book think? Powerful and dangerous; §8.6
The parallel case Who else does this happen to? Theme carried by a secondary figure, which insulates it from the protagonist’s special pleading
The withheld statement Why won’t it say? Refusal as position

Discussion

Thematic closure is the domain most often confused with the ending as a whole, and the confusion produces §8.6. The confusion is understandable: theme is what a reader reports when asked what a book was about, and the ending is where they form the report. But theme is not a proposition the book asserts; it is a question the book has been testing, and the ending’s job is to conclude the test, not to publish the result.

The distinction is operational. Compare two endings for the same novel:

Version What the last page does
Asserted theme A character says, in effect, we can never really know the people we love
Concluded test Three people read a document that proves the person they loved kept something from them, and none of them says anything, and one of them puts on the dead man’s coat

Both deliver the same theme. The first delivers it as a proposition the reader may agree or disagree with; the second delivers it as an experience the reader has just had. Only the second uses the book. This is the same discrimination Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) draws between text and subtext, and Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) between denotation and connotation, applied at the largest available scale.

Where the danger lies. Because the last pages carry privileged position (§3.6), a generalisation placed there acquires enormous authority — it is read as the book speaking. This makes the narratorial generalisation the most powerful thematic carrier and the most easily abused. Eliot uses it magnificently (§9.2) and could do so because the narrator has been generalising for eight hundred pages; a novel whose narrator has been effaced throughout cannot suddenly produce a maxim without the maxim reading as the author’s arrival.

A narrator may state the theme at the end only if the narrator has been the kind of narrator who states things all along. Thematic assertion is a voice-contract question (Chapter 9 §4.1) before it is a thematic one.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the unexamined close. The plot resolves and the book’s central question is simply not present in the final pages, so the reader closes it without a position on the thing it was about.
  • Over: the thesis statement. See §8.6. Also, in a subtler form, the ending that is only thematic — a final image so plainly symbolic that the characters have become its vehicles.

Illustrations

1. Morrison, Beloved (1987) — theme closed by contradiction. The novel’s closing pages state, twice, that it was not a story to pass on — a phrase which in one idiom means not to be transmitted and in another means not to be declined, and which is printed in a book that is doing the transmitting the phrase forbids. Thematic closure here is achieved by an assertion that undoes itself; the reader is left holding a position the sentence will not let them settle. §9.6 analyses this in full.

2. Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925) — theme closed by widening. The last page leaves Gatsby entirely and moves to the Dutch sailors, the fresh green breast of the new world, and the general condition of a people borne backwards. The theme is completed by enlarging the frame until the individual case becomes an instance of something continental. §9.3 dissects the mechanism.

3. Camus, The Stranger (1942; trans. Ward, 1988) — theme closed by an assent the reader cannot share. Meursault’s final acceptance of “the gentle indifference of the world”, and his wish for a crowd of spectators greeting him with cries of hate, closes the novel’s argument about consciousness and meaning by having the protagonist arrive somewhere the reader is not invited to follow. Note the translation caution: the structural claim (the theme completes in an assent) survives every English version; the tonal claim (whether the assent is serene or defiant) does not, and differs measurably between Gilbert, Ward and Smith.

4.4 Emotional Catharsis

What the reader receives: the specific felt release the book has been building — grief processed, tension discharged, dread confirmed, hope earned, anger permitted.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Note
Physical proximity of characters kept apart Are they in the same room? The most reliable and least literary device on this list
Silence after noise Has it stopped? Rhythm as feeling; the paragraph after the last shout
The withheld gesture, performed Did they finally? Requires the withholding to have been established
The permitted feeling Am I allowed to feel this? A character weeping, resting, sitting down, eating
Rhythmic and syntactic release Do the sentences let go? Long sentence after short ones; a period after fragments
The reader’s information advantage discharged Do they know what I know? Dramatic irony closed

Discussion

Catharsis is the domain most resistant to analysis and most damaged by the attempt, and this chapter treats it with corresponding brevity and a warning.

The warning first. Catharsis is not an event the ending causes; it is a release of pressure the book has already created. No technique produces it in a book that has not built the pressure, and almost any competent technique produces it in a book that has. This is why the domain is the one most often “fixed” incorrectly: a writer who feels the ending is emotionally flat adds emotional material to the ending, when the deficiency is nearly always upstream. Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) supplies the relevant concept in the empathy budget: the reader’s capacity to feel for a character is a function of accumulated access, and an ending cannot spend what earlier chapters did not deposit.

Aristotle’s katharsis is the source of the term and is less useful than its currency suggests, partly because what he meant by it — purgation, clarification, or something else — has been contested for two thousand years. What is transferable is the structural claim underneath: that a tragedy produces pity and fear through the arrangement of the incidents, not through appeals to feeling. Emotion is a product of structure.

Walter Benjamin’s remark in “The Storyteller” (1936) is the more useful modern statement, and it is worth quoting because it explains why endings carry emotion disproportionately: the reader of a novel, he suggests, is drawn by the hope of warming their shivering life with a death they read about. What he is describing is the ending as the one place where a life becomes finished and therefore meaningful — an experience unavailable in life, since one’s own is never observed complete. The novel’s ending offers the reader a completed life to hold, and the emotional charge of endings is substantially the charge of that impossible perspective.

Two practical notes.

The physical is more reliable than the verbal. The most dependable catharsis carriers in prose are bodies: people in the same room who have not been, a hand taken, a person sitting down, a door opened, a meal eaten. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is a consequence of the fact that emotional release is registered somatically, and that a described physical event is the cheapest way for a text to produce a somatic response.

Silence is the strongest instrument in the ending’s repertoire. Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) argues that what is not said carries the relational load; at an ending, a scene in which people who have been arguing for a book do not speak is worth any amount of dialogue. §9.10’s three siblings reading in silence is the household narrative’s entire cathartic payload, and it works because Chapter 3’s doorway exchange was adversarial and Chapter 9’s opening had Nadia declining to answer the phone.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the cold close. Technically complete, emotionally inert. Diagnosable by asking what the reader is invited to feel on the last page and finding no answer.
  • Over: the milked ending. Feeling extended past its discharge — a further page of weeping, a further paragraph of consolation. Its mechanism is that emotion decays under attention, so an ending that continues to solicit a feeling the reader has already had converts it into embarrassment.

Illustrations

1. Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939) — catharsis by an act that resolves no plot. The novel ends with Rose of Sharon, whose baby has been born dead, nursing a starving stranger in a barn. Nothing is resolved: the family’s situation is unchanged, the strike is unresolved, the road continues. The ending is nonetheless one of the most complete in American fiction, because the entire pressure of the book — the argument that survival is collective — is discharged in a single physical act. Catharsis without resolution, in one gesture.

2. Tolstoy, The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886) — catharsis by the removal of the thing feared. The novella has spent its length inside a dying man’s terror; it ends with the terror gone and, in place of death, light. The release is proportionate to the pressure, and the pressure was built by a structure — the death announced first, then the life retraced — that guaranteed the reader would arrive at the deathbed with nothing to hope for.

3. Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925) — catharsis withheld and then granted in four words. The novel ends: For there she was. Nothing has been resolved; Septimus is dead; the party is a party. The release is produced by the sentence’s grammar — a stated existence, in the plainest possible words, after a book of hesitations — and by its being Peter Walsh’s perception rather than Clarissa’s, so that the reader is finally permitted to see her from outside and to find her present. Four words, and the whole book’s accumulated longing discharges.

4.5 The Final Image

What the reader receives: the literal last thing the book shows them, which becomes the door they walk out through and the thing they will remember.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Note
The object What am I looking at? Concrete, previously established, and not explained
The gesture What are they doing? An action, preferably small and physical
The grammatical form of the last sentence Does this stop? Herrnstein Smith’s territory; §3.2
The last word What is the final noise? Rabinowitz’s privileged position at maximum concentration
The direction of attention Where am I looking — in, out, back, forward? The most underused variable in the domain
The person of the last clause Who is present at the end? I, we, they, or nobody

Discussion

The final image is a domain rather than a decoration because of privileged position (§3.6): whatever occupies the last sentence is read as a statement about the book. This has a consequence writers routinely underestimate. There is no neutral last sentence. A novel that ends on a character making tea has told the reader that tea is what the book was for. Sometimes that is right; the point is that it will be read that way regardless of intention.

Four variables are worth isolating, because they can be manipulated independently and most drafts manipulate none of them deliberately.

(1) Scale. Does the last image narrow to a detail or widen to a panorama? Beloved narrows to a single word. The Great Gatsby widens to a continent. Both close; they produce opposite after-images, and the choice should be made rather than inherited.

(2) Direction in time. Does the final sentence look back, stay in the present, or project forward? The proleptic close (§6.4, no. 8) — narrating a future beyond the book’s action — is a distinct strategy with distinct costs, and its opposite, the arrested present-tense close, is another.

(3) Grammatical closure. Herrnstein Smith’s unqualified assertion, applied. A last sentence that is short, declarative and unhedged closes harder than one that is long, subordinated and conditional; a last sentence ending on a strong stressed monosyllable closes harder than one ending on an unstressed syllable. These are not superstitions but observable regularities of English prosody, and they are why Bleak House’s final unfinished sentence and Ulysses’s final Yes feel so different in kind.

(4) Who is present. A final image containing the protagonist, one containing another character looking at the protagonist, and one containing nobody are three different endings. The third — the ending that outlives its people — is a distinct and powerful device: Wuthering Heights ends with Lockwood at three graves and the moths and the heath, and the withdrawal of the human is the closure.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the arbitrary stop. The last sentence is simply the next sentence, carrying no more weight than the one before it. Diagnosable by test 10 (§7.1). Its symptom is that the reader turns the page expecting more.
  • Over: the portentous image. A final image so heavily loaded that it announces its own significance — the symbol arriving with its explanation attached, or an image so composed that the prose visibly poses for a photograph. The rule is Chapter 4’s: an image can bear connotation only in proportion to the denotation it has established.

Illustrations

1. Joyce, Ulysses (1922) — closure by affirmation and by punctuation. The novel ends with Molly Bloom’s yes I said yes I will Yes. Almost every closural device Herrnstein Smith catalogues is active at once: unqualified assertion; a terminal word that means assent; capitalisation as a terminal modification of a text that has been resisting capitals; and the closural allusion of memory and sleep. §9.7 develops this.

2. Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) — the final image as a defeat, and its reversal by apparatus. He loved Big Brother. Four words, a stated fact in the past tense, no qualification, no hope. It is one of the most complete final images in English — and it is followed by an Appendix that, by its grammar alone, arguably undoes it (§4.6, §9.5).

3. Saramago, Blindness (1995; trans. Giovanni Pontiero) — the image withheld at the last moment. The novel ends with the doctor’s wife looking up at the sky and then, in the book’s final movement, at the city, in a formulation that declines to give the reader the confirming vision they have been waiting three hundred pages for. §9.9 argues that the withholding is the novel’s ethical position rather than a coyness.

4. Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847) — the image that removes the people. Lockwood at three headstones, wondering how anyone could imagine unquiet slumbers for the sleepers in that quiet earth. The closure is produced by the disappearance of everyone the book was about, by the transfer of the last word to its least reliable and least interested narrator, and by a question that is formally a question and functionally a full stop.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: Formal Closure

The five domains above are inherited from a broadly representational account of fiction: they concern the story — its questions, its people, its argument, its feeling, its last picture. But a reader arriving at the end of a novel is also completing something else. They are finishing an artefact: a physical and formal object with a pattern, and the pattern has its own terminus.

Formal closure. The completion, breaking or fulfilment of the book’s own structural pattern, as distinct from its story: the frame that returns; the repeated chapter shape that alters at its last instance; the final section that answers the first; the suppressed narrator who reappears; the apparatus that displaces the narrative’s authority; the typographic or paratextual device that is finally explained or finally withdrawn.

This is Chapter 9 §4.6’s formal contract falling due. That section argued that the opening pages teach the reader the book’s structural conventions — person and tense, temporal architecture, unit of division, plurality of narration, apparatus — and that a device introduced late reads as a gimmick because the contract was not declared. The contract opened there is closed here, and the closing has its own repertoire and its own failure modes.

Carriers

Carrier What is completed Example
The returned frame A narrator or vantage established at the start reappears at the end Frankenstein; Wuthering Heights; Heart of Darkness
The terminal modification of a repeated shape A pattern of chapters, sections or documents is altered at its last instance The Sound and the Fury’s fourth section abandoning first person
The answered first section The book’s last movement addresses its first directly Middlemarch’s Prelude and Finale
The suppressed narrator returning A voice withdrawn early comes back Madame Bovary’s vanished nous — and the celebrated fact that it does not return
The apparatus An appendix, note, index or editorial frame follows the narrative Nineteen Eighty-Four; The Handmaid’s Tale; Pale Fire
The withdrawal of a device A typographic or formal constraint is released Saramago’s punctuation; Faulkner’s italics
The completed count A numerical or sectional scheme reaches its designed end The seven-part structure of Anna Karenina answered by an eighth

Why it is distinct from thematic closure

The obvious objection is that formal closure is a species of thematic closure — that the form is an argument, and completing it completes the argument. The distinction the chapter maintains is this:

Thematic closure operates on the book’s argument. Formal closure operates on the book’s artefact. They are frequently aligned and are not the same, and the clearest evidence is that formal closure can contradict thematic closure within a single book.

Nineteen Eighty-Four is the demonstration, and it is the strongest single case for admitting the sixth domain. The narrative’s thematic closure is total and totally bleak: Winston is broken; the last line states that he loved Big Brother; the book’s argument about the survivability of the individual under total power is concluded in the negative. Then comes the Appendix, “The Principles of Newspeak” — and it is written in the past tense, by an evidently free scholar, for readers who are evidently not living in Oceania, in standard English, with footnoted reference to the narrative as a historical document.

Nothing in the story changes. No plot question is reopened. No character is rescued. But the artefact now contains a vantage from which Oceania is over, and the reader who notices the tense has been handed a hope the narrative explicitly denied them. This is not thematic closure — the theme was closed four pages earlier — and it is not plot resolution. It is a formal operation on the book as an object, and it is powerful enough that a significant body of criticism regards the Appendix as reversing the novel’s despair. §9.5 examines the argument and does not settle it.

The Handmaid’s Tale performs a related manoeuvre with the opposite valence. The Historical Notes place Offred’s account in an academic conference two centuries later, which formally guarantees Gilead’s fall — and simultaneously stages a male academic being facetious about a woman’s testimony, so that the formal consolation arrives inside a demonstration of how such testimony gets handled. Chapter 10 (The Prologue) analyses the Notes at §9.8 as a threshold arriving at the wrong end of the book; this chapter analyses the same device as a closural one. Both are true, which is why the device is so effective.

Discussion: the three modes of formal closure

(1) Completion. The pattern finishes as declared. A frame opened at the start closes at the end; a book in seven parts ends its seventh; an alternating structure alternates for the last time. Completion is the safest mode and the least remarked, because a satisfied formal expectation is invisible. Its value is nonetheless considerable: much of the sense of “rightness” readers report about endings they cannot analyse is formal completion doing its work below the level of notice.

(2) Modification. The pattern alters at its last instance — Herrnstein Smith’s terminal modification (§3.2). The Sound and the Fury runs three first-person sections and then, for the fourth, moves to third person and to Dilsey; the modification is the closure, and it is why the novel feels finished despite resolving very little. The mode’s danger is legibility: a modification the reader does not register as a modification is simply an inconsistency.

(3) Disruption. The pattern is broken by something the book has not prepared — an apparatus, a voice, a document — which reframes the whole. This is the mode with the largest effects and the highest costs, and it is where the domain earns its keep as a separate category, because disruption cannot be described as thematic closure: it works by changing what kind of object the reader is holding.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the unclosed form. A frame opened and never returned to; an epistolary novel that abandons letters; a book of alternating viewpoints that ends in one of them without acknowledgement. Chapter 10 §8.4 treats this as unresolved distance for prologues; the general case is the same. Note that under-delivery here is frequently invisible to the writer and perfectly visible to the reader as an unlocated dissatisfaction.
  • Over: the formal flourish. A closural device with no relation to the book — an appendix because appendices are interesting, a coda in another voice because the writer wanted to write one. The test is whether the device answers something the book opened.

The argument against a sixth domain

The chapter is obliged to make the case against its own addition, and there is a respectable one.

Position 1: five is enough, and the sixth is a category error. Formal closure is not a purpose an ending serves the reader; it is a means by which the other five are served. When Nineteen Eighty-Four’s Appendix reverses the novel’s despair, what it has done is thematic — it has altered the book’s final position on whether the Party can be survived. That the alteration was accomplished by a formal device does not make it a separate domain, any more than a metaphor is a separate domain because it is a linguistic device. The five domains name what the reader gets; formal closure names how. Admitting it confuses the model’s level of description, and a taxonomy that mixes ends and means loses its diagnostic value.

Position 2: it is separately real, and the five-domain model cannot name its failures. The reply is that the objection proves too much. Every domain is served by means; the question is whether the reader experiences the completion as belonging to the story or to the object. And there is a decisive test case: a book can achieve full thematic closure and produce, in a reader, a distinct and locatable dissatisfaction whose content is the form did not finish. An epistolary novel that ends in ordinary narration has closed its theme and left something conspicuously open, and the five-domain model has no vocabulary for the residue. Formal closure has distinct carriers (frames, apparatus, section schemes), distinct failure modes (the unclosed frame, the abandoned device), and distinct remedies (return the frame, or remove it from the opening). Those are the criteria by which Chapter 9 admitted its own sixth contract, and they are met here.

This chapter adopts the sixth for diagnostic purposes, while conceding that it sits at a different level of description from the other five and that a student who rejects it loses very little. Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class, with the Appendix to Nineteen Eighty-Four as the evidence.

4.8 Must all domains be served at once?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

1. The domains are genre-weighted, and the weighting is not a hierarchy of merit. A crime novel that fails plot resolution has failed absolutely, because the genre’s contract is an epistemic one; a lyric novel of consciousness may resolve nothing at all. The following distribution is descriptive, not prescriptive, and it is worth putting in front of students who have absorbed literary-fiction defaults as universal law:

Mode Load-bearing domains Frequently waived
Detective / mystery Plot; formal (the solution’s disclosure structure) Character arc; catharsis
Romance Plot; catharsis Formal; thematic
Thriller Plot; catharsis Thematic; formal
Literary realist Character; theme; final image Plot resolution, frequently
Modernist / lyric Final image; formal; theme Plot; sometimes character
Tragedy Character; catharsis; theme Plot resolution beyond the death
Epic / systems fiction Thematic; formal Character arc for any single figure

2. A domain may be served by deliberate refusal, and refusal is not absence. The distinction is the one drawn at §4.1: a question declined on the page has been served; a question dropped has not. The same holds in every domain. An ending that refuses catharsis, visibly, has addressed the catharsis domain.

3. The domains are not equal in size, and an ending that serves two well beats one that serves six thinly. This is the direct consequence of §5: multiplicity is the goal, and multiplicity means one carrier serving several domains, not several carriers each serving one.

4. The correct number of domains for a given ending is a question, not a target. The diagnostic form (§7.1, test 5) is not how many? but which, and did you choose? An ending serving three domains deliberately is in better condition than one serving five by accident.

4.9 Proposals from earlier chapters: rulings (second edition)

Four chapters of this edition left a proposed category for this one to judge, and the formal closure proposed in §4.6 is itself left to a later chapter.

Chapter 9’s formal contract: adopted at the scale of a book. Formal closure (§4.6) is the formal contract discharged. An unclosed frame, an abandoned device or an apparatus that answers nothing is a failure only where the opening declared a frame, a device or an apparatus, so the closing domain cannot be applied without the opening contract. The formal closure domain itself is adopted here for diagnosis, conceding that it sits at a different level of description from the other five; Chapter 18 (The Structural Edit, section 3.4) rejects it as a domain of the ending and adopts it at the scale of the book as the form audit, a test of the book’s form on the second rung of its revision ladder: both test cases pass the audit, and in both the formal pattern carries while the payment lies in another domain (the Appendix’s past tense is received as hope, the Six Feet Under montage as the series’ theme), so the objection holds at the ending and the defence holds for the audit.

Chapter 3’s silence domain: rejected as a domain of the ending, kept as a domain of the exchange. Its test case was to be the household mother’s unexplained silence. At the ending that silence is served by plot resolution, as a question visibly declined (§4.1): the reader is shown the three siblings reading a document that explains their father and says nothing of their mother. The siblings’ own silence as they read is a carrier of catharsis (§4.4). At this scale the objection Chapter 3 conceded, that silence is a means rather than a value that changes, holds; at the scale of a dialogue exchange, where a silent participant turns the exchange into a performance, the domain stands.

Chapter 6’s recasting close: not a separate category at a book’s last line. The recasting close is defined against the chapter ending that points forward; at the last line nothing comes next, so every close looks back and the category no longer discriminates. It remains a type of chapter ending. The question it leaves, how far a close may recast the whole book, from the parallel that adds a layer (strategy 2) to the retrospective reframe that changes what the book was (strategy 13), passes to Chapter 15 (The Reveal), which answers it with the teller rule: a close may falsify only what a teller in the book could have shaped.

Chapter 10’s last ordinary day: confirmed as a type of prologue. The household ending (§9.10) pays off the two objects of the father’s last ordinary day, the good pen and the unfinished In case, and leaves two of its details, the boy at the glass and the sign never turned to CLOSED, unredeemed. That is the type’s characteristic profit and its characteristic risk: a last ordinary day deposits more than an ending can spend.
