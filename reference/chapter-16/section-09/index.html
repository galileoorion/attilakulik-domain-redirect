Chapter 16 · The Ending

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Nine close readings of published endings, followed by the chapter’s capstone revision case. The analytical apparatus is the same throughout: identify the closural strategy or strategies (§6.4), audit the domains (§4), assess the multiplicity (§5), and — where the case permits — name the critical disagreement rather than resolving it.

The first reading is the longest, and deliberately. The two endings of Great Expectations constitute the best single case study available anywhere on this subject, because a major novelist wrote two endings for the same book, both survive, the circumstances of the change are documented, and a century and a half of competent criticism has failed to agree about which is better. Nothing else in the discipline lets a student watch the earned/unearned distinction, the congruence criterion, the tonal-match test and the imposed-ending question operate simultaneously on the same material.

9.1 Dickens, Great Expectations (1861) — the two endings

The situation

Great Expectations ran as a weekly serial in All the Year Round from December 1860 to August 1861. Pip, a blacksmith’s boy raised by hand, is given an anonymous fortune, believes it comes from Miss Havisham and is intended to secure him Estella, discovers that his benefactor is the convict Magwitch and that Estella is Magwitch’s daughter, loses the money, loses Estella to the brutal Bentley Drummle, nurses Magwitch to his death, is nursed in turn by Joe, and goes abroad to work in the merchant house of Clarriker.

The novel then has to end, and there are two published endings.

The original ending

Dickens’s first close, which he wrote and set in proof, is brief. Pip has returned to England after some years. Walking in London with little Pip — Joe and Biddy’s son, named for him — he encounters Estella in the street. She is a widow: Drummle has died, following an accident with a horse he had ill-treated. She has remarried, to a Shropshire doctor who had attended her during her marriage and who is poor. She takes little Pip for Pip’s own child. They exchange a few words and part. Pip’s closing reflection is that he was glad afterwards to have seen her, because in her face and voice he could see that suffering had been stronger than Miss Havisham’s teaching, and had given her a heart to understand what his heart used to be.

The intervention

As the serial was completing, Dickens showed the proofs to Edward Bulwer-Lytton — a friend, a fellow novelist, and the author whose next work was to follow Great Expectations in All the Year Round. Bulwer-Lytton objected to the ending and urged a happier one. Dickens rewrote it. The textual history is documented in the scholarly editions, notably Edgar Rosenberg’s Norton Critical Edition, and Dickens’s own account survives in his correspondence with John Forster: he recorded that Bulwer-Lytton was strongly of that opinion, that he had made the change, and that he had no doubt the story would be more acceptable for it.

Two further textual facts matter and are frequently omitted from teaching versions of this story.

First, there are more than two states. The revised ending appeared in All the Year Round with the final sentence reading I saw the shadow of no parting from her. For the one-volume edition of 1862 Dickens altered it to I saw no shadow of another parting from her — a small change with a real semantic consequence, discussed below. A student who speaks of “the two endings” is simplifying a textual situation with at least three relevant states.

Second, Forster preferred the original and printed it. John Forster reproduced the cancelled ending in his Life of Charles Dickens, which is why it survives at all in general circulation, and why the disagreement has been available to readers since the nineteenth century rather than being a discovery of modern textual scholarship.

The revised ending

Pip returns to England after eleven years. He goes to the site of Satis House, which has been demolished and sold; only the garden wall remains, and the garden. In the evening, in the mist, he finds Estella there. Drummle is dead; she has been “bent and broken, but — I hope — into a better shape”. They speak. She asks that they continue friends apart. They leave the ruined place together, and Pip closes:

I took her hand in mine, and we went out of the ruined place; and, as the morning mists had risen long ago when I first left the forge, so the evening mists were rising now, and in all the broad expanse of tranquil light they showed to me, I saw no shadow of another parting from her.

Structural comparison

Original Revised
Closural strategy (§6.4) 3 (tangent) and 11 (anticlimax): a chance street meeting, deliberately small 2 (parallelism) and 1 (circularity): the garden returned to; the mists of the forge answered by the mists of Satis
Location A London street; public, accidental Satis House garden; private, saturated
Estella’s condition Remarried, to a poor doctor; suffering has softened her Widowed, unattached, “bent and broken”
Contact A handshake, and parting Hands joined, and departure together
Formal closure (§4.6) None: the ending does not complete a pattern Strong: the mists frame the novel; the ruined garden answers the untouched one
Plot resolution Complete and flat Complete, with one question left grammatically open
Character arc Pip’s arc completes in his relief; he wants nothing Pip’s arc completes in his taking a hand he was never permitted to take
Thematic closure Expectations were false and are finished Contested — see below
Final image Two people separating in a street Two figures leaving a ruin in mist

The case for the original

The case is Torgovnick’s congruence criterion (§3.5) applied strictly, and it is strong.

The novel is a systematic dismantling of the idea that Pip’s expectations were ever real. Its whole argument is that the fortune was a delusion, that gentility was a fraud, that Estella was manufactured as an instrument, and that the only durable goods in the book are Joe’s forge and Biddy’s plainness. An ending that grants Pip the one thing he wanted, in the very garden where he was taught to want it, gives back at the last minute what five hundred pages removed.

The original ending, on this reading, is congruent and the revision is imposed. It leaves Pip with what the novel has actually earned him: work, a modest competence, an affection for another man’s child, and a moment of adult perception in which he can register Estella’s suffering without needing anything from her. It is also, formally, an anticlimax in the precise sense of §6.4 no. 11: the encounter is brief, accidental and unheightened, and the deflation is the point.

The advocates are distinguished. John Forster, Dickens’s friend and first biographer, preferred the cancelled version and printed it. George Bernard Shaw preferred it too, and used it in the edition he prefaced, arguing that the revision falsified the book; a good deal of twentieth-century criticism followed him. The general form of the objection is §8.4: a mismatched final note, produced by §8.12’s mechanism, external pressure.

The case for the revision

The case is subtler and, in the view of a good deal of modern criticism, stronger.

(1) The revision is more heavily planted. Test 2 favours it decisively. The revised ending uses the garden at Satis House, the mists, the ruin, and the phrase-shape of Pip’s first departure — all material the novel established at length. The original uses a street the novel has not established, a Shropshire doctor who does not exist until this page, and a horse. On the plant audit, the original is the ending that introduces new elements, and the revision is the one that closes accounts.

(2) The revision completes a formal pattern. The morning mists rose when Pip first left the forge; the evening mists rise now. This is textbook circularity and it is Herrnstein Smith’s terminal modification simultaneously: the same image at the opposite end of the day and of the book. The original completes no pattern at all.

(3) The revision is not, on close reading, happy. This is the point most often lost. Satis House is demolished. The garden is a ruin. Estella describes herself as bent and broken. Nothing is restored; two damaged people meet in the wreckage of the place that damaged them. And the final sentence is grammatically careful in a way the paraphrase “they live happily ever after” cannot capture:

I saw no shadow of another parting from her.

Pip sees no shadow. He does not say there is none. The sentence reports the limits of his perception in a novel whose entire subject is a man perceiving wrongly — and Dickens’s 1862 alteration from the shadow of no parting to no shadow of another parting sharpens this, by moving the negation from the parting to the shadow and inserting another, which concedes that there has already been one. A reader who has attended to five hundred pages of Pip being confidently wrong has every reason to distrust the sentence, and many readers do.

(4) The novel’s operative question may not be the one the original answers. Per §8.7: a novel’s stated question and its operative question frequently differ. Great Expectations states a question about money and class. What it develops, over its length, is a question about whether a person made instrumental by another can recover the capacity for attachment — a question about Estella, not about Pip’s fortune. The original ending answers it in report (Pip observes that suffering has changed her). The revised ending answers it in action (she asks to be friends; she takes his hand). On this reading the revision resolves the operative question and the original resolves the stated one.

What cannot be settled, and why the chapter refuses to settle it

The disagreement is not a matter of one side having overlooked evidence. It turns on a prior question about what kind of book Great Expectations is, and competent readers answer it differently:

If the novel is fundamentally… Then the congruent ending is…
A satire on gentility and expectation The original: Pip must be left with nothing he expected
A study of a damaged man learning to perceive Either, but the revision gives him something to perceive with
A romance whose obstacles are social and psychological The revision: the obstacles are removed and the pair remain
A tragedy of two people manufactured by others The revision, read pessimistically: two ruins in a ruin

There is also a methodological point the case establishes better than any other in the chapter, and it can be stated as a general principle:

The provenance of an ending is not evidence about its quality. That Bulwer-Lytton asked for the change tells us why the second ending exists. It tells us nothing about whether it is better. An ending written under pressure may be the one the book required, and an author’s first instinct may be a habit rather than a judgement. Congruence must be assessed by reading, not by biography.

What the case teaches

  • Test 2 and test 8 can disagree. The revision is better planted and more tonally suspect. When the plant audit and the tonal-match test conflict, no procedure resolves them; a judgement must be made about what the book is.
  • A tonal shift at the close may be a modulation rather than a failure. §8.4’s exception is this case, and the limit it establishes on the test is real.
  • Ambiguity can be produced by grammar rather than by content. I saw no shadow of another parting is a closed ending in its plot and an open one in its epistemology.
  • Endings can be revised without being repaired. Both versions are competent; the second is not a correction of an error in the first.

This is set as seminar question 1 at §12.2, and it is the best available assessment task in the chapter, because a student can be required to argue either side from the same evidence and will find both arguable.

9.2 Eliot, Middlemarch (1871–72) — closure by widening

Strategies: 6 (coda or finale), 8 (proleptic close), 10 (aphoristic close).

The structure

Middlemarch opens with a Prelude about Saint Theresa of Ávila and the difficulty faced by later women of ardent nature born into an age with no coherent social faith to give their ardour form. It closes with a section headed Finale, which reports the subsequent lives of the principal characters and then generalises.

The Finale is, by the standards of §8.5, an extreme tidy-everything ending. Fred and Mary are settled and their children accounted for. Lydgate acquires a prosperous practice, writes a treatise on gout, dies at fifty of diphtheria, and regards himself as a failure. Rosamond remarries, comfortably. Dorothea marries Will Ladislaw, bears a son, and is absorbed into the political life of a man of moderate distinction — an outcome the Finale describes without enthusiasm, noting that many who knew her thought it a pity that so substantial a nature should be known in a limited circle as a wife and mother.

And then the last sentence:

…for the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs.

Why the ending does not close by tightening

Nearly every ending discussed in this chapter closes by narrowing: to a gesture, an image, a word. Middlemarch closes by widening, and the widening is the mechanism.

Movement What happens to the frame
The individual settlements The frame is at its narrowest: named people, specific outcomes
“Her finely-touched spirit had still its fine issues, though they were not widely visible” The frame lifts from the person to the category of person
“the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts” The frame widens to history as such
“things are not so ill with you and me” The frame includes the reader — the only place in the novel where you and me appears in this position
“unvisited tombs” The frame closes on a place where nobody goes

The final phrase is Herrnstein Smith’s closural allusion (§3.2) at maximum strength — a tomb, unvisited, is about as terminal a referent as English possesses — and it arrives inside an unqualified general assertion, which is her other strongest device. Formally, this is one of the most heavily closed sentences in the language.

The domains

Domain How served
Plot Comprehensively, and with graded weight: Lydgate’s failure gets more space than Fred’s contentment, because it cost more
Character Dorothea’s arc completes as a diminishment that is not treated as one — the novel refuses both the triumph and the tragedy
Theme The Prelude’s question is answered directly: there is no Saint Theresa available, and the ardour goes into unrecorded acts
Catharsis Withheld from the characters and granted to the reader, in the last clause, by inclusion
Final image An unvisited tomb: nobody present, nothing to see
Formal Prelude answered by Finale. The book’s own structural frame closes (§4.6)

Two complications worth teaching

(1) The Finale was itself revised. Eliot altered the Finale after the first edition, softening a passage that laid the blame for Dorothea’s circumscribed life more squarely on the society around her. This is a second instance of the Great Expectations problem in miniature — a great novelist adjusting the emphasis of her own close — and it is worth putting beside §9.1 to establish that authorial revision of endings is normal rather than scandalous.

(2) The ending is D. A. Miller’s best evidence and its best refutation at once. On Miller’s reading (§3.9), the Finale is the containment he describes: Dorothea’s narratable ardour is disposed of into a marriage, and the novel’s disruptive energy is administratively closed. That reading is available and is not perverse.

But the Finale is also the strongest available counter-instance, because Eliot says so. The novel does not conceal the containment; it names it, regrets it, attributes it to “the conditions of an imperfect social state”, and closes on a sentence about lives whose value cannot be recorded. The narratable is not eliminated; it is declared to be unrecordable, which is a different operation. This is the pattern predicted at §3.9: the strongest endings close the book while leaving something still moving. What remains moving in Middlemarch is the question of what Dorothea’s ardour was for, and the Finale’s achievement is to make the reader feel that the question survives the answer.

9.3 Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925) — the last page

Strategies: 9 (terminal image), 10 (aphoristic close), 3 (tangent, mildly — the book leaves its subject entirely).

The movement

Gatsby is dead, the funeral has been attended by almost nobody, Tom and Daisy have retreated into their money, and Nick has closed up his affairs to go west. On the last night he goes down to the beach and lies on the sand. The final page performs a sequence of frame-shifts that repay being tabulated, because they are the mechanism.

Stage Frame
Nick on the beach; the houses melting away The present, one man
The island as the Dutch sailors first saw it — the fresh, green breast of the new world Three centuries back; a continent
The transitory enchanted moment, man face to face with something commensurate to his capacity for wonder Human capacity as such
Gatsby’s green light at the end of Daisy’s dock Back to the novel’s own central image
The orgastic future that year by year recedes Forward, indefinitely
So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past. Everyone, always

The last sentence is worth the same word-level attention §5.2 gives to Achebe:

Element Function
So Consequential; the sentence claims to follow from what precedes, which grants it the authority of a conclusion
we The pronoun shifts from Gatsby, and from Nick, to the reader and the species. This is the single largest move on the page
beat on Effortful, continuous, unrewarded; a verb of persistence rather than progress
boats against the current The novel’s water imagery — the Sound, the dock, the rain at the reunion — cashed in a figure
borne back Passive: the effort is real and the motion is backwards
ceaselessly The one polysyllable in the sentence; it slows the line at the point where the meaning is “without end”
into the past The last two words return the novel to its own subject — Gatsby’s attempt to repeat the past — and close on the noun the whole book has been arguing about

Note the prosody, which is not decorative. The sentence ends on past — a single stressed syllable, a hard consonant cluster, a full stop. Herrnstein Smith’s account of closural force in verse applies directly: a terminal stress closes harder than a terminal unstressed syllable, and English readers register this without knowing they do. Compare an ending on into the past again and the difference in finality is immediate.

The domains, and one instructive absence

Domain How served
Plot Served earlier, in the accounting of the funeral and of Tom and Daisy; the last page adds nothing
Character Nick’s arc completes in his decision to go west, several pages before the close
Theme Served entirely here, and by enlargement: Gatsby’s error becomes the national condition
Catharsis Granted by the change of pronoun. The reader is invited into the sentence
Final image Absolute
Formal The green light returns; the novel’s controlling image closes

The instructive absence is plot. The last page of The Great Gatsby resolves nothing whatever; every plot question was settled in the preceding chapter. This is the strongest available demonstration of §2.3.2’s distinction: resolution occurs earlier, closure occurs here, and the two are separable operations that the same book performs in different places.

The risk it runs

The page is a maximal instance of the aphoristic close, and it is worth teaching what licenses it, since a student attempting the same move will usually fail. Nick has been a generalising narrator from the novel’s first paragraph — his father’s advice, “reserving judgments is a matter of infinite hope” — and the book has established him as a man who thinks in propositions. Chapter 9 §4.5 analyses that opening as a retrospective promise: a narrator’s posture claiming that something happened which reorganised a life. The last page is that promise being honoured. A narrator who had not been established as a maker of general statements could not close this way; the aphorism would read as the author arriving.

9.4 Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878) — Part Eight

Strategies: 6 (coda), 8 (proleptic elements), and — structurally — the aftermath as a full movement.

The problem the ending solves

Anna dies at the end of Part Seven. The novel then continues for an entire further part, beginning some two months later, in which: Koznyshev’s six years’ book is published to indifference; the Serbian war fever produces a train of volunteers; Vronsky, half-destroyed, boards a train for Serbia with a toothache; and Levin, at Pokrovskoe, passes through a crisis of meaning that resolves in an exchange with a peasant about living for the soul, a storm, a fright about his infant son, and a private, unannounced arrival at faith. The book ends inside Levin’s consciousness, on the recognition that his life has an unquestionable meaning of goodness which he has the power to put into it.

By every contemporary standard this is §8.3, the endless aftermath, and it has been criticised as such since publication; Tolstoy’s serial publisher declined to print Part Eight, which appeared separately.

Why it is not a failure

The novel has two arcs, and Chapter 14 (The Subplot) supplies the vocabulary for what follows. Levin’s thread is not a subplot in the satellite sense; it is a second kernel, and the two never converge at the level of plot — Levin and Anna meet once, late, and the meeting changes nothing. What links them is that both are testing the same proposition about how a person is to live once the available social forms have stopped answering.

Two arcs cannot complete at the same climax. Anna’s completes at the station. If the book ended there, Levin’s thread would be a long, beautifully written appendage with no terminus, and the novel would collapse into what a century of abridgements have tried to make it: a book about an adulteress’s destruction.

The general principle the case establishes, and it is one of the most useful in the chapter:

An aftermath is not too long if it is completing an arc. It is too long if it is reporting consequences. Part Eight reports very few consequences of Anna’s death — that is what readers complain of — and completes an entire second arc, which is why it cannot be cut.

The mechanism of the transfer

What Tolstoy does structurally is a repeatable technique. Anna’s death is not the novel’s climax for Levin; it barely registers in his section. What Part Eight does is transfer the reader’s accumulated grief into a thread where it can be discharged, since Anna’s own thread admits no consolation. The catharsis available at the end of Part Seven is horror. The catharsis available at the end of Part Eight is something else, and it is only available because the horror preceded it.

Note also the deliberate coldness of the transfer. Vronsky’s last appearance is a man with toothache, going to be killed in someone else’s war, unable to think about anything. Tolstoy refuses the surviving lover any grandeur, and the refusal is what prevents Part Eight from reading as consolation.

The live disagreement

Whether the Levin thread converges is a real critical question and is set as seminar question 3 at §12.2. Two positions:

  • It converges thematically and not literally, which is the point: the novel’s argument is that these lives do not intersect, and the formal separation enacts it.
  • It does not converge at all, and Part Eight is Tolstoy attaching a religious conclusion to a novel that did not require it. On this reading, Levin’s arrival at faith is §8.6 — the explained ending — in its purest form, since a character does in fact spend the last chapters formulating what the book means.

The second position is stronger than students usually expect, and a seminar in which nobody argues it has not read Part Eight carefully.

9.5 Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) — the last line and the Appendix

Strategies: 9 (terminal image) and 10 (aphoristic close) for the narrative; 14 (appendix) and 3 (tangent) for what follows.

The narrative’s close

Winston, released, sits in the Chestnut Tree Café with gin and a chess problem. He has met Julia; each has betrayed the other; there is nothing left. A telescreen announces a victory in Africa. Something breaks in him, and the novel ends:

He loved Big Brother.

Four words. Consider what is packed into their form rather than their content. It is an unqualified assertion in Herrnstein Smith’s exact sense: declarative, past tense, no hedge, no subordination, no adverb. It is monosyllabic apart from the name. It ends on the name of the thing that has destroyed him, so the last word of the book belongs to the Party. And it is a statement of fact by the narrator, not a report of Winston’s belief — the free indirect narration that has been inside Winston’s head for three hundred pages here delivers a verdict about him from outside, which is the grammatical form of a man’s inner life being annexed.

By the domain audit: plot resolved (the Party wins), character arc completed as annihilation, theme closed in the negative, catharsis delivered as despair, final image absolute. Formal closure: none.

And then the Appendix

The novel is followed by “The Principles of Newspeak”, some twelve pages of expository prose on the structure and purpose of the language, its A, B and C vocabularies, and the intended completion of its adoption by 2050. It contains no narrative. It is written in standard English. And it is written in the past tense, by a scholar who quotes and analyses the Times of the period as a historical document, and who explains Newspeak’s principles to readers who evidently do not use it.

Nothing in Winston’s story changes. But the artefact the reader is holding now contains a vantage from which Oceania is over — a free scholar, writing afterwards, in a language the Party was designing Newspeak to abolish. The argument that the Appendix therefore reverses the novel’s despair has been made in various forms by later novelists and critics, Margaret Atwood and Thomas Pynchon among them, and it is one of the strongest cases in fiction for §4.6’s contention that formal closure is a distinct domain, because the operation is performed on the object and not on the story.

Three observations sharpen it.

(1) The reversal is accomplished by a tense. No content is required. This is §5.3’s third carrier — grammar as the cheapest closural instrument available — operating at the scale of a whole novel’s meaning.

(2) Orwell defended the Appendix’s presence. It is documented that his American book club asked him to cut the Appendix along with the Goldstein book, and that he refused, at a substantial commercial cost. Whatever he intended by it, he regarded it as load-bearing.

(3) The reading is contested, and should be. The counter-argument is that the Appendix is a piece of worldbuilding apparatus, that its tense is the ordinary tense of an encyclopaedia article, and that reading a fall of the Party out of it is a modern reader’s need for consolation projected onto a footnote. This position has the merit of noticing that the Appendix is placed after the novel — that is, outside it — and that Orwell’s title for it is descriptive rather than framing.

The chapter takes no side, but records the methodological point: the disagreement is possible only because formal closure and thematic closure are separable. If the two were the same domain, the Appendix could not contradict the last line, and the argument could not arise. Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts this to the class as the test case for the sixth domain.

A note on the pairing with The Handmaid’s Tale

Atwood’s Historical Notes perform the same structural operation with a different outcome, and the two make the best available paired teaching example.

Nineteen Eighty-Four Appendix The Handmaid’s Tale Historical Notes

Effect on the protagonist None; Winston is not mentioned Offred’s fate is investigated and cannot be determined
Effect on the reader Consolation, arguable Consolation, immediately withdrawn: the scholar is facetious about the testimony

Chapter 10 (The Prologue) analyses the Historical Notes at its §9.8 as a threshold arriving at the wrong end of the book. That analysis and this one are compatible: the same device is a displaced prologue and a closural apparatus, and it is effective because it is both.

9.6 Morrison, Beloved (1987) — the phrase that contradicts itself

Strategies: 4 (incompletion), 9 (terminal image), and a terminal modification (§3.2) executed on a sentence.

The close

The novel’s last chapter withdraws from scene into a choral register. Beloved is gone; the neighbourhood forgets her; her footprints by the stream appear and disappear; the story is described as one that everyone deliberately puts down. And a sentence recurs:

It was not a story to pass on.

It appears, is repeated, and then — in its final occurrence — arrives with its tense altered to the present. The novel then closes on a single word standing alone: Beloved.

The contradiction inside the phrase

The line does at least three incompatible things at once, and the incompatibility is the point.

Reading of pass on Meaning of the sentence What it does
To transmit, hand down This story must not be told Forbids the book that contains it
To decline, to refuse (as in cards) This story cannot be refused Requires the book that contains it
To die, to pass away This story will not die Neither forbids nor requires; asserts persistence

All three are current English. The sentence is therefore not ambiguous in the ordinary sense — it does not have a meaning the reader must guess — it is self-cancelling, and it is printed inside a four-hundred-page act of transmission that makes the first reading demonstrably false. The reader cannot settle it, and the inability to settle it is the closest a sentence can come to representing the novel’s subject: an event that must not be spoken about and must not be forgotten.

Two formal operations

The tense shift. The move from it was to this is is a terminal modification in Herrnstein Smith’s exact sense (§3.2): a pattern established by repetition is altered at its last instance. It also does something specific to the reader’s position. It was not a story to pass on describes the characters’ relation to the past. This is not a story to pass on describes the reader’s relation to the book in their hands. In three words the novel steps out of its own frame and addresses the act of reading it — and does so without any of the announcements a metafictional gesture usually requires.

The final word. The book ends on the name, alone, as a one-word paragraph. Consider what this does with the domains: it is simultaneously plot (the entity has been dispersed and the name survives), character (the unnamed baby finally has a word), theme (what remains of a person when the story is put down is the name on the headstone), catharsis (an address rather than a statement — the last thing the book does is speak to her), and final image (a word on a white page). It is also the novel’s title, which makes it a formal closure of the most economical kind available: the book ends by becoming its own name.

Note that this is the same device analysed in Chapter 9 §9.5, from the opposite end. Beloved opens with a number where a name should be — 124 was spiteful — and closes with a name and nothing else. The novel’s whole trajectory is legible in the movement from an address to a name, and the two privileged positions are doing it between them.

The disagreement worth staging

Beloved is the standard modern counter-instance to §8.9, ambiguity as evasion — and a sceptic can put the opposite case, which is worth hearing. The objection: the sentence’s undecidability is produced by a lexical accident of English, the reader who notices it is performing a critic’s operation rather than a reader’s, and the choral last chapter is a register the novel has not otherwise used, arriving after the story is over to supply an effect the story did not produce.

The reply, and it is strong: the choral register has been used — the unattributed monologues of the preceding section establish it — so the last chapter is a completion rather than an arrival; and the novel has been explicitly concerned with what can and cannot be said since its first page. But a seminar in which nobody puts the objection has not tested the ending.

9.7 Joyce, Ulysses (1922) — closure by affirmation

Strategies: 10 (aphoristic close, in an unusual mode), 9 (terminal image), 1 (circularity, at the level of the whole day), 14 (apparatus, in the smallest possible form).

The close

The novel’s final episode, “Penelope”, is Molly Bloom’s unpunctuated interior monologue, conventionally divided into eight great sentences. It closes with a recollection of Howth Head and Bloom’s proposal, and ends:

and yes I said yes I will Yes.

Then, below it, in most editions: Trieste–Zurich–Paris, 1914–1921.

Why this is the most technically interesting close in the chapter

Almost every closural device Herrnstein Smith catalogues is operating at once, in a text that has spent seven hundred pages refusing them.

Device How it operates here
Unqualified assertion Yes is the shortest possible unhedged affirmative; there is no more assertive word in English
Terminal modification The episode has run without capitals or full stops for forty pages. The final Yes is capitalised and followed by a full stop. The pattern is broken at its last instance, and the breaking is the closure
Closural allusion Memory, dusk, a hillside, a proposal, sleep. The referents are all terminal
Repetition with intensification yes… yes… Yes — the same word three times in eight words, rising
Circularity The last word of the book is the first word of the episode; the day that began with Bloom’s breakfast ends in his wife’s assent to having married him

And then the colophon. Trieste–Zurich–Paris, 1914–1921 is not part of the fiction; it is the author’s record of composition, and by long convention it is printed as though it were the last line. Its effect is a formal closure of the sixth-domain kind (§4.6): the reader is moved, without transition, from Molly’s consciousness in 1904 to the fact of a book having been made across three cities and seven years of war. The novel’s last gesture is to become an object.

The domain audit, and what is missing

Domain How served
Plot Barely. Nothing is resolved. Bloom has come home; Boylan has been; nobody has decided anything
Character Molly’s arc, such as it is, completes in a memory rather than an action; Bloom’s completes in her assent to him, which he does not hear
Theme Fully: the novel’s argument about ordinary life as sufficient subject is delivered by an unqualified affirmation of an ordinary marriage
Catharsis Very high, and produced almost entirely by rhythm and repetition rather than by event
Final image Howth, the rhododendrons, a proposal accepted
Formal Maximal: the pattern breaks, the day closes, the book declares itself made

Plot resolution is essentially absent, and Ulysses is nonetheless one of the most completely closed novels in English. It is the chapter’s cleanest single demonstration of §2.3.2: resolution and closure are separable, and a book may have all of the second and none of the first.

The caution

The strategy is close to unavailable to most books, and the reason should be given rather than leaving students to attempt it. The final Yes works because seven hundred pages have established a texture of relentless qualification, irony, interruption and doubt. An unqualified affirmative closes hard in proportion to the qualification it terminates. In a book whose prose has been affirmative throughout, the same word closes nothing. This is the general form of the point made about Eliot at §8.6 and about Fitzgerald at §9.3: a closural device is licensed by what the book has been doing, not by its own properties.

9.8 Cervantes, Don Quixote (Part II, 1615) — ending the character’s availability

Strategies: 8.8’s death (used as the exception it names), 5 (the frame closed), and a strategy no other book in this chapter attempts: closure as prohibition.

Quotations follow Edith Grossman’s translation (2003); the structural claims survive translation, the texture claims do not.

The close

In the last chapter of Part II, Alonso Quixano returns home ill, sleeps, and wakes sane. He abjures the books of chivalry, declares that he is no longer Don Quixote de la Mancha but Alonso Quixano the Good, makes his will — including a clause disinheriting his niece if she marries a man who reads books of chivalry — receives the sacraments, and dies. Sancho, weeping, begs him to get up and go looking for adventures.

Then the narrative apparatus takes over. The notary observes that he has never read of a knight errant dying so calmly and so Christianly in his bed. And Cide Hamete Benengeli, the fictional Arabic historian whose manuscript the whole novel purports to translate, addresses his own pen, hangs it up, and declares that for him alone Don Quixote was born and he for Don Quixote: the one to act and the other to write. He warns off the “false writer of Tordesillas” from disinterring the knight’s bones or attempting further adventures.

What is being closed

This is the only ending in the chapter that closes something other than a story. Consider what it actually accomplishes:

Level What ends
Plot Very little. The third sally has concluded; the plot has been episodic throughout and has no accumulating question to answer
Character Alonso Quixano recovers his wits — which is to say the character of Don Quixote ceases to exist before its host does. The madness ends, then the man
Theme The novel’s argument about reading and delusion completes: the cure is worse than the disease, and everyone present would rather have him mad
Catharsis Sancho’s plea to get up and try again is the release, and it is placed in the mouth of the character who has spent the book being sceptical
Formal The frame closes: the historian, the manuscript, the pen
The book’s future Closed by decree

The last row is the point. In 1614, between the two parts, a writer under the name Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda published a spurious continuation of Don Quixote. Cervantes’ Part II absorbs the insult into the fiction — his characters encounter people who have read the spurious continuation, and the itinerary is altered so that Don Quixote will not go where the counterfeit says he went — and the ending completes the operation. The character is killed, sanity is restored first so that no resurrection can be plausible, and the historian’s pen is hung up with an explicit prohibition attached.

This is an ending designed to prevent further text. It is the exact inverse of §8.11’s series hook, and it can be named as a distinct closural operation:

Closure by withdrawal of availability. The book ends not by resolving its questions but by removing the conditions under which more of it could be written. What is closed is not the plot but the character’s capacity to have further adventures.

Why this is the strongest exception to §8.8

§8.8 holds that death fails as an ending when it substitutes for a decision. Here the death is preceded by a decision — Alonso Quixano’s renunciation — and the death itself is almost an afterthought, reported briskly, with the will given more textual space than the dying. What the ending stages is a man choosing to stop being someone, and the novel’s grief is not that he died but that he stopped first.

The general principle: death closes a book when what dies is a possibility rather than a person.

A note on Part I

Part I (1605) ends with the opposite gesture: an explicit promise of a third sally and an invitation to whoever might write it. That invitation produced Avellaneda. The two endings of Don Quixote therefore constitute the discipline’s best natural experiment in the series hook (§8.11): the same author, the same character, one open ending and one closed, with a documented consequence in between. It is set as an exercise at §12.1.

9.9 Saramago, Blindness (1995) — the withheld confirmation

Strategies: 9 (terminal image), 4 (incompletion), and the withholding of a payoff the novel has spent its length promising.

Read in Giovanni Pontiero’s translation (1997). The structural claims below survive translation; the claims about the run-on syntax are claims about Pontiero as much as about Saramago, and the point is made in Chapter 9 §4.6 and again here.

The close

An unexplained epidemic of white blindness has emptied a city. Throughout, one character — the doctor’s wife — has retained her sight, concealed it, and used it to keep a small group alive at enormous personal cost. She has spent the novel expecting, sentence by sentence, to lose her sight too.

At the end, as arbitrarily as it came, sight returns to the population. The group recovers. And the doctor’s wife goes to the window and looks up — and what she sees is white: the whiteness that has been the novel’s image of blindness throughout. Her own turn appears to have come. Then she lowers her eyes, and the city is there.

Near this, in one of the novel’s most quoted exchanges, the characters agree that they do not think they went blind; they think they are blind, and always were: blind people who can see but do not look.

The mechanism: a payoff withheld and then converted

The novel has run on a single suspense: will she lose her sight? It is the book’s central pull-forward question, established early and maintained for three hundred pages. Every closural convention available says that this question must be answered.

Saramago answers it and immediately makes the answer worthless. She looks up and sees white — and the white is the sky. The reader’s dread is triggered, held for a clause, and dissolved into weather. What was going to be the novel’s largest plot payoff is spent on an optical joke, and in its place the book substitutes a thematic proposition that reframes the entire epidemic: sight was never the issue.

This is closure by substitution of question, and it is the constructive form of §8.7. The ending resolves a different question from the one the plot posed — and it works, where §8.7’s failures do not, because the substituted question is one the novel has been building in parallel from the beginning, and because the reader is shown the substitution happening rather than having the original question quietly dropped.

The domains

Domain How served
Plot Resolved perfunctorily and deliberately: sight returns, unexplained, as it left. The book refuses to make an event of it
Character The doctor’s wife’s arc completes not in relief but in the discovery that her sight was never the privilege she took it for
Theme Fully, and by explicit statement — the closest the novel comes to §8.6, licensed by a narration that has been sententious throughout
Catharsis Deliberately small. The book declines the release it has earned
Final image A woman at a window; a city; whiteness that turns out to be sky
Formal The typographic constraint — no quotation marks, no paragraphing of speech — is never released, which is itself a closural decision: the book does not return the reader to normal prose, because it does not think normality is available

That last row is worth dwelling on. Chapter 9 §4.6 analyses Blindness’s typography as a formal contract declared on the first page. The formal closure question is what happens to a declared constraint at the end, and Saramago’s answer is the least common one: nothing. The device is neither completed nor withdrawn. A book that has made its reader work for three hundred pages does not, at the close, hand the work back — and the refusal to release the constraint is the formal analogue of the refusal to grant the confirming vision.

Why this is not §8.9

Ambiguity as evasion requires that the reader be unable to say what is unresolved. Here they can say it in one sentence: it is not known why the blindness came or went, and it is not known whether the recovery will last. Both gaps are marked in the text, both are consistent with a novel whose subject is the arbitrariness of catastrophe, and neither could be closed without converting a parable into a plot.

9.10 The capstone revision case: the household ending, and the audit of a whole textbook

The nine readings above are of published fiction. What follows is the chapter’s own worked example, and it was also the first edition’s capstone: the household narrative that runs through every chapter, brought to its conclusion, with every element traced back to the chapter that planted it.

The case is the capstone for a reason that is structural rather than sentimental. §7.1’s test 2 — the plant audit — is the only test in this book that requires the whole work, and it has therefore been impossible to demonstrate until now. The earlier chapters have been depositing material. This section is where the deposits are checked.

9.10.1 The situation

A small shop. A father who kept its ledger in a careful, slanted hand with crossed sevens for eleven years — prices and repairs and the ordinary arithmetic of keeping a small shop alive. He died in March, after an illness he documented and told nobody about. Their mother changed the locks. Three adult children — Elena, who drives a truck and wants to know why; Nadia, who counts things and left the flat over the shop seventeen months ago for one with heating; and Dov, who held the doorway, gave Elena the key, withheld the reason, and has been quietly selling the shop — have spent the book reconstructing what happened from the objects he left.

The novel’s climax is the bounded viewpoint chapter analysed in Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) §9.9: the evening of March the ninth, Dov at the ledger, the good pen not where it should be, and the decision to go on writing. The ending follows it.

9.10.2 Version B: the ending the book has been building

The working text, corrected in the second edition in one clause (see §9.10.7, item 4):

They read it together, in the end — Elena at the register, Nadia on the stool, Dov standing because he said he’d rather. Their father’s hand ran clean and careful for eleven years of entries, prices and repairs and the ordinary arithmetic of keeping a small shop alive, and then, three pages from the end, a different kind of entry altogether: dates, a doctor’s name misspelled twice and corrected once, and beneath it, in the same careful hand as always, a list of what needed doing if he ran out of time to do it himself. He had known. He had not told them. He had, instead, done what he always did, which was write it down properly, in case anyone ever needed to check.

Dov’s hand picked up where his father’s left off on March the ninth, looser at first and then not. He hadn’t known what else to do with a ledger that had stopped making sense the way ledgers were supposed to.

It was Nadia who went up to the coat, in the end. It was hanging on the hook where nobody had moved it. She didn’t ask permission. She took it down, and after a moment, so the sleeves wouldn’t drag, she put it on.

9.10.3 The domain audit

Domain How it is delivered Carrier
Plot The father knew he was ill and documented it rather than telling anyone; Dov continued the ledger from the evening of 9 March The three pages from the end; the changed hand
Character Nadia takes down and wears the coat she has spent the book refusing to touch; in Chapter 7 her hand went no further than its pocket The coat, and the gesture of gathering the sleeves
Theme “In case anyone ever needs to check” recurs, now understood by everyone in the room including the reader The father’s own line, returned
Catharsis All three siblings physically together, in silence, for the first time in the book Bodies in one room; nobody speaking
Final image Nadia in a dead man’s coat, managing the sleeves The coat
Formal The ledger, the object the novel has been reading in fragments, is read whole and in company; the reading that began alone (Chapter 6’s stool) is completed by three people The ledger as the book’s structural spine

Six domains, three carriers. That ratio is the point of §5: the coat serves character, final image and catharsis simultaneously; the ledger serves plot, theme and formal closure; and the room serves catharsis by itself. Nothing in the passage is doing only one job, and no domain has a scene of its own.

The closural strategies (§6.4) are 2 (parallelism) — the coat returned to, the doorway exchange answered by a room in which nobody argues — and 9 (the terminal image). There is no coda, no epilogue, no proleptic close, and no explanation. The ending is covert in Torgovnick’s sense (§3.5): the last scene is formally indistinguishable from any other scene in the book, and it closes anyway.

Findings.

  • Nothing load-bearing arrives for the first time in the ending. Every item in rows 1–11 traces to a chapter, most of them to two or three. Row 18, the list of what needed doing, is the one element that appears first in the ending, and it is new material rather than new load: remove it and the ending still shows that he knew and wrote it down, on the strength of the dates and the doctor’s name (row 11). The ending contributes no material; it contributes a combination — three people, one room, one document read continuously — which is what §6.3 requires.
  • The two elements carrying the largest weight are the two most heavily maintained. The father’s line (row 2) appears in two chapters before the ending; the coat’s untouchability (row 9) appears in two, and Chapter 7 tests it by letting her hand go no further than the pocket. This is not accidental, and it is the practical form of §6.2’s rule: the plants that matter most should be the ones that have passed as ordinary the longest.
  • Three plants are unredeemed (rows 15–17). One is deliberate and defended below. Two are honest debts, and a textbook that concealed them would be teaching the wrong lesson. Chapter 10 predicted the failure of row 16 in advance, which is a point in favour of the diagnostic protocol and not a defence of the ending.
  • Row 5 deserves a note, because it looks like a violation and is not. Dov’s decision is deposited only one chapter before the ending. That would be fatal if the ending disclosed it — the reader would be receiving load-bearing information at the close (test 3). It does not. The reader already knows; what the ending supplies is the sisters finding out, and the effect is dramatic irony discharging rather than information arriving. The audit question is not “when was this deposited?” but “does the ending depend on the reader learning it here?”

9.10.5 Version A: an ending that resolves by a new element

The following is a deliberately weak alternative, written to fail in an instructive way. It is a counterfactual and is not part of the household canon; in particular, the mother’s account of herself given below is invented for the purpose of being rejected, and the household narrative does not contain it.

The letter came on a Tuesday, forwarded twice, and it was Elena who opened it. Whitlock & Ames, Solicitors, regretted to inform the family of the late proprietor that the deeds to the premises had been placed in trust the previous February, and that a copy of the medical report lodged with them at that time was enclosed for the beneficiaries’ attention.

She drove out to her mother’s that evening. Ma let her in without being asked and put the kettle on and stood with her back to it while Elena read the report aloud; and when it was finished she said that she had known since January, that he had made her promise, and that changing the locks had been the only way she could think of to stop the three of them getting into the ledger before she had decided what to tell them. She was sorry. She had not known how to say it. She was saying it now.

Nadia came at nine and they told her, and Dov drove over at eleven, and by midnight there was nothing any of them did not know.

Diagnosis.

2 — Plant audit Fails. The solicitors, the trust, the medical report and the February date are four load-bearing elements deposited nowhere
3 — Earned Fails on all three of §7.1’s severities at once. New information (the report); new agent (the firm); and — worst — the resolution is delivered by someone outside the book’s causal system

The mechanism. Version A commits a single error from which the rest follow: it makes the novel’s question what happened? rather than what do these three do with what they can reconstruct? Those are different books. The first is answerable by a document. The second is answerable only by the siblings, and only in each other’s presence.

Once the question is misidentified, everything else is forced. If the answer can arrive in the post, the ledger is redundant. If the ledger is redundant, Dov’s continuation of it is a curiosity rather than a confession. If the mother explains herself, the family’s central silence is dissolved by exposition. And if everything is known by midnight, there is nothing left for anybody to do — which is why Version A cannot produce a final image: an ending needs an act, and Version A contains only receipt.

Note also what Version A does to the material it does use. The changed locks, planted in Chapter 3 as an unexplained act of withholding, are converted into a practical measure with a stated purpose. The plant is not merely unredeemed; it is spent, in the way Chapter 10 §9.9 describes its own Version A spending the good pen. A wrong ending does not simply fail to pay its debts. It cancels them.

9.10.6 What the ending does, procedurally

Set against Version A, the working ending can be described as a series of decisions rather than as an inspiration.

  1. It answers the operative question, not the stated one (§8.7). The stated question — why were the locks changed — is not answered. The operative question — can these three be in a room together — is answered by putting them in one.
  2. It resolves by combination, not by material (§6.3). Every element is old. What is new is that the ledger is read continuously, by all three, in the shop. The novel has shown the ledger four times and always in fragments, always alone.
  3. It uses a single carrier for three domains (§5.3, carrier 1: the object already in the room). The coat closes character, final image and catharsis.
  4. It completes the arc by a repeated situation with the terms altered (§4.2). Nadia has stood in front of that coat since Chapter 4 and not taken it down; in Chapter 7 her hand goes no further than the pocket. She takes it down. Nobody says anything about it.
  5. It preserves the constant. So the sleeves wouldn’t drag is Nadia measuring, adjusting, managing — the behaviour that has characterised her since Chapter 9’s opening. The change is legible because the constant is visible in the same clause.
  6. It ends on a decision, not a consequence (§6.6). Putting on the coat is the last decision anybody takes, and the passage stops within a sentence of it.
  7. It refuses to explain (§8.6). “He had known. He had not told them.” Three facts, no interpretation. The reader performs the inference that a lesser ending would perform for them.
  8. It lets the father speak last, through a phrase, without a scene. Write it down properly, in case anyone ever needs to check is returned as the explanation of an act rather than as a moral, and it is the only generalisation in the passage — licensed because it is quoted from a dead man rather than asserted by a narrator.

9.10.7 What Version B has not solved

Six reservations, offered because a capstone that ends in triumph would contradict everything in §16.

1. The mother is unresolved, and this is a choice with a cost. She changed the locks in March and the novel never says why. She has no scene of her own in the book. The ending does not approach her; the three siblings read a document that explains their father and says nothing about their mother, and the book stops.

The defence is that her silence is the novel’s last piece of narratable material, deliberately preserved — the pattern §3.9 predicts of endings that close a book while leaving something moving. The novel is about what a family withholds from itself; an ending that closed every withholding would have argued that withholding is a solvable problem, which is what the book denies. There is also a structural defence: the reader now possesses, from the ledger, an explanation of the father’s silence, and is therefore in a position to extend the same charity to the mother’s without being told to. The unexplained silence beside the explained one is the ending’s most economical thematic act.

The cost is real and should be named. A reader may reasonably close the book feeling that the largest question has been sidestepped, and that the ending has resolved the tractable withholding and declined the intractable one. Chapter 13 §9.9 reached the same verdict from the viewpoint side — it solves Dov and leaves the mother — and a reader entitled to ask why always the siblings has Eliot’s authority for the question. The honest statement is that this is an allocation, not a discovery, and a different novelist would write the other book.

2. Two prologue plants are left on the table. The boy at the glass and the unflipped sign (rows 16 and 17) are deposited in a privileged position and never redeemed. Chapter 10 predicted the first would be fragile because a person is harder to recognise than an object. By the promise-inventory logic Chapter 9 applies to openings, these are unredeemed promises, and the remedy is either a second appearance earlier in the book or deletion from the prologue. Chapter 11 proposes the second appearance (see 9.10.8).

3. The passage may be over-determined (§5.4). Every noun in it means something: the ledger, the hand, the dates, the doctor’s name, the coat, the sleeves. There is no inert material — no bus going past, no radiator — and the audit at §5.5 would return “no inert sentences at all”. Whether that reads as concentration or as arrangement is a legitimate editorial disagreement, and it is worth staging in a workshop. The available correction is a sentence that means nothing, placed between the ledger and the coat.

4. The move to the flat is made in a clause. The first edition read Nadia was the one who found the coat, which imported a discovery the situation does not contain, since she has known where the coat is for four months; the second edition reads It was Nadia who went up to the coat. The correction exposes a smaller problem: the three read in the shop and the coat hangs in the flat above it, so the last paragraph changes place in a single clause, and a reader may stumble on stairs the sentence does not mention.

5. Elena receives least. She is the investigator; she drove to the shop, opened the drawer, found the changed hand, and drove the book’s whole forward motion. In the ending she is at the register, reading. Her arc completes by presence rather than by act, which is the weakest form of completion in §4.2’s table. The defence is that she has already had her decision — in the truck, in Chapter 2’s sequel — and that a novel cannot give three characters a final act without diluting all three. The objection is that the reader has spent more time with her than with anyone. Chapter 12 proposes an act for her at the convergence (see 9.10.8).

6. The ending is covert, and covert endings fail test 7 more often than overt ones. There is no formal signal that this is the last scene: no coda, no time-shift, no change of register, no terminal modification. A reader may reach the last line and turn the page. The counter-argument is that the coat is the signal — that an object withheld for the length of a book, finally taken, is as strong a terminal marker as a formal device — and that the book’s whole method has been to let objects do the work that a more explicit novel would give to structure. But it is a risk, and it is the risk this ending takes.

9.10.8 The plant audit rerun across the second edition

The audit in 9.10.4 was run against the first edition, whose twelve chapters were the only depositors. The second edition adds six chapters, and each of them did two things to the household material: it deposited new elements, and it proposed a way to pay a debt that the first audit had recorded. An audit of the ending that ignored them would certify a book that no longer exists, so this section runs test 2 (§7.1) again, over every element any of the eighteen chapters deposits, and asks of each the question 9.10.4 ends on: does the ending depend on the reader learning it here?

Two terms need fixing first. An element is paid where the book uses the load it carries, and maintained where the book returns to it before the payment without using it. A payment marked proposed rests on a revision that one of the new chapters makes in its household case and leaves for the author to confirm; nothing in an earlier chapter’s text is changed by it. The proposals are in the household cases of Chapter 8 (the arrangement of times), Chapter 11 (the middle), Chapter 12 (Elena’s arc), Chapter 15 (the reveal of the illness), Chapter 17 (the book’s genre) and Chapter 18 (the edit of the whole), each in section 7, and in the subplot case of Chapter 14, section 9.9. Findings. The thirty-two rows fall into four groups. Sixteen are paid in the working texts of the chapters (rows 1 to 9, 11, 13, 14, 17 and 23 to 25). Nine are paid only by a proposed revision (rows 15, 21, 22 and 26 to 31). Three belong to what the book declines rather than pays (rows 18, 20 and 32). Four carry no load: the cuff, which is present in the object and never named; the list, the one element that appears first in the ending; the stool; and the till counted twice, a seed that nothing grows from. The sum is 16 + 9 + 3 + 4 = 32.

The first finding of 9.10.4 survives the rerun. Nothing load-bearing arrives for the first time in the ending, and the list (row 12) is still the only element that does, kept there because no earlier chapter could show it without a sister reading the illness entries or Dov reading back. The six new chapters deposited nine elements (rows 25 to 32 and the extension of row 18), and every one of them is paid at or before the reading, most of them at the convergence. None of them is spent in the ending’s three paragraphs, which 9.10.2 gives unchanged.

The first audit’s three unredeemed plants have changed status. The changed locks (row 20) remain unredeemed by design, but the second edition gives the decline its signs, which the first edition lacked: Dov’s refusal in the doorway, and three decisions of Elena’s not to ask, in the truck (row 32), at the empty drawer (row 27) and at the convergence, where she rings Nadia rather than her mother. Chapter 17 argues that a book in the mystery mode may decline the mystery’s solution only if it declares the decline, and these are the declaration. The boy at the glass and the sign (rows 21 and 22), which 9.10.7 item 2 left as debts, are paid by the scene Chapter 11 proposes at the empty drawer; if the author rejects that scene, they return to the table, and the remedy remains deletion from the prologue.

The rerun also finds a cost the first audit could not see. The convergence, Dov bringing the ledger to the shop, now pays five rows (25, 27, 28, 29 and 30) and the sale besides, in a scene that the first edition did not contain. The ending’s own passage is no more crowded than it was, but the scene before it is the most heavily loaded in the book, which is the over-determination that 9.10.7 item 3 found in the last paragraphs, moved one scene earlier. Chapter 12 records the crowding as unsolved, and the rerun agrees.

The brushing of the coat (row 18) was the one element the first audit did not list at all, since the first edition’s ending does not use it. Under the mystery mode that Chapter 17 identifies, an unexplained weekly act on a dead man’s coat reads as a clue, and a clue never paid is a question of fact declined without a sign. Chapter 18 proposes the sign: Nadia says that Dov thinks she brushes the coat, she does not, and neither sister asks the next question. The row moves from debt to decline, at a price Chapter 18 names: the sign makes the person who brushes the coat inferable by elimination, and the inference points at the mother.

Three objections of 9.10.7 are answered in part by the new chapters. Item 2, the two prologue details, is answered by Chapter 11’s scene, as above. Item 5, that Elena completes by presence, is answered by Chapter 12’s decision at the convergence: she lays her palm on the ledger, does not open it, and rings Nadia, so that her completion is an act, the change of her rule from find out for yourself to what concerns the three of them is read by the three of them. Item 6, the covert ending, is unchanged, and so are items 1 and 4: the mother stays unresolved, and the move from the shop to the flat is still made in a clause.
