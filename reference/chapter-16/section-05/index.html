Chapter 16 · The Ending

5. Multiplicity: The Economy of the Ending

Continue in Core

5.1 The principle, stated as a constraint

Multiplicity — the practice of making a single unit of text discharge several narrative functions at once — is introduced in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) and recurs in every chapter of this textbook. At the ending it operates at its highest stakes, and for reasons opposite to those that govern the opening.

Chapter 9 argued that multiplicity is an arithmetical necessity in the opening pages, because the opening has the fewest words, the most simultaneous obligations and the least reader patience. None of those constraints applies at the close. An ending may be long; the reader has already decided to finish; and the obligations, while numerous, could in principle be discharged serially across a final chapter of any length.

The constraint at the ending is different in kind, and stating it correctly is the whole of this section:

At the ending, multiplicity is not an economy of space. It is the difference between a conclusion the reader watches and one they undergo.

An ending that works through the domains in sequence — the confrontation, then a chapter in which the protagonist reflects and is seen to have changed, then a passage in which the theme is stated, then a final image — is not too long. It is legible as a procedure. The reader can see the machinery, because the machinery has been laid out end to end where anyone can watch it operate. And the reader’s response to visible machinery at an ending is not boredom but something worse: the retroactive suspicion that the preceding four hundred pages were also machinery, which is the retroactive damage §1.1 describes.

The usual form of this point is a warning against treating the five domains as a checklist. The stronger form of the claim, and the one this section argues:

The strongest endings close several accounts in a single image rather than working through the domains in sequence — and the reason is not efficiency but simultaneity. When plot, character and theme resolve in the same gesture, the reader cannot separate them, and what they experience is not three resolutions but one meaning.

This is why so many of the most admired endings in fiction are short. The last page of The Great Gatsby; the last four words of Mrs Dalloway; the last sentence of Things Fall Apart; the last word of Beloved. It is not that brevity is a virtue at endings. It is that an ending which has found the single carrier does not need length, and one that has not cannot be rescued by it.

A corollary for revision, which inverts the instinct:

A long ending is usually a symptom, not a problem. When an aftermath sprawls, the cause is almost always that no single carrier has been found and the domains are being served one at a time. Cutting the aftermath does not fix this; it produces a short ending that still serves the domains serially. The fix is upstream: find the object, gesture or image capable of carrying more than one account, and let the others go.

5.2 Anatomy of a multiplying ending

The clearest available demonstration in a single paragraph is the last movement of Achebe’s Things Fall Apart (1958). Okonkwo has hanged himself; his people cannot cut him down, since a man who takes his own life is an abomination; the District Commissioner arrives, listens, reflects on the book he is writing, and the novel closes:

He had already chosen the title of the book, after much thought: The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger.

The novel does not end with Okonkwo. It ends with the sentence in which a colonial administrator names his own book. Dissected:

Textual element Domains discharged
He — the last pronoun of the novel belongs to the Commissioner Formal (the vantage has been captured); thematic (whose account survives?)
had already chosen — the decision precedes the events; the pluperfect Character (a man for whom Okonkwo’s death was material); plot (the outcome was settled before the encounter)
after much thought Character (self-regard); catharsis — the reader’s fury has a target, which is the release the novel’s final movement has been withholding
The Pacification Thematic (the word that converts destruction into administration)
Primitive Tribes Thematic (the register that will replace the one the reader has spent the book inside)
of the Lower Niger World / final image (the specific place, reduced to a geographical category); formal (a title inside a title)
The paragraph’s scale — Okonkwo’s whole life is contemplated as a paragraph Plot (Okonkwo’s arc is closed by being summarised); formal (the novel demonstrates the closural form that would have contained it)

Every domain is touched, in one sentence, and the sentence performs no visible closural work at all: no summary, no reflection, no image of finality, no statement of theme. It is a man deciding on a title.

Three observations for the classroom.

(1) The multiplicity is achieved by a change of vantage, not by compression. Achebe has not packed six things into a sentence; he has found the one position from which one thing means six. This is the distinction Chapter 9 §5.1 draws between compression and selection, at the largest scale it can operate.

(2) The domains are served in a way that would be impossible serially. Suppose Achebe had written the same content as four separate passages: Okonkwo’s death; a passage on the loss to the clan; a passage on the Commissioner’s indifference; a closing statement about colonial historiography. Every element would survive and the effect would be gone, because the effect depends on the reader discovering the four things simultaneously in a single flat sentence and having nowhere to put the resulting anger.

(3) The multiplication is retrospective. None of this is legible in the sentence itself. It is legible only because the reader has spent two hundred pages inside the world the Commissioner is about to describe in a paragraph, and it is therefore an exact demonstration of the retroactive lens: the ending confers meaning on the preceding book, and the preceding book confers meaning on the ending, in one operation.

5.3 Where closural multiplicity actually lives

Novice revision looks for multiplicity in symbol, on the assumption that the last page is where a book’s images should become significant. In practice, the multiplying work at an ending is done in four humbler places, none of them figurative.

1. The object already in the room. The most efficient closural carrier available is a physical thing the book has established, used, and left lying about. It carries plot (what it is), character (whose relation to it is at issue), theme (what it has come to mean) and image (what the reader is looking at) without a single word of explanation, because all four meanings were deposited earlier. The coat in §9.10 is this; so is Queequeg’s coffin in Moby-Dick’s epilogue, which is a plot device, a character’s gift, a thematic statement about the uses of death, and the final picture, all at once, and which was built as a coffin in chapter 110, sixteen chapters before it is caulked as the ship’s life-buoy.

2. The gesture the book has shown before. A repeated action, performed differently, discharges character (the change), plot (the situation has altered), and formal closure (parallelism) simultaneously. It is the most reliable device in the repertoire because the comparison is done by the reader, which means the writer never has to state it.

3. Tense, aspect and person. The cheapest closural instrument in the language, and the most consistently overlooked. A shift from past to present, from present to past, from I to we, from a character’s name to a pronoun or from a pronoun to a name, will alter a final paragraph’s whole relation to time and to the reader without adding content. Nineteen Eighty-Four’s Appendix is a formal reversal accomplished, in the last analysis, by a tense. Chapter 9 §5.3 makes the same observation about openings (had once been; was to remember): grammar is the least expensive worldbuilding available, and it is also the least expensive closure.

4. Who is given the last position. The choice of which consciousness, or which voice, occupies the final paragraph is a structural decision that most drafts make by default and that the strongest endings make deliberately. Giving the close to the protagonist, to a witness, to a minor character, to a document, or to nobody produces five different books. Flaubert gives it to Homais; Achebe to the Commissioner; Brontë to Lockwood; Orwell to a lexicographer. In each case the assignment is the argument.

Symbol can multiply — Rose of Sharon in The Grapes of Wrath does — but it is the highest-risk instrument available at a close, for the reason Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) gives about connotation generally: an image that overreaches its established denotation draws attention to the author. At an ending, where every element is being read as a statement about the book, the attention is fatal.

5.4 The ceiling: the over-determined ending

Multiplicity has an upper bound at the close as it does at the opening, and the failure at that bound has a distinct character.

The opening’s ceiling failure is strain: too much information per sentence, no rests, no emphasis (Chapter 9 §5.4). The ending’s ceiling failure is different, and worse, because it is a failure of credibility rather than of comprehension.

The over-determined ending is one in which every element visibly means something. The weather participates. The object is a symbol. The minor character returns to close a rhyme. The last line answers the first. Nothing is present that is not doing work.

Three symptoms mark it:

Symptom What the reader experiences
No inert material Every noun in the final pages has been assigned a function; the world has stopped existing independently of the meaning
Convergence without cause Threads arrive together because the ending requires it, not because anything made them
The audible click The reader can hear the book locking shut, and the sound is the author’s

The mechanism is the one Barthes identifies in “The Reality Effect” (1968), running in reverse. Barthes’s argument is that certain details signify nothing except reality itself, and that their apparent uselessness is what authenticates a fictional world. An ending in which nothing is useless has withdrawn that authentication at the exact moment the reader is deciding whether the book was true.

The remedy is counter-intuitive, and it is stated here as a rule because it is the opposite of what a writer polishing an ending will instinctively do:

An ending needs some material that means nothing. A bus going past. A radiator. The stool somebody sits on. These are not padding; they are the evidence that the world is not being operated. An ending assembled entirely from significant elements does not read as meaningful; it reads as arranged.

The limit case is instructive. Middlemarch’s Finale is, by any measure, an over-determined ending: it settles every major figure, generalises about human significance, and closes on a sentence of enormous rhetorical weight. It nonetheless does not read as arranged, and the reason is that Eliot has spent eight hundred pages establishing a narrator who does this all the time. Over-determination is a failure of consistency rather than of quantity. A book whose narration has been generalising throughout may end on a maxim; a book that has been rigorously scenic may not.

5.5 Exercise: the closural audit

A practical procedure, and the most reliable teaching instrument in the chapter’s first half. It is deliberately mechanical, and its output is a distribution rather than a verdict.

  1. Print the last three pages of the draft, double-spaced.
  2. In the margin beside each sentence, write the letters of every domain it discharges: P (plot), C (character), T (theme), E (emotional catharsis), I (final image), F (formal).
  3. Mark, separately, every sentence that discharges nothing — the inert material of §5.4.
  4. Count.

Interpretation:

Pattern Diagnosis First move
Most sentences carry one letter, and the letters appear in blocks The ending is serial. The domains are being worked through in sequence and the reader can see it Look for a single carrier — object, gesture, or vantage — that could take two of the blocks. Delete one block entirely and test whether it is missed
One sentence or image carries four or more The spine has been found Build the close around it; consider moving it later; check that nothing after it is anticlimactic
No inert sentences at all Over-determined (§5.4) Restore two or three sentences that mean nothing
More than a third inert The aftermath is running on residual momentum Locate the last sentence that discharges a domain and test whether the book ends there
A column is empty across all three pages A domain is unserved Deliberate refusal, or oversight? (§7.1, test 5)
The letters are dense in the first of the three pages and thin in the last two The ending has already happened and the book is still going §8.3; consider whether the real last line has been passed

The last row is the exercise’s most valuable single output. In student drafts, by this book’s estimate rather than any count, the true ending is often two to four pages before the printed one, and the audit locates it without argument.
