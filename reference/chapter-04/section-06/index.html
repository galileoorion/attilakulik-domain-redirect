Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

6. Denotation and Connotation

Continue in Core

6.1 The two layers

Every descriptive detail operates simultaneously on two layers, and the distinction between them is the mechanism by which selection becomes meaning.

Working mechanism.

Denotation — the literal referent: what the detail names, plainly, and what any competent reader of the language would agree it names.

Connotation — the freight: the mood, class signal, foreboding, moral judgement or thematic resonance that arrives because this true thing was said rather than another equally true one.

The classic demonstration is a teacup. A chipped teacup and a bone-white teacup can denote the same object; the cup may be both chipped and pale. Neither report is more accurate than the other. But the first connotes neglect, or poverty, or a small grief nobody has got round to attending to; the second connotes fragility, formality, and something about to break. The writer’s choice of which true attribute to report is where the meaning enters the sentence — and a detail that carries nothing beyond its denotation (the cup was a teacup) has done the minimum the sentence required and nothing more.

The pair is the exact analogue, at the level of description, of text and subtext in Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange). A line of dialogue that means only what it says is flat for the same reason a detail that means only itself is flat: the reader is given a fact and no work. Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) reuses the pair again, for the flashback that must carry a present-tense charge.

The technical vocabulary is worth acquiring, because it makes the mechanism precise rather than atmospheric. In the semiotic tradition — Hjelmslev’s, taken up by Barthes in Elements of Semiology and applied to narrative in S/Z (1970) — connotation is a second-order system: the whole first-order sign (chipped teacup, signifier plus signified) becomes the signifier of a further signified (neglect). Two consequences follow that a purely intuitive account misses.

  • Connotation is cultural, not natural. It is supplied by the reader’s repertoire, not by the object. A chipped cup connotes poverty in one culture and wabi in another; a barometer connoted provincial bourgeois respectability to Flaubert’s first readers and connotes almost nothing to a reader now. This is the source of the historical volatility discussed at §6.6, and the reason connotative effects age faster than structural ones.
  • The first order is not thereby cancelled. The chipped cup remains a cup. A detail that ceases to be plausibly present in the room in order to carry its freight has become an emblem, and the reader will feel the object leave the world (§8.12).

6.2 Selection is already argument

The layered account lets the chapter’s first principle be stated in its sharpest form.

If a room contains ten thousand true facts and the writer reports three, the reader — who has no access to the room except through those three — necessarily treats them as the salient ones. That is not a mistake on the reader’s part; it is the only possible reading protocol, and it is what Rabinowitz’s rules of notice (developed in Chapter 9, The Opening Pages, §3.2) describe. It follows that:

Every act of description makes a claim about what matters here, whether or not the writer intended to make one. There is no neutral inventory. An unselected list is not neutral; it is merely undirected — and the reader will read undirectedness as a claim too.

This is why the inventory passage (§8.1) reads as flat rather than as objective. The reader looks for the principle governing the selection, finds none, and concludes — correctly — that the writer was not choosing. Flatness is the affect produced by a claim with no content.

Two scholarly traditions have formalised this from opposite directions, and it is useful to hold both.

Description as subordinate. Genette, in “Frontières du récit” (in Figures II, 1969), argues that description in narrative has almost always been the servant of narration — ancilla narrationis — tolerated to the extent that it is functional, and that classical rhetoric therefore had no independent theory of it. Philippe Hamon’s work on descriptive discourse (from “Qu’est-ce qu’une description?”, 1972, to Introduction à l’analyse du descriptif, 1981) takes the opposite tack and treats description as a system in its own right, with its own lexical constraints, its own predictable expansions, and its own devices for being motivated — the window a character conveniently stands at, the pause while a character waits, the expert who happens to be present. Hamon’s insight, in a craft register, is that description is nearly always given an alibi, and that the quality of the alibi is a large part of what separates absorbed description from an authorial aside.

Description as ideology. Lukács (“Narrate or Describe?”, 1936) and Barthes (“The Reality Effect”, 1968) both read description as doing work that is not narrative at all — for Lukács, converting participants into spectators; for Barthes, concealing the text’s constructedness. Neither is a craft manual, and both are more useful to a writer than most craft manuals, because they insist that the question why this detail has an answer even when the writer has not supplied one.

6.3 A taxonomy of descriptive strategies

The instruction “choose a better detail” is not actionable. What is actionable is a repertoire of named strategies, each with a characteristic purchase and a characteristic cost, against which a draft passage can be compared and — more importantly — into which it can be rewritten (§6.4).

Twelve are set out below. They are not mutually exclusive; strong description frequently braids two, and several of the exemplars could have been filed under a neighbour.

# Strategy Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 The metonymic detail Names a thing by something associated with it — the possession for the person, the instrument for the trade Gatsby’s shirts Enormous compression; the abstraction arrives embodied The association must be legible to this reader; ages badly
2 The synecdochic detail Names a whole by one of its parts Charles Bovary’s cap The part is memorable where the whole would be generic Risks caricature; a person reduced to an attribute
3 The estranged detail Describes the operation and withholds the name, so the familiar must be perceived Tolstoy’s opera; Kholstomer Restores perception; makes an argument without stating one Slow; conspicuous; unusable at speed
4 The catalogue An ordered list, its principle of ordering doing the work Gatsby’s party guests; Homer’s ships Scale; the impression of a world exceeding the frame Modern patience is short; needs an explicit organising principle
5 The negative detail Names what is absent, missing, or refused Satis House: no daylight, no moving clock, one shoe unworn Absence is felt more sharply than presence; implies a prior state Requires the reader to know what should be there
6 The comparative detail Simile- or metaphor-led; the described thing arrives through a second object The chokecherry tree on Sethe’s back Precision unavailable to literal report; can carry an ethics Imports a second object into the scene; the figure’s owner must be plausible
7 The focalised detail Selected explicitly by a perceiving consciousness, and characterising in the selection Mrs Ramsay’s boeuf en daube Two domains at once, always; solves plausibility Constrains what may be described to what this person would see
8 The reality-effect detail Unrecuperable; signifies only that a world is being reported Flaubert’s barometer Authentication; air; the sense of a world exceeding its uses Indistinguishable from slackness when overused or badly placed
9 The recurring detail (identical) Repeated in the same words until repetition itself signifies Dickens’s fog Incantation; the repetition becomes an argument Reads as a tic unless the sameness is the point (§8.4)
10 The varied motif Repeated in a changed state or context, so the change is legible Gatsby’s green light Measures elapsed change without narrating it; the standard closing instrument Needs at least three appearances; easily over-managed
11 The ekphrastic set piece Extended description of a made object, often a work of art The shield of Achilles; the bride-cake Licensed indulgence; a whole world inside a frame Halts the narrative absolutely; must be earned
12 The technical register Description in the specialised vocabulary of a trade or discipline Melville’s cetology; the language of rigging Authority; a world with expertise in it; characterises the knower Excludes; can become the info-dump (§8.5)

Notes on the strategies

1–2. Metonymy and synecdoche are the two workhorses, and they are routinely confused. Metonymy substitutes by association (the crown for the monarchy, the shirts for the fortune); synecdoche substitutes by part-for-whole (the cap for Charles, sails for ships). The distinction matters in revision because they fail differently. A metonym fails when the reader does not share the association; a synecdoche fails when the part is not characteristic, so that the reduction feels arbitrary rather than revealing. Roman Jakobson’s argument that realist prose is fundamentally metonymic — proceeding by contiguity, from the character to the setting to the objects — while poetry is metaphoric, is the standard theoretical statement, and it explains why so much of the best novelistic description consists of naming the things next to a person rather than the person.

3. The estranged detail is Shklovsky’s device (§3.3). Its operational form is a rule: describe the operation, withhold the label. It is the strategy most likely to produce something no other writer would have written, and the one most likely to be over-applied, since a novel written entirely in estranged description has established a mannerism rather than a perception.

4. The catalogue is defended at length at §8.1. The single thing that distinguishes a catalogue from an inventory is that the catalogue’s order is doing work — geographical in Homer, social and satirical in Fitzgerald’s list of who came to Gatsby’s house that summer, economic in Balzac. An inventory has no ordering principle and could be shuffled without loss. Shuffle-testing a list is the fastest available diagnostic (§7.1, test 10).

5. The negative detail is the most under-used strategy in the taxonomy and the cheapest to deploy. Naming what is absent does three things at once: it establishes the expected state, it establishes the deviation, and it implies a perceiver who noticed the difference. Satis House is built almost entirely from negatives — no daylight, no moving clock, no second shoe on the foot, no dressing of the table since the cake was cut — and Woolf’s “Time Passes” section of To the Lighthouse is a sustained study in describing a house by what has stopped happening in it.

6. The comparative detail carries an ethical weight the others do not, because a figure is always someone’s. When Amy Denver calls Sethe’s scars a chokecherry tree, the simile is a girl’s, and the novel makes the reader feel both its tenderness and its inadequacy (§9.6). A simile in unfocalised narration has no owner and therefore no defence; a simile owned by a character is doing character work whether it succeeds as description or not.

7. The focalised detail is not a strategy so much as a discipline that can be applied to any of the others, and §4.2 has made the case for it. It is listed separately because in practice writers treat it as optional, and it is a reliable upgrade available to a flat descriptive passage.

8. The reality-effect detail is included in a taxonomy of strategies with some discomfort, since Barthes’s point is that it looks unstrategic. But it can be deployed deliberately, and skilled writers do deploy it: an object named once, given no attribute that matters, never mentioned again. The craft term for the deliberate version is simply air.

9–10. The two repetition strategies are treated at §6.5.

11. The ekphrastic set piece is the oldest descriptive form in the Western tradition — Homer’s shield of Achilles is its founding instance — and it comes with a warning attached from Lessing’s Laocoön (1766), which observed that Homer does not describe the shield as an object but narrates its making, panel by panel, because poetry is an art of succession and painting an art of coexistence. Lessing’s inference is directly usable: an extended description survives better as a process than as a picture, which is the same conclusion Scarry reaches from cognitive premises two centuries later (§3.5).

12. The technical register buys authority faster than any other strategy, because a reader assumes that a writer who knows the name of a thing knows the rest of it. Its danger is that expertise is interesting to the expert. The discrimination that keeps it safe is the one at §8.5: technical description is safe while it is someone’s competence being exercised, and becomes an info-dump the moment it is the author’s knowledge being displayed.

6.4 How to use the taxonomy: substitution testing

The taxonomy’s purpose is not classification but substitution. A descriptive passage that is not working is very often the right material in the wrong strategy, and the productive exercise is to rewrite it in two or three others before line-editing the original.

The procedure:

  1. Name the strategy the passage currently uses. (Most flat passages are unfocalised catalogue.)
  2. Rewrite the same material as a focalised detail — same room, but only what this person, wanting what they want, would register.
  3. Rewrite it again as a negative detail — describe the room by what is missing from it.
  4. Rewrite it a third time in a technical register — as it would be described by someone whose trade this is.
  5. Compare. Do not choose until all three exist.

The exercise reliably teaches more in twenty minutes than three rounds of adjective-pruning, because it changes the principle of selection rather than the wording, and the principle of selection is where description succeeds or fails.

A worked miniature, using the household of §9.10. The object is a coat on a hook in a hallway.

Strategy Rendering What it buys
Unfocalised catalogue A coat hung on the hook beside the door, next to an umbrella and a shopping bag. Nothing; the reader learns a hallway exists
Focalised She took the long way round the hook, the way she had been taking it since March. Character, relationship, and a date, in one clause
Negative Nobody had moved the coat, and nobody had put anything on the hook beside it. The household’s paralysis, and a second person implied
Temporal The shoulders were still free of dust; someone was still doing that. Duration, maintenance, and an unnamed carer
Technical Army green, the wool gone the colour of dishwater; left cuff frayed through at the thumb. Authority; the eye of someone who knows fabric and wear

None is the right answer in the abstract. The last two are the ones the finished passage at §9.10 uses, because the novel is about who is still doing what for a dead man, and about what wear records.

6.5 Motif: how repetition converts a detail into meaning

This subsection is load-bearing for Chapter 16 (The Ending), whose principal closing instrument is the varied return of a detail planted early.

A single detail cannot carry theme. Meaning at the thematic level is a property of relation, and the commonest available relation is recurrence: an object that appears more than once, in changed circumstances, so that the reader measures the change.

Genette’s category of frequency (Narrative Discourse, 1972; trans. Jane E. Lewin, 1980) gives the precise vocabulary. A narrative may relate once what happened once (singulative), relate n times what happened once (repeating), or relate once what happened n times (iterative). The motif is a particular use of the first two: the same object is narrated singulatively on each of several occasions, and the reader performs the comparison the text declines to make.

Why identical repetition fails and varied repetition accrues

This is the mechanism, and it needs stating carefully, because craft advice usually gets it half right.

An identical repetition delivers no new information. The second appearance of an unchanged detail in an unchanged context adds nothing to the reader’s model, and the reader — who is running the rules of notice, and therefore treats a repeated element as marked — looks for the addition, finds none, and attributes the repetition to the author. Hence the diagnosis in §8.4: the static motif reads as an authorial tic. The reader has correctly identified that the only new fact on offer is the writer likes this image.

A varied repetition delivers a differential. The object is the same and something about it or around it is not, and the difference is legible because the object is constant. This is the whole engine:

A motif is a measuring instrument. The object is the ruler and the change is the reading. An unchanging ruler applied to an unchanging quantity produces no measurement — only a repeated number.

Four things can vary, and they can be ranked by how much work they do:

What varies Example shape Strength
The object’s own state The coat dusted; the coat dusty; the coat worn Strongest — change is intrinsic and undeniable
Who is in relation to it Nobody touches it; then one person does Very strong — carries plot and character at once
The context it appears in Same object, safety then danger Strong
The words used for it Same object, a different register of naming Weakest alone; excellent in combination

The economics of a motif

Three practical rules, all of them derivable from the differential account.

  1. Three appearances is the working minimum. One is an object. Two is a coincidence, and readers do not reliably register it. Three establishes a series, and once a series exists the reader begins to anticipate the fourth — which converts the motif from a decoration into a source of expectation, and expectation is the currency Chapter 16 spends.
  2. The first appearance must be unemphatic. A motif announced on introduction is a symbol (§8.12), and the reader will watch it rather than the story. The strongest motifs are planted as ordinary world detail and only retrospectively promoted — Achebe’s yams, Dickens’s file, the good pen in the household’s prologue.
  3. The last appearance should be the most changed and the least explained. A motif that ends with a paragraph explaining what it meant has spent its accumulated charge on a summary the reader had already made. Chapter 16 treats this as the central discipline of endings.

The exemplars

Varied. Fitzgerald’s green light at the end of Daisy’s dock appears three times in The Great Gatsby, and the variation is a textbook of the technique. On its first appearance Gatsby is reaching towards it and Nick does not know what it is. On its second it has been reached, and Nick suspects that for Gatsby its colossal significance has now vanished — the object is unchanged and its charge has collapsed, which is the novel’s whole argument about desire delivered in a sentence about a lamp. On its third, in the closing page, it has been generalised into the orgastic future that recedes before us. Same lamp, three states of a man’s relation to it, no commentary until the end and then only obliquely.

Identical, and successful. Dickens’s fog in the first chapter of Bleak House repeats the word fog at the head of clause after clause without variation, and the passage is one of the great openings in English. §8.4 explains the exception in full; the short form is that the repetition is local, dense and rhetorical — it occurs within a single passage, where it reads as incantation and enacts the undifferentiated condition it describes — rather than distributed across a book, where identical repetition reads as forgetfulness. The two uses of repetition are different instruments that happen to share a name.

A caution about over-management. A motif whose every appearance is charged, changed and placed at a chapter’s end is doing its work too visibly. The strongest motif series contain at least one appearance in which the object is simply present, unremarkably, doing nothing — which is the reality effect (§3.4) operating inside a thematic structure, and the reason a well-handled motif does not feel managed.

6.6 Historical drift, and a caution

Connotation is the fastest-ageing component of prose, for the reason given at §6.1: it is supplied by a shared cultural repertoire, and repertoires change.

Detail Connotation then Connotation now
A barometer in a parlour Provincial respectability; the aspiration to scientific modernity Almost none; a curiosity
A pair of yellow spectacles on a hoarding Optometry advertising; the new roadside commerce Period signal; immediately “the 1920s”
A wedding cake kept for years A known custom, faintly macabre Wholly macabre; the custom is unfamiliar
Tobacco quit twice Ordinary; a small vice A health signal; possibly a cause of death
A landline ringing unanswered Deliberate avoidance Requires dating; the object itself is now a period marker

Three consequences.

  1. Structural claims about description survive; connotative readings do not. This is the same distinction the series maintains about translation, and for the same reason. That Flaubert’s barometer is unrecuperable is a structural fact and is still true. That it signalled bourgeois modernity is a historical fact about its first readers, and a modern reader must be told.
  2. A writer’s connotative intuitions are calibrated to their own moment, which is not a defect but is worth knowing. Details keyed to technology and commerce date fastest; details keyed to the body, to weather, to food, to wear and to social relations date slowest. Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) makes the same observation about genre signals anchored to consumer technology.
  3. The contemporary preference for the sparse, connotatively loaded detail is itself a convention (§3.6), and the taxonomy above is weighted towards it. Strategies 4, 11 and 12 — catalogue, ekphrasis, technical register — are all currently out of fashion in literary fiction and all are periodically revived, most recently by the documentary and essayistic novel. A student should be able to write in all twelve and should not assume that the six the workshop prefers are the six that work.
