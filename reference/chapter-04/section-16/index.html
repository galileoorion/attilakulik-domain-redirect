Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

16. Coda: Against the Tyranny of the Purposeful Detail

Continue in Core

Everything in this chapter is an instrument for noticing. None of it is a rule, and one of its central principles is, on its own terms, false.

The principle is no detail without a job, and its usefulness is beyond dispute. It is the correct remedy for the inventory passage, the wallpaper detail, the undirected paragraph and the flat world. It converts a vague dissatisfaction — this description isn’t working — into an answerable question: this passage has no selection principle, or nothing here carries duration, or this is a catalogue that wants to be a negative. Specific questions can be answered; vague ones can only be worried at.

But applied without limit it produces a describable and unpleasant result, and every workshop has read it. A novel in which every object has been vetted, every adjective justified, every recurrence varied and every sensory channel balanced does not read as a well-made world. It reads as a set: a space in which nothing exists that is not going to be used, where a reader learns within forty pages that anything mentioned will matter, and where the pleasure of noticing is replaced by the labour of decoding. The prose is airless in the precise sense that there is nothing in it that is merely there.

Barthes explained why, in 1968, before the workshop existed to be warned. A world is persuasive partly because it exceeds its own uses. The barometer in Mme Aubain’s parlour is not doing a job, and the room is real because of it. Take the barometer out, leave the piano and the heaped boxes, and the room becomes a diagram of a household in decline — accurate, legible, and no longer a place. The detail that means nothing is what certifies that the others were found rather than manufactured.

This is not an argument for slackness, and it is important to state the difference exactly, because students hear permission where a distinction is intended.

Air Slackness
Position Subordinate; inside a clause with other business Emphatic; its own sentence, or a paragraph’s end
Attributes None, or one plain one Modified, figurative, worked
Proportion A minority of a passage’s elements The passage’s default mode
Recurrence Never mentioned again Nothing recurs anywhere
Company Surrounded by recuperable details Surrounded by other inert details
Effect The world exceeds the story The writer had nothing in mind

The distinguishing fact is that air is only perceptible against function (§9.3). One unrecuperable detail among six purposeful ones is a room; six unrecuperable details is an inventory. The reality effect is not the opposite of selection; it is something selection makes possible.

There is a second reason to resist the purposive model at full strength, and it is not aesthetic. It concerns what description is for at the level of a reader’s experience. A great deal of the best description in fiction — Woolf’s daube, Achebe’s kola, the yellowed dress in Satis House, the coat on a hook — does its work by conferring attention on something that would not ordinarily receive it. That is a moral operation as much as a technical one, and it does not survive being audited. Amy Denver’s chokecherry tree (§9.6) is not a detail with a job. It is a girl finding a way to look at something unbearable, and the novel’s ethics live in the fact that the figure is both a kindness and a falsification. No test in §7 will tell a writer whether to keep it.

So the chapter ends where it began, with a question rather than a rule, and with the honest admission that the question has two forms which cannot both be pressed at once.

Out of everything that could have been mentioned here, why this?

And, less often asked and equally necessary: what is in this room that I did not put there for a reason?

A book that cannot answer the first is undirected. A book that cannot answer the second is a machine. The novels in §9 answer both, and none of them does it by rule.
