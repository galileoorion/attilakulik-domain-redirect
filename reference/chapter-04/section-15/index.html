Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

15. Further Reading

Continue in Core

Annotated, grouped by what each source is good for. Starred items are the recommended first three.

15.1 On the detail itself

  • ★ Barthes, Roland. “The Reality Effect” (1968). Eight pages, and the most consequential thing ever written on the literary detail. Read it before any craft manual on description, and read it twice: once for the argument about the barometer, once for the sting in the tail about ideology. It is the reason §16 exists.
  • ★ Auerbach, Erich. Mimesis (1946). Read at minimum the first chapter, “Odysseus’ Scar”. It contains, in twenty pages, the entire theory of descriptive economy that craft pedagogy has been paraphrasing without attribution for seventy years.
  • Schor, Naomi. Reading in Detail: Aesthetics and the Feminine (1987). The historical and political account of why the detail was long regarded as trivial and ornamental, and what changed. Essential corrective to the assumption that the modern preference for the concrete particular is a neutral technical advance.
  • Hamon, Philippe. Introduction à l’analyse du descriptif (1981). The most systematic treatment of description as a discourse in its own right. Untranslated in full and demanding, but the concept of the descriptive alibi — the pretext a text supplies for pausing to describe — is worth acquiring even at second hand.

15.2 On realism and its particulars

  • ★ Watt, Ian. The Rise of the Novel (1957). Chapter 1 alone. The account of formal realism explains why the novel is obliged to particulars and why abandoning them changes genre rather than tightening prose. Contested by later scholarship on its account of the novel’s origins; unsurpassed on its account of the novel’s manner.
  • Lukács, Georg. “Narrate or Describe?” (1936). The great dissent. Read it as the strongest available argument that the descriptive turn in the novel was a loss, and set it against your own instincts about Flaubert.
  • Spiegel, Alan. Fiction and the Camera Eye (1976). For the argument that cinema reorganised the novel’s way of seeing. Useful evidence for the historical claim at §3.6.

15.3 On estrangement and perception

  • Shklovsky, Viktor. “Art as Device” (1917). Short, polemical, and the origin of the most practically usable idea in this chapter. The Tolstoy examples are the part to study.
  • Robbe-Grillet, Alain. For a New Novel (1963). The most rigorous defence of description freed from interiority and metaphor. Read as a limit case rather than a programme; it clarifies by exaggeration.
  • Nabokov, Vladimir. Lectures on Literature (1980). For the insistence that literature is made of details and that a reader’s business is to attend to them. Also for the diagrams — Nabokov drawing the layout of Gregor Samsa’s flat is a lesson in what novelistic space actually specifies.

15.4 On how prose makes images

  • ★ Scarry, Elaine. Dreaming by the Book (1999). Difficult, original, and unlike anything else on this list: an account of the specific instructions prose gives the imagination, and why motion, transparency and light produce vivacity where inventory does not. The chapter on “radiant ignition” repays the effort.
  • Zwaan and Radvansky, “Situation Models in Language Comprehension and Memory” (1998). For what readers actually track and how coarsely. Directly relevant to §7.1’s count test.
  • Bachelard, Gaston. The Poetics of Space (1958). Not a scientific work and not a craft manual; a phenomenology of houses, drawers, corners, nests and shells. Read for the way it makes ordinary domestic objects available as material for meaning — which is the skill §4.4 struggles to teach directly.

15.5 Craft manuals, used critically

  • Gardner, John. The Art of Fiction (1983). For the “vivid and continuous dream” and for its account of how a false detail breaks it.
  • Le Guin, Ursula K. Steering the Craft (rev. 2015). The best short treatment of how description interacts with sentence rhythm and point of view, with exercises that work.
  • Wood, James. How Fiction Works (2008). The section on detail is the most intelligent short craft treatment available in English, and — unusually for the genre — it engages Barthes directly rather than around him.
  • Burroway, Janet. Writing Fiction. The standard university textbook; useful chiefly for its anthology of quoted description and its exercises.

15.6 Within this textbook

  • Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) — the domain model and the value-shift test, of which §7.1’s removal test is the detail-level form.
  • Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) — text and subtext, the exact analogue of §6’s denotation and connotation.
  • Chapter 5 (The Transition) — Genette’s duration categories, and the descriptive pause as a structural cost.
  • Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) — focalisation in full; the declaration requirement that §8.11 depends on.
  • Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) — expositional distribution, and the reason §8.5’s info-dump spends an asset.
  • Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) — the rules of notice, and the world contract, whose treatment of Barthes this chapter takes further.
  • Chapter 16 (The Ending) — the payoff audit, which depends entirely on §6.5’s account of the varied motif.
