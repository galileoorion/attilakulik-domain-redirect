Chapter 4 · The Descriptive Detail

12. Studio Work

Continue in Core

12.1 Practical exercises

Exercise 1 — The domain audit. Perform the audit at §5.5 on one descriptive paragraph of your draft. Then have a reader who does not know your intentions perform it on the same paragraph. Write 200 words on the gap between the two results.

Exercise 2 — The substitution ladder. Take one object described in your draft. Write five further true statements about it — not better ones, other ones. Place each in the text in turn and write one sentence on the different book each implies. (§7.1, test 4.)

Exercise 3 — Strategy substitution. Take one descriptive passage and rewrite it three times using three strategies from §6.3: focalised, negative, and technical register. Do not judge which is best until all three exist. Write 300 words comparing what each buys and costs.

Exercise 4 — The room in three, the room in thirty. Describe the same room twice: once in three details, once in thirty. Then give both to a reader a week apart and ask them, without the text, to describe the room. Record what survived from each.

Exercise 5 — Estrangement drill. Choose an object your viewpoint character uses daily. Describe its operation without naming it, in fewer than sixty words, so that a reader recognises it only at the end. Then write the ordinary version and compare what each costs in speed. (§3.3.)

Exercise 6 — The negative pass. Rewrite one descriptive paragraph using only absences: what is missing, what has stopped, what nobody has done. Note which of the resulting details you would keep in a mixed version.

Exercise 7 — The motif ledger. Search your draft for one recurring object. Tabulate every appearance against four columns: the object’s state, who is in relation to it, the surrounding context, the words used. Identify which appearances are doing nothing, and either vary or cut them. (§6.5, §7.1 test 7.)

Exercise 8 — The sense displacement. Take a page of your most visual description and replace exactly three visual elements with three non-visual ones — no additions. Read both aloud. (§7.1, test 9b.)

Exercise 9 — The air exercise. Add to a finished, heavily worked descriptive passage one object that means nothing: no attribute, subordinate position, never mentioned again. Give both versions to two readers and ask which world feels more real. Write 300 words on the result and on Barthes’s account of why. (§3.4, §16.)

12.2 Seminar questions

  1. Does the reality effect refute “no detail without a job”? §3.4 offers three responses — that the terms differ, that the levels differ, and that the craft principle is a period preference. Argue for one against the other two. Test your position on a paragraph of Balzac and a paragraph of contemporary literary fiction.

  2. Five domains or six? Argue for or against admitting the temporal domain (§4.6) as a separate category. Is a category whose content can be destroyed by a change of grammatical aspect, without any change to the world described, thereby distinct — or does the same argument admit a social domain, an embodied domain, and eventually everything?

  3. Whose figure is it? Amy Denver’s chokecherry tree (§9.6) makes a wound beautiful. Is the ethical safeguard the fact that the figure belongs to a character rather than to the narrator, or is that a technical solution to a problem that is not technical? Compare with a passage in which a narrator supplies the figure directly.

  4. Is the two-detail norm a discovery or a fashion? §3.6 argues that the collapse in descriptive volume is a period convention driven partly by photography and film. Test the claim: find a contemporary novel that describes at nineteenth-century length and account for how it earns the licence.

  5. Lukács’s charge. “Narrate or Describe?” argues that the ascendancy of description turns characters into observers and readers into spectators. Is that a description of Flaubert’s method or an indictment of it? Take a position using §9.1 and §9.2.

  6. Where does the shirts scene get its meaning? §9.5 argues that Daisy’s tears resist decoding and that the resistance is the achievement. Is an uninterpretable detail a triumph of the objective correlative or a failure of the passage to mean anything determinate? Both positions have distinguished defenders.

12.3 Assessment rubric

For a submitted descriptive sequence (1,000–1,500 words, forming part of a scene) with a 750-word critical commentary.

Band Craft (60%) Commentary (40%)
Distinction A statable selection principle; details carrying multiple domains without strain; focalisation consistent and characterising; motifs varied; some element deliberately left unrecuperable Names its strategies from §6.3 and defends them against alternatives; identifies its own risks including over-purposiveness; engages at least two theoretical sources critically rather than decoratively
Merit A selection principle discernible; some multiplicity; focalisation mostly consistent; recurrence present if under-varied Accurate self-analysis; some engagement with theory; risks acknowledged if unresolved
Pass Details specific rather than generic; description competent but largely single-domain and unfocalised Describes the passage accurately; theory referenced but not applied
Fail Inventory or category detail throughout; no selection principle; one or more failure patterns unrecognised Description without analysis; no engagement with the model
