Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

6. The Grant: Architectures of Viewpoint

Continue in Core

6.1 The grant as a structural commitment

A viewpoint shift is an event. A grant is a commitment, and it has four properties that a writer should be able to state for every viewpoint character in a manuscript:

Property The question Why it matters
Recipient Who holds it? §4 and §7.1 tests 1–3
Extent How much of the book? §5.5; determines whether this is a viewpoint character or a bounded grant
Onset When does it begin? §6.6; the difference between a first and a later grant
Termination Does it end, and does the reader know? An unannounced ending reads as abandonment (§5.5)

Most craft treatments handle only the first. The other three are where structural failure actually occurs, and they are properties of the whole book rather than of any scene, which is why they are invisible while drafting forward and obvious in an audit.

The four properties combine into a small number of recurring shapes. Those shapes are what this section calls viewpoint architectures, and a manuscript almost always belongs to one of them whether or not its author chose it.

6.2 The scholarly treatment

The idea that a novel’s handling of vantage constitutes a describable form rather than a series of local choices arrives with Henry James and is codified against him. §3.5 gives the history; what belongs here is what each party contributed to the description of whole-book shapes.

James’s contribution is the reflector, or centre of consciousness. His best-known figure for it — the house of fiction with not one window but a million, at each of which a watcher with a distinct field and instrument stands — states the principle: the aperture and the watcher together constitute the subject, and a different watcher at a different window is looking at a different novel. Two features of his position are usually lost in transmission. He presents restriction as a gain in intensity purchased by a loss in scope, which is a trade rather than a rule; and his own practice is less austere than his advocacy, since the narrator of The Ambassadors generalises and stands back with some freedom even while remaining largely fixed on Strether.

Lubbock turned the trade into a doctrine and Forster dissented within six years (§3.5). Forster’s position — that shifting vantage is a resource, and that what governs its success is the reader’s confidence in the writer’s command rather than any consistency rule — is the one this chapter adopts.

Norman Friedman’s “Point of View in Fiction” (PMLA, 1955) produced the first systematic typology in English — editorial omniscience, neutral omniscience, I as witness, I as protagonist, multiple selective omniscience, selective omniscience, the dramatic mode, the camera. Friedman’s categories mix voice and focalisation, which is the confusion Genette resolved seventeen years later; but the ambition of the piece, to describe whole-book shapes rather than sentence-level effects, is the one this section inherits.

Stanzel’s typological circle (§3.2) supplies the crucial structural insight for a taxonomy: architectures are not discrete species but neighbouring positions, and books drift between them. Several entries below are best understood as adjacent regions rather than as boxes.

6.3 A taxonomy of thirteen viewpoint architectures

The taxonomy describes whole-book shapes. It is not exhaustive, the categories are not mutually exclusive, and accomplished novels frequently braid two — Bleak House is an alternating dual whose second narration is a mobile omniscient, and Beloved is a hub-and-spokes that becomes an unattributed chorus for one movement.

# Architecture Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 Single fixed One focaliser for the whole book; the reader never leaves The Remains of the Day; The Trial; What Maisie Knew Maximum depth; the knowledge boundary as a permanent engine; unreliability made structural Everything off-stage must be reported or inferred; no dramatic irony except within the vantage
2 Single mobile (the roving third) Variable internal focalisation, one mind at a time, moving freely but never within a unit Mrs Dalloway; To the Lighthouse; Madame Bovary Coverage without the machinery of declared sections; contrast at close range Requires very strong differential noticing, or the vantages blur; readers may not perceive a pattern to rely on
3 Alternating dual Two vantages in regular or irregular alternation for the length of the book Bleak House; Wide Sargasso Sea (sequential rather than alternating) Contrast at maximum clarity; a rhythm the reader can feel; irony as a structural default The reader nearly always prefers one; the weaker half is felt as an interruption (§8.9)
4 Rotating ensemble Three or more vantages in a repeating cycle As I Lay Dying; The Poisonwood Bible Coverage; a social rather than individual subject; polyphony in Bakhtin’s sense Dilution (§8.3); the cycle becomes a metronome; late-arriving readers cannot locate the centre
5 Hub-and-spokes One dominant vantage, plus subordinate vantages used briefly and for specific purposes Mrs Dalloway (Clarissa and Septimus); Beloved; Anna Karenina (a two-hub variant) Depth and coverage together; the proportions themselves say what the book is about Requires the writer to accept an unequal cast, which drafts resist on grounds of fairness
6 The bounded single grant One chapter or section given to a consciousness that never holds the reader again Addie’s section in As I Lay Dying; Casaubon in Middlemarch ch. 29; the prologue device (Chapter 10) A late grant at a fraction of the price; a single decision dramatised rather than reported The reader may expect a return; the grant must be shaped as complete in itself
7 Frame narrator with embedded tellers An outer narrator receives and transmits accounts from inner ones Wuthering Heights; Frankenstein; Season of Migration to the North Reliability questions; distance; a listener whose reactions guide the reader Two openings to earn; the outer frame must justify its own existence (§8.12)
8 Documentary polyphony The book is an assembly of letters, diaries, transcripts, cuttings Dracula; The Woman in White; The Color Purple Authenticity; access to incompatible vantages; epistemology built into the form Interiority is always performed for a reader, so sympathy is hard; voices tend to converge (§8.5)
9 The collective “we” A group focaliser: a village, a class, a generation, a chorus The Grapes of Wrath (intercalary chapters); The Virgin Suicides; the abandoned nous of Madame Bovary A social mind (Palmer, §3.4); a moral vantage no individual could hold Nobody in particular is known; sustained use exhausts quickly
10 The mobile omniscient narrator Zero focalisation: a narrating intelligence with free access, exercising it, including within scenes Middlemarch; War and Peace; Vanity Fair; Tom Jones Everything: scope, irony, commentary, analysis, moral range Requires a narrator strong enough to hold it; reads as authorial intrusion to readers trained on limitation
11 The unattributed choral voice Consciousness rendered without assignment; the reader infers or is denied the speaker Beloved, Part Two; The Waves; Blindness Effects unavailable to attributed narration: merging, possession, anonymity, the collective wound Very high comprehension cost; unusable for long stretches
12 The second person You as focaliser: the reader positioned as, or addressed as, the character If on a Winter’s Night a Traveller; Bright Lights, Big City Immediate implication; estrangement; a formal argument about reading itself Novelty fatigue; ambiguity about whether you is the reader, a character, or the narrator addressing himself
13 External focalisation — the refused grant No interiority granted to anyone; behaviour and speech only “Hills Like White Elephants”; Jealousy; and locally, Dracula himself throughout Stoker’s novel Maximum inference; the reader does the work; opacity as subject Sustains poorly at novel length; readers supply interiority anyway and may supply the wrong one

Notes on the harder entries.

On 2 versus 4. The difference between the roving third and the rotating ensemble is not the number of vantages but the presence of a declared pattern. Woolf moves between consciousnesses without announcement or regularity, and the reader learns to expect movement rather than to expect a sequence; a rotating ensemble teaches a cycle, and the cycle becomes a promise. The practical consequence is that a roving third can add or drop a vantage at any point without structural cost, while a rotating ensemble cannot: an ensemble that quietly stops visiting one of its members has broken a rule it taught.

On 5. Hub-and-spokes is the architecture most drafts are actually writing and least often admit to. Its virtue is that it resolves the coverage–depth trade rather than choosing a side: the hub is inhabited, the spokes are visited. Its difficulty is entirely psychological — writers who have grown fond of a secondary character resist giving them three pages when they have imagined thirty. §8.9 addresses the inverse failure, the ensemble that is secretly a hub.

On 6. The bounded grant is, in this chapter’s estimate, under-used in contemporary fiction, and it is the structure this chapter recommends most often in revision. Its logic is a discount: the cost of a later grant (§6.6) is largely a cost of disruption to an established pattern, and a grant that visibly closes behind itself disrupts the pattern only once. The shaping requirement is that the section must read as complete — a self-contained account with its own beginning and end — rather than as the first instalment of a thread the book then drops.

On 9 and 11. These two are frequently confused. A collective we has a determinate, if plural, speaker: the boys of the neighbourhood, the townspeople, the migrants. An unattributed choral voice withholds the speaker, and the reader’s uncertainty is the effect. Morrison’s Part Two monologues in Beloved begin attributed and end unattributed, which is the movement from 9 to 11 performed inside a single sequence (§9.8).

On 13. External focalisation is included because a taxonomy of grants must include the decision not to grant. Its most instructive appearance is local rather than global: a novel otherwise rich in interiority that keeps one character resolutely outside. Dracula, Bertha Mason in Jane Eyre, and — in the household narrative running through this book — the mother are all instances, and each is a case where the refusal carries the book’s argument or its damage.

6.4 How to use the taxonomy: substitution testing

The taxonomy’s purpose is not classification but substitution. A manuscript that is not working is very often the right material in the wrong architecture, and the fastest way to discover this is to specify the alternatives enough to compare.

The procedure:

  1. Name the current architecture from §6.3. Most writers get this wrong on the first attempt: drafts that describe themselves as rotating ensembles are usually hubs with underfed spokes.
  2. Name two neighbours — the architectures immediately adjacent on the coverage–depth axis.
  3. For each neighbour, write two hundred words stating what the book would gain, what it would lose, and which scenes would have to be rewritten.
  4. Rewrite one chapter in the strongest neighbour.

Some standard substitutions and what they typically reveal:

From To What the test usually reveals
Rotating ensemble (4) Hub-and-spokes (5) That one vantage was always the book’s centre, and that three others exist to cover the map
Alternating dual (3) Single fixed (1) That the second vantage’s material is reportable, and that its removal produces suspense rather than confusion
Single fixed (1) Bounded grant added (6) That only one off-stage decision needed dramatising, and that everything else was fine as inference
Rotating ensemble (4) Documentary polyphony (8) That the voices were never differentiated by mind, and that giving each a genre of document does the work by other means
Single mobile (2) Mobile omniscient (10) That the writer has been suppressing a narrator who wants to speak, and that the suppression was producing flatness
Any multi-vantage form External focalisation (13) for one character That a character the draft was explaining is more powerful unexplained

The last row is the most frequently productive and the least frequently attempted. A great deal of what a draft calls “developing” a difficult character consists of granting them interiority, and a great deal of what makes such characters powerful is its absence.

6.5 How a viewpoint change is signalled

Chapter 5 (The Transition) owns the crossing — what carries the reader from one side of the gap to the other, and the anchoring that resolves who on arrival (§4.5, §6.3 there). This section owns the prior question: by what device does the book tell the reader that a change of vantage is the kind of thing that happens here?

The distinction matters because the two operate at different scales. An anchor resolves one crossing. A signal establishes a convention, and once established it makes every subsequent crossing cheaper (Emmott’s frame recall, §3.4).

Signal Where it operates Legibility Cost Note
Chapter or section heading naming the focaliser Paratext Total Commits the book to the pattern for its whole length; consumes the heading as a resource As I Lay Dying’s fifty-nine named sections; a common commercial convention
Part or book divisions Paratext High Coarse; permits only a few changes Wide Sargasso Sea; The Sound and the Fury’s dated sections
The proper name in the first clause Prose High Almost none The workhorse; Chapter 5 §4.5
Absolute register shift Prose High once learned, low at first occurrence Requires two voices with no shared features Bleak House: present-tense panorama against retrospective I
Section break (white space) with no other marker Typography Low on its own None Signals a change, not which; must be paired with a prose anchor
Change of document type Form Total Commits the book to an epistolary or documentary architecture Dracula: journal, phonograph diary, telegram, cutting
Change of person or tense Grammar Very high Very high if not sustained Rhys’s Part Three shift; risks reading as error
Typographic differentiation (italics, no quotation marks, spacing) Typography High Fatigues; unusable for long stretches Morrison’s Part Two; Faulkner’s italics for time-shifts
Explicit narratorial handover Prose Total Period-marked; declares a mobile-omniscient architecture Middlemarch §5.2; Vanity Fair

Three principles govern the choice.

(1) Signal the architecture once, at maximum strength; signal each crossing at minimum strength. The first viewpoint change in a book should be unmistakable — a heading, a part division, an absolute contrast of voice. The twentieth needs only a proper name. Books that reverse this (a quiet first change, heavy signposting thereafter) are the ones readers describe as confusing, even though the confusion occurred once, near the start, and coloured everything after it.

(2) The declaration should precede the reader’s first deep investment. This is Chapter 9 §4.6’s formal contract, and §6.6 supplies its mechanism. In practice: the first viewpoint change should occur at the first chapter break, not at page ninety.

(3) Naming headings are a commitment, not a convenience. A book whose chapters are headed with names has promised that every chapter belongs to a named vantage. It can no longer include an unattributed chapter, a chapter focalised through a minor character, or a chapter of pure omniscient panorama, without the reader experiencing the omission as a puzzle. Faulkner keeps the promise absolutely in As I Lay Dying, including for Addie, who is dead. Writers who adopt the convention for its convenience frequently discover in the third act that they have foreclosed the manoeuvre the book needs.

6.6 The first grant and the later grant

The chapter’s central mechanism can now be stated with the theoretical apparatus behind it.

First grant. Viewpoint established while the reader’s investment across the cast is still forming — in practice, within the first chapters. Cheap, because the reader has not yet committed and a new vantage simply joins the field.

Later grant. Viewpoint introduced after the book has trained the reader to follow a smaller set of vantages. Expensive, because it withdraws attention from positions the reader has already paid into, and because it retroactively revises the book’s declared form.

The cost of a later grant is not a single quantity. It is the sum of three distinct charges, and separating them explains why some late grants are ruinous and others are the best thing in the book.

Charge What it is Reduced by
Formal surprise The reader learns the book is a different shape than they thought Early signalling of the possibility of movement, even if not exercised
Attention withdrawal Pages that would have gone to established vantages go elsewhere Boundedness — a short grant withdraws little
Investment cold start The new vantage has no accumulated familiarity; the reader must build a model from zero at a point in the book where they are least willing Prior presence — the character has been on the page, observed from outside, for the whole novel

The third charge is the one drafts most often mismanage, and it yields the most useful practical rule in this section:

A later grant is cheap in proportion to how long the reader has already been curious about the recipient. A character observed from outside for three hundred pages, whose silence has been the book’s standing question, costs almost nothing to enter. A character introduced and granted viewpoint in the same chapter, at the two-thirds mark, costs everything.

This is why the bounded grant (§6.3, no. 6) is the standard remedy. It pays the first charge once, the second charge trivially, and the third not at all if the recipient has been present throughout. The household case at §9.9 is this configuration, and so is Casaubon’s chapter in Middlemarch: a character the reader has watched from outside for twenty-eight chapters, entered once, and left.

The declaration window. Combining Chapter 9 §4.6 with the charges above yields a rough scheduling principle rather than a rule:

Grant onset Status Requirement
First chapter First grant None beyond ordinary competence
Within the opening movement (roughly the first fifth) First grant, late The pattern should be legible by the end of this movement
Middle of the book Later grant Must be either bounded, or supported by a domain payoff that no report could deliver
Final quarter Late later grant Bounded, or the book must be one whose subject is the insufficiency of its own vantages (§4.6)
Final pages Extreme Legitimate only as a formal argument. Wide Sargasso Sea’s Grace Poole; The Sound and the Fury’s fourth section

The bottom two rows are the interesting ones, and §8.4 supplies their counterexamples. A very late grant is not an error; it is a manoeuvre with a narrow success condition, which is that the arrival of a new vantage must be legible as the book’s argument rather than as the author running out of alternatives.

6.7 Historical drift, and a caution against universalising the present

§3.5 established that the prohibition on mid-scene movement is recent. This section makes the wider point about architectures, which have moved at least as much. The table is a sketch of tendencies drawn from the novels this chapter reads, not a measured survey.

Period Dominant architectures Driver
18th century Documentary polyphony (8); frame with embedded tellers (7); mobile omniscient (10) The fiction of the found document; the novel’s need to claim factual standing
Early–mid 19th century Mobile omniscient (10), with occasional dual (3) Serial publication’s need for many threads; the social-panoramic ambition
Late 19th century Single fixed (1) and single mobile (2) rising; omniscience under attack James; the aesthetic of restriction; the rise of psychological realism
Modernist Single mobile (2), rotating ensemble (4), unattributed chorus (11) Consciousness as subject; the loss of confidence in a governing vantage
Mid-century Anglo-American Single fixed (1) dominant; multi-viewpoint at chapter boundaries only Workshop pedagogy; Lubbock’s codification; editorial practice
Contemporary literary Alternating dual (3) and hub-and-spokes (5) The two-hander; the interest in structural contrast; the marketability of a “braided” novel
Contemporary commercial Rotating ensemble (4) with named-chapter headings Series length; ensemble television; the demand for coverage of large worlds

Three inferences, which pull in different directions.

The advice in §§5, 7 and 8 is calibrated to the last two rows. Its cautions about dilution, late grants and head-hopping describe how a reader trained on contemporary convention responds. That is a reason to know the cautions, and not a reason to mistake them for laws.

The novels that violate them are not tolerated exceptions. Middlemarch moves between minds within a paragraph; War and Peace changes vantage at will and interrupts to discourse on historiography; Bleak House introduces its second narration in Chapter 3 with no announcement whatever. A pedagogy that can only say this would be edited out today has explained an industry and not a book.

The drivers are material as often as aesthetic. The named-chapter heading is not a discovery about reading; it is, on this chapter’s reading, a solution to the problem of a very long fantasy novel with many viewpoint characters, and it plausibly spread because it worked for that problem. Serial publication produced the multi-thread nineteenth-century novel. Ensemble television has probably shaped the contemporary rotating ensemble, though the influence is conjecture. Knowing which of one’s convictions about viewpoint are aesthetic and which are inherited from a distribution format is part of a writer’s education.

The properly critical position is double: learn the present conventions well enough to break them deliberately, and read historically well enough to know that they are conventions.
