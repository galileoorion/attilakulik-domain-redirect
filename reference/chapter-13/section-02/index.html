Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion of viewpoint is unusually prone to conflation. “Point of view” is made to carry person (first, third), distance (close, far), reliability, focalisation, narrative voice and the identity of the narrating agent, all at once, with the result that four different problems receive the same diagnosis. This section separates them before the argument begins.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A viewpoint shift is a deliberate change in the consciousness through which the narration is filtered, made at a structural boundary, in a book built to support more than one vantage. The decision underlying it — the grant — is the allocation to a particular character of access to the reader’s identification: how much, and beginning when.

Three elements of that definition deserve emphasis.

  • “Deliberate … at a structural boundary.” This excludes the uncontrolled mid-scene change treated at §2.3 — not because that change is always wrong (it very often is not; see §8.1) but because it is a different object with different consequences.
  • “The consciousness through which the narration is filtered.” Not the person speaking. A first-person retrospective narrator speaks throughout while filtering the narration through her much younger self; the speaker has not changed and the filter has. Genette’s separation of voice from focalisation (§3.1) exists to make this sayable.
  • “The grant.” The chapter’s central object is not the shift but the decision it expresses. A shift is an event in the text; a grant is a structural commitment. One grant may produce forty shifts, or one.

2.2 Scale and extent

Viewpoint decisions are made at four scales, and the chapter treats them differently.

Scale The decision Where treated
The sentence Whose judgement is in this adjective? §5.3; largely a matter of free indirect style
The scene Whose consciousness holds this unit? §7.1 test 9; Chapter 1
The chapter or section Whose turn is this, and for how long? §6.5; Chapter 6
The book Who has viewpoint at all, how many, in what proportion? §5, §6.3 — the chapter’s primary scale

The primary scale is the last, and this is the chapter’s principal departure from most craft treatments of viewpoint, which operate at the first two. A manuscript can be flawless at sentence and scene level and still be structurally wrong: five well-executed viewpoints, each anchored cleanly, each internally consistent, can constitute a book that has spread itself so thin that no one in it is known.

Extent — how long a grant is held — is a separate variable from number, and one that drafts routinely fail to consider. A book may grant viewpoint to six characters and yet be a single-viewpoint novel in every practical sense, if five of the grants are one chapter each. §6.3 treats extent as a defining feature of architecture rather than as a detail of execution.

2.3 The term most often confused: head-hopping

Head-hopping is a change of focalisation within a scene, without a structural boundary, such that the reader’s access moves from one character’s interiority to another’s in the course of continuous action.

The distinction between head-hopping and a structural shift is standardly drawn as follows:

Head-hopping Structural viewpoint shift
Location Mid-scene, mid-paragraph, sometimes mid-sentence At a scene, section or chapter boundary
Signal None White space, chapter heading, an anchoring first clause
Reader experience (as usually described) Momentary uncertainty about whose perceptions are being reported A managed reorientation
Diagnosis in workshop practice An error A design decision

The chapter reproduces this table because a student must know the convention. It then spends §3.5 and §8.1 arguing that the bottom row is wrong as stated, and that the table describes a local literary convention of roughly the last century, not a property of narrative.

The case in short: Middlemarch, Anna Karenina, War and Peace, Vanity Fair and Tom Jones move between minds inside a single continuous scene constantly, deliberately and to enormous effect. What a contemporary workshop calls head-hopping is, in these books, simply omniscience — a narrator with free access to any consciousness, exercising it. The prohibition dates from the codification of Jamesian practice by Percy Lubbock and his successors, and it is a prohibition on an effect (unintentional wobble in an otherwise limited narration) that has been mistaken for a prohibition on a technique.

The operative discrimination is therefore not where the change happens but whether the narration has a stable governing convention that the change belongs to:

A mid-scene change of mind is head-hopping when the book has established a limited narration and then leaks. It is omniscience when the book has established a mobile narration and then moves. The reader’s uncertainty is produced by the mismatch, not by the movement.

This is developed at length at §8.1, which the chapter treats as its intellectual centre.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the viewpoint shift
Focalisation Genette’s term for the regulation of narrative information according to a perceiving position; who perceives, as distinct from who speaks The precise term for what a viewpoint shift changes
Focaliser Bal’s term for the subject of focalisation — the agent whose perception organises what is presented The character receiving a grant
Narrator The agent who speaks the narrative May be unchanged across a viewpoint shift, and often is
Reflector / centre of consciousness James’s terms for a character through whom the narration is refracted without their narrating The single-fixed-internal architecture (§6.3, no. 1)
Filter and slant Chatman’s pair: filter is the character’s perceptual and conceptual mediation; slant is the narrator’s attitudinal colouring Useful for separating a character’s seeing from a narrator’s judging within one sentence
Free indirect style Third-person narration whose diction and judgement are contaminated by the character’s The chief carrier of internal focalisation in the modern novel; see §5.3
Narrative distance How close the narration stands to the focaliser’s consciousness A within-grant variable; a book may change distance without changing viewpoint. Chapter 9 §4.1
Person First, second, third Independent of focalisation: a first-person narrative may be externally focalised, a third-person one internally
Character-space Woloch: the delimited position a character occupies within the narrative as a whole The scholarly form of what a grant confers, and of what its absence costs
Character-system Woloch: the arrangement of many character-spaces into a structure of distributed attention The scholarly form of a book’s viewpoint architecture
Head-hopping Mid-scene change of focalisation in a book with limited narration §2.3, §8.1
Omniscience Zero focalisation: narration not restricted to any character’s knowledge §3.5, §6.3 no. 10

Two of these pairings cause most of the confusion in practice, and are worth isolating.

Person is not focalisation. The Great Gatsby is first person and largely externally focalised on Gatsby: Nick reports what he sees and infers, and the interior of the man the book is about is closed to him. Mrs Dalloway is third person and internally focalised at extremely close range on a succession of consciousnesses. A student who believes “first person” means “close” and “third person” means “distant” will misdescribe both.

Distance is not viewpoint. A book may move from She crossed the yard to Gravel. Not the grass, not today without changing whose consciousness it occupies. That is a change of narrative distance, treated in Chapter 9 §4.1. It feels to an inexperienced reader like a viewpoint change, and it is diagnosed and remedied differently: distance drift is a prose problem, viewpoint drift is a structural one.

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • The mechanics of crossing. How a reader is anchored on the far side of a viewpoint change — the proper name in the first clause, the register shift, the shared object — belongs to Chapter 5 (The Transition), §4.5 and §6.3. This chapter cross-refers and does not duplicate.
  • Where the break falls. The placement of the boundary at which a shift occurs, and the pacing consequences of cutting away at a tense moment, belong to Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break).
  • The declaration requirement as an opening problem. That a multi-viewpoint structure must be declared early is established in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), §4.6. §6.6 here supplies the mechanism and the cost curve; it does not restate the case.
  • Unreliability. Whether a narrator is to be believed is a distinct axis, treated in Chapter 9 §4.1 and grounded in Booth. It intersects this chapter at §4.6 and §9.5, where multiple unreliable vantages become an epistemological structure rather than a characterisation device.
  • Voice differentiation at sentence level. How two narrators are made to sound different is a prose matter belonging to Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) and to Chapter 9 §4.1. This chapter is concerned with whether they notice, want and know differently — §7.1 test 4.
  • Retrospective narration and the past self. The relation between a narrating I and an experiencing I is a focalisation problem, and where it is a temporal one it belongs to Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback).
