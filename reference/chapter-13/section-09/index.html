Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Eight close readings and a revision case. The apparatus is the same throughout: identify the architecture (§6.3), audit the domains (§4), and assess the allocation (§5, §7).

9.1 Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — the dual narration as an architecture

Architecture: alternating dual (§6.3, no. 3), in which one of the two vantages is itself a mobile omniscient (no. 10) and the other is a fixed retrospective first person (no. 1).

Bleak House is discussed in Chapter 9 as an opening and in Chapter 5 as a transition system. The question here is different and prior to both: why does this book have two narrations at all, and what does the allocation between them do?

The two vantages, as a pair of decisions.

The anonymous narrator Esther Summerson
Person and tense Third, present First, past
Position in time Contemporaneous with the events; no hindsight Retrospective; the closing chapter places the writing at least seven years after
Extent of knowledge Institutional, national, systemic; but not uniformly interior Strictly personal; often the last to understand what the reader has grasped
Relation to judgement Judges constantly, at volume, in the second person and the imperative Declines to judge, conspicuously and to the reader’s discomfort
Share Conventionally counted as thirty-four of the sixty-seven chapters Thirty-three
Domains served Access, contrast, pacing, epistemology Sympathy, contrast, irony

The allocation is the argument. Bleak House is a novel about a society whose parts cannot see that they are connected — a Chancery suit, an aristocratic marriage, a crossing-sweeper, a slum. Its formal problem is therefore that no available human vantage can perceive its own subject. Dickens solves this by refusing to solve it: he supplies a vantage that sees the connections and cannot feel them, and a vantage that feels everything and sees almost nothing. The reader is the only position in the book from which both are available, and the novel’s design is an argument that such a position requires an effort of assembly that no character in England is making.

A frequently missed nuance. The anonymous narrator is not fully omniscient in Genette’s sense. He repeatedly declines interiority — describing Tulkinghorn from outside, speculating about what a face may mean, reporting rumour as rumour. Critics have noticed this for decades, and it matters for this chapter: the narrator’s restraint is what preserves the novel’s mysteries in a book that would otherwise have given them away. Zero focalisation is a licence, not an obligation, and a mobile narrator who declines to enter a mind has made a decision as consequential as one who enters.

Esther’s grant is the harder of the two. Her narration is a sustained exercise in a character reporting praise of herself while disclaiming it, and readers have divided since 1853 about whether this is unbearable or exquisite. The structural point is that her modesty is a knowledge boundary made of character rather than of position: she is present at scenes whose meaning she cannot admit to herself, and the reader’s task in her chapters is to read past her. This is the sympathy domain and the irony domain operating through the same sentences.

On the declaration problem. Esther arrives in Chapter 3 with no heading, no announcement and no bridge. By Chapter 9 §4.6’s criterion this is a late declaration; by the test at §7.1, no. 6 it falls after the reader’s first substantial investment. It succeeds because the differentiation is absolute — the two narrations share no feature of person, tense, temper or syntax — and because the first narration is so plainly not a person’s that the reader has not yet formed an attachment to be interrupted. The case shows the criterion doing real work rather than merely licensing convention.

9.2 Faulkner, The Sound and the Fury (1929) and As I Lay Dying (1930) — polyphony declared

Architectures: The Sound and the Fury, a four-part sequence of bounded grants (§6.3, no. 6) closing into third-person narration; As I Lay Dying, a rotating ensemble (no. 4) that is in practice a hub-and-spokes (no. 5).

The two novels, written a year apart, are the best available paired demonstration that the same formal device serves different domains depending on the reader’s task.

The Sound and the Fury. Four sections, three of them interior monologue, dated April Seventh 1928, June Second 1910, April Sixth 1928 and April Eighth 1928. The dates are the declaration: a reader who reads the section headings knows before the first sentence that the book is plural and non-chronological.

The grants are graded by interpretive capacity, and the grading is the design:

Section Focaliser Capacity What the reader must do
I Benjy Perceives; cannot interpret, cannot order time Construct a chronology the narration does not have
II Quentin Interprets compulsively; cannot stop Distinguish event from obsession
III Jason Interprets confidently and wrongly Correct a narrator who is certain
IV Third person, centred on Dilsey Reports from outside all three Notice that the outside was available all along

The novel is the chapter’s clearest instance of the epistemological domain (§4.6). The three interior sections narrate substantially the same family history, and they do not add up; the fourth does not adjudicate. Faulkner’s much-repeated account of the book — that he told the story once, failed, told it again, failed, and was still failing after four attempts — is the domain’s motto.

Note what the fourth section costs and buys. By the criteria of §8.4 it is an unearned late arrival of the most extreme kind: a change not of focaliser but of narrative mode, in the final movement. It works because the reader has been sealed inside three consciousnesses for two hundred pages, and the sensation of an exterior is unavailable to any book that had not sealed itself first.

As I Lay Dying. Fifteen focalisers, fifty-nine sections, one journey. Where The Sound and the Fury grades its vantages by capacity, As I Lay Dying distributes them by proximity: Bundrens and neighbours, insiders and observers, so that the family is described from within and surrounded at the same time.

Three structural observations belong to this chapter.

(1) The distribution is unequal by design. Darl holds nineteen sections; several characters hold one. §8.9 treats this as the counterexample to the secret-favourite pattern: the imbalance is legible, and its content is the novel’s plot, since Darl is the family member who sees and the family’s action is to have him committed.

(2) Addie’s section is the chapter’s model bounded grant. One section, placed roughly two-thirds through, spoken by a woman who has been dead since the novel’s early pages, and it reorganises everything before it. §8.8 explains why the ontological breach reads as premise rather than exemption. What is worth adding here is the timing: had Addie spoken first, the novel would have been a story with an explanation attached. Speaking late, she converts the preceding two hundred pages retroactively — which is a use of viewpoint allocation as a structural reveal, and one of the few available.

(3) Section length is a pacing instrument. Vardaman’s shortest section — five words asserting that his mother is a fish — arrives with the force it does because the sections around it are long. A chapter-based architecture cannot modulate at this resolution. §4.5.

9.3 Eliot, Middlemarch (1872) — the mobile narrator, and “why always Dorothea?”

Architecture: the mobile omniscient narrator (§6.3, no. 10), in its most refined English form.

The sentence itself is anatomised at §5.2. What belongs here is the architecture that makes it possible and the argument it serves.

The narrator as the reader’s constant. Eliot’s narrator is a continuously audible intelligence: it generalises, it addresses the reader directly, it makes moral discriminations, and it can be quoted as a mind in its own right. This is what §8.1 identifies as the missing asset in modern head-hopping — the movement is safe because the narrator is stable. The reader never loses their footing, because their footing was never any character.

The novel theorises its own focalisation. Twice, memorably. The pier-glass parable at the opening of Chapter 27 observes that a scratched mirror, held to a candle, arranges its random scratches into concentric circles around the flame — and that the circles move when the candle does. Every consciousness organises the same world around itself, and the arrangement feels like a discovery about the world rather than about the observer. This is a theory of egoism and, simultaneously, a description of internal focalisation. A narration able to move the candle can demonstrate that the circles are not there.

The second is Chapter 20, where the narrator observes that if we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel’s heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence. This is the empathy budget stated as a fact about consciousness rather than as a fact about books. Attention is finite because human beings are finite; the novel’s economy of attention is a model of a moral economy. It is the strongest available answer to §16’s objection that the budget metaphor is merely borrowed from economics.

“Why always Dorothea?” as a Woloch demonstration. The turn to Casaubon at Chapter 29 does three things at once:

  • It names the allocation as an allocation — the only major English novel to interrupt itself to do so.
  • It executes a bounded grant to a character the reader has been permitted to find ridiculous, without exculpating him.
  • It generalises: the narrator goes on to protest against the concentration of interest on the young and blooming, on the grounds that the older and more eating griefs are what such concentration neglects. That is Woloch’s thesis, one hundred and thirty years early, offered as a complaint about the reading habits of novel-readers.

The limits of the case. Middlemarch fails almost every test in §7. It head-hops constantly; its narrator knows what no one could know; its viewpoint allocation is not declared by any signal a modern reader would recognise; and it grants and withdraws vantages at will. It is also among the two or three most intelligent novels in English about the problem this chapter treats. The proper conclusion is not that the tests are worthless but that they describe a convention within which most contemporary novels operate, and that a book able to establish a different convention is not bound by them.

9.4 Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878) — two hubs, and a dog

Architecture: a two-hub variant of hub-and-spokes (§6.3, no. 5) inside a mobile omniscient narration (no. 10).

Anna Karenina is treated in Chapter 1 as a source of scenes, in Chapter 5 as a transition system and in Chapter 14 as a subplot structure. The allocation question is distinct: how does a novel with two centres distribute itself, and what does the distribution mean?

The two-hub structure. Anna and Levin are both hubs, each with spokes — Vronsky, Karenin, Dolly and Kitty variously attach to one or the other, and Stiva Oblonsky functions as the bridge, being the only character with substantial business in both worlds. The book’s page share between the hubs is not constant: Levin’s share rises through the second half as Anna’s world contracts, and Part Eight, which follows Anna’s death, contains no Anna at all. The proportions enact the argument. A life that consumes itself ends; a life that is being made continues, unresolved, into an eighth part that many readers find anticlimactic and that Tolstoy plainly regarded as the point.

Whether the two plots converge is a real and long-standing critical disagreement. They meet once, late and briefly. Tolstoy insisted the novel had an architecture and that its unity lay in an internal linkage rather than in plot. Readers have disagreed ever since about whether the linkage is structural or merely thematic. It is set as a seminar question (§12.2), and Chapter 14 (The Subplot) treats it from the plot side.

Karenin, and the withdrawal of contempt. §4.4 and §8.10 have already used him. The structural observation to add is that Karenin’s grants are small: he holds relatively few pages, and their effect is out of all proportion to their extent. This is the best available evidence for §3.6’s third qualification — depth is not proportional to page share — and it is the empirical basis for the argument at §9.9 that a single bounded chapter can do what a rotating grant could not.

Laska. During the woodcock shooting in Part Six, the narration enters Levin’s setter: her scent-world, her impatience with the men’s blundering, her assessment of her master’s competence. The passage tests every definition offered in §3.1, and it is worth being exact about what it demonstrates.

  • It is internal focalisation in Genette’s sense, on a focaliser who has no language. The narration must therefore supply language for a wordless consciousness, which is what Cohn’s psycho-narration (§3.2) is for: the narrator’s words, the character’s mind.
  • It is not anthropomorphism, and the distinction is technical. Laska’s world is organised by scent, by degrees of certainty about scent, and by irritation at the humans’ slowness. She is not given human thoughts; she is given a human grammar for canine ones.
  • It is epistemically motivated. On the only question the scene cares about — where the birds are — the dog knows and the men do not. The grant is made to the character who is right.
  • It is a bounded grant of a few paragraphs, made in a book whose narrator has established free movement from its opening. §5.2’s point about architecture applies: this is a masterstroke in Tolstoy’s architecture and would be a catastrophe in a limited third.

Tolstoy did this more than once. The novella Kholstomer (Strider, 1886) is focalised largely through a horse, and it uses the animal vantage for defamiliarisation in Shklovsky’s sense — the horse cannot understand property, and the incomprehension is an argument.

A translation note. The Laska passage exists in English in Garnett’s version (1901), the Maudes’, Pevear and Volokhonsky’s (2000), Bartlett’s and Schwartz’s (both 2014), and they differ in how far they permit the dog articulate thought — some render her impatience in near-human indirect discourse, others keep it closer to sensation. The structural claim survives all of them: the narration enters a non-human consciousness and is epistemically justified in doing so. The texture claim does not survive, and any student writing about the passage should name the version they used.

9.5 Rhys, Wide Sargasso Sea (1966) — allocation as argument

Architecture: sequential rather than alternating dual (§6.3, no. 3), with a single-page grant to a third vantage in the final part.

This is the chapter’s central case, because it is Woloch’s thesis dramatised: a novel that exists because a character in another novel was denied a vantage.

The structure.

Part Focaliser Approximate share Function
One Antoinette, as a child in Jamaica Roughly a quarter Establishes the world the second part will misread
Two The English husband, unnamed throughout, with an interpolated section by Antoinette Roughly half The appropriation performed in real time
Three Grace Poole, then Antoinette in England The remainder The keeper’s voice, then the voice Jane Eyre silenced

The argument carried by the allocation. In Jane Eyre, Bertha Mason has no vantage whatever. She is the compressed character at the limit: Woloch’s distortion in its purest form — a laugh, a face at a door, an animal noise — which is what a person becomes when a novel needs them present and has decided they are not a consciousness. Rhys’s novel grants her the vantage, and the grant is not a device but the entire reason the book exists.

Two features complicate the simple version of that account, and both are essential to teach.

(1) The husband holds the largest share. A novel written to restore Antoinette’s voice gives more of its pages to the man who takes it away. Critics disagree about whether this is a concession, a flaw, or the sharpest thing in the book — the demonstration that even a corrective must contend with the volume of the account it corrects. §8.9 treats it under the secret-favourite pattern; §12.2 sets it as a seminar question. The most defensible reading is that Rhys is dramatising the mechanism of character compression itself: the reader watches Antoinette being converted from a person into a case, from inside the consciousness doing the converting.

(2) The husband is never named. Jane Eyre’s Rochester is present in every structural particular and is denied the name. This is an exact inversion of the source novel’s allocation, and it is performed by withholding rather than by granting. A student who has understood §6.3, no. 13 — external focalisation as the refused grant — will see that Rhys uses two opposite instruments on the same target: she gives the husband a vantage and withholds his name, so that he acquires interiority without acquiring identity.

Grace Poole’s arrival. §8.4 treats it as the counterexample to the unearned late arrival. The additional point for this section is that Grace Poole is the Jane Eyre character whose account of Bertha the earlier novel accepts. Handing her a vantage, briefly, immediately before Antoinette’s final section, stages the competition of accounts that the whole novel is about.

The critical context. Gayatri Spivak’s “Three Women’s Texts and a Critique of Imperialism” (1985) reads Jane Eyre and Wide Sargasso Sea together as evidence about the imperial conditions of English literary subjectivity, and remains the standard starting point. It should be read alongside the dissent: Kamau Brathwaite and other Caribbean critics have questioned whether a white creole figure can stand for the Caribbean at all, which is a disagreement about who is entitled to a vantage rather than about how vantages are distributed within a text. Both positions are arguments about allocation, and putting them side by side is the most useful thing a seminar can do with this novel.

9.6 Stoker, Dracula (1897) — documentary polyphony

Architecture: documentary polyphony (§6.3, no. 8), with a refused grant (no. 13) at its centre.

The novel is a compilation: Jonathan Harker’s shorthand journal, Mina’s journal and later typescript, Lucy Westenra’s letters and diary, Dr Seward’s phonograph diary, newspaper cuttings, telegrams, a ship’s log, Van Helsing’s memorandum. Chapter 10 treats the prefatory note as a threshold device; the allocation question here is what the architecture buys and what it costs.

What the form buys.

  • Access without omniscience. The plot spans Transylvania, Whitby, London and the Carpathians and cannot be assembled from any single vantage. The documents supply coverage while preserving each witness’s ignorance, which is what a novel of dread requires.
  • A built-in knowledge boundary. Each document is limited by what its author knew when writing, and by what its author is willing to commit to paper. Seward’s phonograph confessions and Mina’s decorous journal have different boundaries, and the difference is characterisation.
  • Pacing by document type. Long journal entries alternate with telegrams and cuttings; the rhythm is achieved by form rather than by scene construction (§4.5).
  • Epistemology as premise. The prefatory note asserts that the papers have been arranged so that the facts may stand for themselves. The novel then demonstrates that the facts do not stand for themselves at all — a point Stoker makes explicit in the closing note, where Harker observes that the mass of material contains hardly a single authentic document, only typescript.

What the form costs. Two things, and the novel converts both into assets.

  • Voice redundancy. §8.5: the narrators write a common late-Victorian prose and are frequently hard to tell apart. The evidentiary logic requires it. Corroboration is produced by witnesses who agree.
  • Sympathy is hard to generate. A document is a performance for a reader, so interiority in it is always partly self-presentation. Stoker’s solution is Seward’s phonograph — a recording medium with no reader in view, in which a man can be heard to be wretched.

The refused grant is the novel’s most consequential decision. Dracula himself produces no document. He speaks at length in Harker’s journal, in Harker’s transcription, framed by Harker’s fear. The Count is therefore the only major figure in the book who is externally focalised throughout, and the refusal produces the character: a consciousness that cannot be entered is a consciousness that cannot be predicted, bargained with or pitied. Compare Rhys (§9.5), where the same instrument — withholding — is used on the character with the power rather than on the monster.

The teaching point. Dracula is a popular novel of 1897 that runs a rotating ensemble of eight vantages, declares its architecture on the first page, sustains an epistemological structure, and makes its central allocation decision by refusal. Students who suppose polyphony to be a modernist invention should begin here.

9.7 Lessing, The Golden Notebook (1962) — one consciousness, divided

Architecture: a formal hybrid — nested documentary (§6.3, no. 8) inside a frame (no. 7), all of it belonging to a single mind.

The novel is the chapter’s most useful case for a specific reason: it applies the whole apparatus of multiple viewpoint to one person.

The structure. A conventional short novel titled “Free Women”, about Anna Wulf and Molly, narrated in the third person and divided into five parts; between those parts, sections from four notebooks that Anna keeps — black (her time in Africa and the novel she wrote about it), red (politics and the Communist Party), yellow (fiction: a story about a woman called Ella, who is Anna at one remove), blue (a diary intended to record fact) — and finally a single golden notebook in which the divisions collapse.

Why the divisions exist. Anna divides her notebooks because she believes that keeping the parts of a life separate will prevent a breakdown. The novel’s argument is that the division is the breakdown, and that the golden notebook’s fusion is the recovery. The formal device and the psychological subject are the same thing.

What this demonstrates for the chapter.

  • Multiple focalisation does not require multiple people. Genette’s category (§3.1) is defined by the same events recurring from different positions, and Anna supplies four positions herself: the novelist’s, the militant’s, the fictionaliser’s and the diarist’s. The yellow notebook’s Ella is a focaliser who is and is not the focaliser of the blue.
  • The epistemological domain (§4.6) applied inwardly. The accounts do not reconcile. The “Free Women” sections, which look like the reliable outer novel, are exposed by the notebooks as the most simplified version of all — a conventional narrative Anna could write about herself because it leaves out what will not fit.
  • The frame’s authority is destabilised rather than established. Compare Wuthering Heights, where the frame stabilises. Here the outermost layer is the least trustworthy, which inverts the usual expectation of architecture 7.

A caution. Lessing’s 1971 preface complains that the book’s shape — which she describes as the argument — was widely ignored in favour of its content. It is a useful warning for a chapter of this kind: a viewpoint architecture is a statement, and readers routinely read past statements made in form. A writer who intends an architecture to carry an argument should assume that most readers will receive the argument only if the content supports it too.

9.8 Morrison, Beloved (1987) — the unattributed monologues

Architecture: hub-and-spokes (§6.3, no. 5) that becomes, for one movement, an unattributed choral voice (no. 11).

Chapter 9 treats the novel’s opening; Chapter 4 treats the chokecherry tree. The allocation question is what happens in Part Two.

The sequence. After a novel largely centred on Sethe, with substantial access to Denver, Paul D and Baby Suggs, Part Two presents a run of monologues: Sethe’s, Denver’s, and then Beloved’s — the last unpunctuated, without conventional sentence boundaries, in a syntax that will not resolve into ordinary speech. The sequence then merges into an antiphonal passage in which the three voices interleave, claim one another, and become difficult to assign.

What the architecture does.

(1) It grants viewpoint to a being whose ontological status the novel has not settled. Beloved may be a revenant, a survivor of the Middle Passage, a delusion, or all three. Granting her a monologue does not resolve the question; the monologue’s form — a consciousness without punctuation, in which a face in dark water and a woman picking flowers occupy the same grammatical plane — is a claim that the question cannot be resolved from inside. Compare §8.8: this is the corpse’s-eye view as premise, and the premise was declared in the novel’s opening sentences, where a house is spiteful and full of a baby’s venom.

(2) It converts possession into a formal property. Beloved’s assertion of belonging — I am Beloved and she is mine — is repeated across the sequence with the pronouns exchanged, so that the reader cannot determine who is claiming whom. Where an attributed narration would have had to state that the three women are losing their separateness, the unattributed one performs it. This is the strongest available demonstration that an architecture can carry content no sentence in the book has to state.

(3) It is bounded, and the boundedness is what makes it legible. The choral movement occupies a small fraction of the novel and is surrounded on both sides by attributed narration. §6.3’s note on architecture 11 applies: unattributed narration has a very high comprehension cost and is unusable for long stretches. Morrison’s placement — late, brief, after two hundred pages of ordinary access — is what allows the reader to understand what has happened to the grammar.

(4) The novel’s compression is deliberate and painful. The character with the least interiority in Beloved is the one whose act the whole book turns on — not because Sethe lacks a vantage, but because the act itself is approached repeatedly and entered only obliquely, from the outside, through the eyes of the men who came for her. Morrison’s allocation withholds the one interior the reader most wants, and the withholding is an argument about what can be represented.

9.9 A revision case: should Dov ever hold viewpoint?

The preceding readings analyse allocations already made. This case is a decision being taken, which is the form most useful in a workshop.

The situation. In the household narrative that runs through this book, Dov has been observed only from outside. Elena sees him in the doorway, holding it, giving her the key and withholding the reason (Chapter 3, The Dialogue Exchange). Nadia hears him in unanswered calls (Chapter 9, The Opening Pages). His sale of the shop and his deadline reach the reader by report (Chapter 14, The Subplot). The ledger is first seen as an object in Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail), and the looser hand that continues it after the father’s entry of March the ninth is discovered by Elena, without her reading it, in Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break): an object, not an act.

The question is whether the book should grant him viewpoint, and if so, in what form.

Version A: Dov as a rotating viewpoint character from page one

The alternative this chapter rejects. Constructed here so that its failure can be diagnosed rather than asserted.

Dov had been at the shop since seven, which was earlier than he needed to be and later than he wanted. The buyer’s agent was due at eleven. He had the papers in the folder on the passenger seat and had not told either of his sisters, and he told himself this was because Elena would want to argue about the valuation and Nadia would want to argue about everything else, and there was no version of that conversation that ended with the shop sold before another winter of standing orders and no stock moving. He went through into the back and put the kettle on and looked at the coat on the hook the way he looked at it every morning, which was quickly. Someone had to keep the place open. Someone had to answer the phone. He had been answering the phone since March and neither of them had asked him what it cost.

Diagnosis.

Test Result
1 — Necessity Fails. Every fact here is available by report or inference: the sale, the secrecy, the deadline, the resentment. The book already conveys all four from Elena’s and Nadia’s vantages.
2 — Domain Access (the shop before opening), and a thin sympathy (he is tired and unthanked). No contrast: Dov’s appraisal vocabulary — argue, valuation, standing orders — is a difference of subject matter, not of mind. No irony, since the reader learns nothing the sisters will not shortly learn.
3 — Investment Passes trivially at page one, where nobody has investment yet. This is the trap: a first grant always passes the investment test, which is why the test must be run together with tests 2 and 5.
4 — Distinguishability Fails on the decisive point. Swap this passage into Elena’s vantage and change three nouns, and it survives. All three siblings in this version audit, count and resent.
5 — Page-share Fails structurally. A third of the book to a character whose function in every other chapter of this book is to be the withheld reason.
10 — Character compression Fails, and this is the largest failure. Granting Dov a vantage from page one converts the mother from an unexplained silence into a reported one — because Dov knows what she said to him, and a focalised Dov must either think about it or be seen conspicuously not thinking about it. The book’s one deliberately open thread is closed by the allocation itself.

The mechanism of the failure. Version A destroys the book’s engine, and the destruction is a viewpoint decision rather than a plot one. The household narrative is an investigation: two sisters reconstructing what was withheld from them. Its dramatic value depends entirely on the reader’s information being close to the investigators’. Granting the withholder a vantage converts a mystery into a study of a man who has a secret, and Version A does not supply a replacement question strong enough to cover the loss. This is §8.10, arriving through the front door.

Version A also commits the redundant vantage (§8.5), and the reason is instructive: the three siblings were differentiated in this draft by circumstance — one drives, one counts, one sells — rather than by mind. §5.3’s four levels have not been applied.

Version B: a single bounded chapter, near the climax

This chapter’s conclusion, presented with the diagnostic behind it.

The grant. One chapter. Placed after Nadia finds the good pen in the coat pocket (Chapter 7, Backstory and Flashback) and before the three of them read the ledger together (Chapter 16, The Ending). It dramatises one thing: the evening of March the ninth, and the decision to go on writing.

The last entry in his father’s hand was three lines long and the third line stopped halfway, so that the doctor’s name — misspelled, then misspelled differently, then got right — trailed off into a scratch. The name meant nothing to him. Dov sat with it for a while. The leather was faintly warm from the lamp. He looked for the good pen where it was always kept, beside the till, and it was not there, and he did not think about that then; he thought about it for years afterwards. What he used was a ballpoint from the drawer that stuck. He wrote the date. He wrote the count. He did not write anything else, because his father had not written anything else on the ninth either, and it seemed to him that the ledger had a way of being written in, and that if he did it wrong someone would be able to tell.

Diagnosis.

Test Result
1 — Necessity Passes. No other vantage witnessed it, and report can state the fact — he kept the ledger going — but cannot dramatise the choosing. Note that the fact has already been stated by the object, about half the book earlier. What is unavailable by any other means is the making of the choice, which is a decision, not information.
2 — Domain Passes on three. Sympathy engineering: from outside, the continuation of the ledger reads as forgery or as the beginning of the sale; from inside, it is filial. Contrast: Dov’s instinct is to protect through concealment, mirroring his father, against his sisters’ instinct to expose. Dramatic irony: after this chapter the reader carries into the ledger scene a knowledge the sisters do not have.
3 — Investment Passes. Dov has been present continuously since the doorway (Chapter 3), wanting something legible (the sale, the silence held) and resisted. The cold-start charge (§6.6) is nil, because the reader has been curious about him for the length of the book.
4 — Distinguishability Passes, and by the four instruments of §5.3 rather than by dialect. Noticing: he registers the pen’s absence and the warmth of the leather, where Nadia would have registered a count and Elena a lock. Judgement: he appraises the ledger’s manner of being written in, which is a category neither sister has. Boundary: he does not know why the locks were changed, and the chapter does not tell the reader. Naming: he thinks his father, where Nadia thinks in objects and Elena thinks in questions.
5 — Page-share Passes. One chapter of a book otherwise divided between two sisters. The proportion is the point: the smallest possible allocation, at the highest possible value.
7 — Late-grant Passes. The three charges of §6.6 are paid at their minimum. Formal surprise: once. Attention withdrawal: one chapter. Cold start: none.
8 — Knowledge boundary Passes, and is the chapter’s chief instrument. Dov does not know where the good pen is; he does not know his father was ill, since the name on the third line means nothing to him and in the four months he keeps the ledger he writes forward and never reads back, so that he learns of the illness only when the three of them read the ledger together (Chapter 16), and not before; he does not know why his mother changed the locks. The narration’s the doctor’s name belongs to its small retrospective gap (he thought about it for years afterwards), not to what Dov understood that evening. The chapter is made of what he cannot see.
10 — Character compression Passes, narrowly, and only because of the boundary. The mother remains unexplained, because the vantage granted is one that does not know. Had the chapter been Ma’s, the book’s open thread would have closed.

What the revision did, procedurally.

  1. Reduced extent to the minimum that carries the payload. The conclusion — cheap because bounded — is §6.6’s cost curve applied.
  2. Moved the grant to the point of maximum prior curiosity, so that the cold-start charge falls to nothing.
  3. Selected a decision rather than a fact. The fact was already delivered by an object, about half the book earlier; the grant supplies only what an object cannot.
  4. Built distinguishability from noticing, judgement, boundary and naming rather than from voice (§5.3).
  5. Used the knowledge boundary as the chapter’s engine. The absent pen is the chapter’s centre and Dov does not understand it; the reader, who has read the Prologue, does. This is dramatic irony generated for free by a restriction.
  6. Preserved a character compression deliberately. The mother is not explained, and cannot be, from this vantage.

What Version B has not solved.

Four things, and a workshop should be made to argue about all of them.

  • The reader may want more. A single chapter that succeeds creates an appetite the book then refuses. Some readers will experience the refusal as discipline and others as a withheld pleasure, and this is not a defect that can be designed away — it is the cost of the bounded grant, and it is paid every time.
  • It solves Dov and leaves the mother. The character-compression test passes for the vantage granted and fails for the household as a whole: Ma appears throughout, changes the locks, speaks once at a remove, and is never entered. That is a defensible allocation — the book treats her silence as its one open thread — but it is an allocation, and a reader entitled to ask why always the siblings? has Eliot’s authority for the question.
  • The chapter risks the special-pleading failure (§4.4). A vantage granted immediately before a scene in which the character is judged is a vantage that looks arranged. The defence is that the chapter exculpates nothing: Dov still sells the shop, still signs without telling his sisters, and the ledger entries after March the ninth are still, in the plainest description, someone else writing in his father’s book. The interiority makes the act harder to name, not easier to forgive.
  • Dov’s never reading back needs a reason. The knowledge boundary rests on his writing forward for four months without turning back through his father’s pages. The ledger had a way of being written in begins to supply the reason, and the chapter may need a sentence more; a reader who doubts it will doubt the boundary, and with it the claim that none of the three knew of the illness before the end.

A note on the hypothetical. Nothing above dramatises the mother’s vantage, and the specimen in this section should not be read as evidence that the book intends to grant her one. Her viewpoint is used here strictly as a test case for §7.1, no. 10 — the character whose exclusion the allocation must be able to defend.
