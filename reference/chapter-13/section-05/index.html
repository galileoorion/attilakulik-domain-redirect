Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

5. Multiplicity: The Economy of the Grant

Continue in Core

5.1 The principle, stated as a constraint

Multiplicity — established in Chapter 1 and reused throughout this book — is the practice of making a single unit of narrative discharge several functions at once. At the scale of the sentence it is a virtue. At the scale of the viewpoint grant it becomes something harder: an admission requirement.

The reasoning is a constraint problem with three terms.

  • A viewpoint grant is the most expensive instrument in the novelist’s kit, because it commits the book’s scarcest resource (identification, mind-modelling capacity, page share) for the duration of the book rather than for the duration of a scene.
  • Its commonest justification — access — is the one for which the cheapest substitutes exist. Report, inference, reassignment, document and ellipsis (§4.1) can all deliver information, and four of the five cost almost nothing.
  • Therefore a grant justified by access alone is, in nearly every case, an expensive solution to a cheap problem.

Which yields the principle in its operational form:

Full access to a mind is the costliest tool available. Spending it on plain information delivery rarely earns back what it cost. A grant should be able to name two domains, and one of them should not be access.

The threshold of two is a working heuristic rather than a law, and §4.8 has already qualified it. What matters is the discipline it enforces: before granting viewpoint, the writer must be able to complete two sentences —

  1. This vantage gives the reader ______ that no existing vantage could deliver.
  2. The cheaper instruments I rejected are ______, and I rejected them because ______.

A writer who cannot complete the second sentence has not made a decision; they have followed a convenience.

The corollary, which is less obvious and more useful. Multiplicity in this chapter runs in both directions. A well-chosen grant does more than one job — but it should also be true that the character receiving it was already doing more than one job in the book. A vantage awarded to a character who exists only to occupy it will read as an appointment. The strongest grants in fiction go to characters who were structurally load-bearing before they were focalisers: Esther Summerson is the plot’s hidden centre; Levin is the novel’s thematic counterweight; Jason Compson is the family’s economic engine. The grant confirms an existing importance rather than manufacturing one.

5.2 Anatomy: a viewpoint reallocation dissected

The most instructive single sentence in English fiction on this subject is George Eliot’s, at the opening of Chapter 29 of Middlemarch (1872):

One morning, some weeks after her arrival at Lowick, Dorothea — but why always Dorothea? Was her point of view the only possible one with regard to this marriage?

The sentence begins a chapter in the manner the novel has trained the reader to expect, breaks off mid-clause, interrogates its own procedure, and reallocates the reader’s attention to Casaubon — from whose interiority the following pages proceed. It performs, in twenty-eight words, the entire argument of this chapter.

Textual element Functions discharged
One morning, some weeks after her arrival at Lowick, The ordinary machinery of a chapter opening: time anchor, place anchor, continuity with the preceding narrative. It establishes a norm so that the break can register as a break (Chapter 5 §6.5 on drift; Chapter 6 on the expectations a chapter division sets)
Dorothea Names the expected focaliser — the character the reader has been trained across twenty-eight chapters to follow. Woloch’s protagonist convention invoked so it can be suspended
— but The syntactic pivot. A dash and a conjunction perform a viewpoint change that most novels would spend a section break on
why always Dorothea? Converts an allocation from a habit into a question. The word always is doing the structural work: it identifies the reader’s expectation as cumulative, built by repetition, and therefore as something the narrator has been building on purpose
Was her point of view Names the object under discussion in the reader’s own critical vocabulary. Eliot is not merely shifting focalisation; she is telling the reader that focalisation exists and is chosen
the only possible one Establishes plurality as a fact about the world rather than a device of the book. There are other possible views because there are other people
with regard to this marriage? Fixes the scope. Not about everything — about this marriage, the specific object on which two vantages will now be shown to differ. Bal’s subject–object relation (§3.1) made explicit in the prose

Six observations follow, and they are the substance of this section.

(1) The grant is bounded and the boundary is announced. Eliot does not make Casaubon a rotating viewpoint character. She takes the reader inside him for a stretch, and returns. This is the bounded grant (§6.3, no. 6) executed at chapter scale, and it is the cheapest form of a later grant available.

(2) The reallocation is performed by the narrator, not by the structure. No white space, no heading, no chapter break. This is possible only because the book’s architecture is the mobile omniscient narrator (§6.3, no. 10), which has held the right of free movement from its opening pages. A limited-third novel attempting the same move at Chapter 29 would be committing the omniscient slip (§8.6). The same sentence is a masterstroke in one architecture and an error in another, which is the chapter’s recurring lesson.

(3) The move is a sympathy grant, not an exculpation. Casaubon is not revealed to be secretly admirable. He is revealed to have an interior — to be, as the narrator goes on to insist, as intensely conscious and as spiritually hungry as anyone else. Nothing about the marriage improves. What is withdrawn is the reader’s licence to find him merely absurd. Compare §4.4’s distinction between exculpatory and non-exculpatory sympathy.

(4) It multiplies. The sentence serves sympathy engineering (Casaubon acquires an inside), contrast (two incompatible readings of one marriage), and — by naming its own procedure — a thematic argument about the moral cost of attention that the novel pursues to its last page.

(5) It is a lesson in what an established convention buys. Eliot can afford this because she has spent twenty-eight chapters establishing a narrator who generalises, addresses the reader, and moves. The move costs almost nothing here because it was paid for there. §6.6’s cost curve is this observation formalised.

(6) It is the seed of the chapter’s Woloch material. “Why always Dorothea?” is, in a single clause, the question The One vs. the Many takes a book to ask. Eliot’s narrator notices that attention is finite, that its distribution is a choice, and that the choice has moral weight — and then acts on the noticing.

The architecture in which this sentence sits is analysed at §9.3; the sentence is treated here for its allocation mechanics rather than for its place in the novel’s design.

5.3 Where the multiplying work actually lives

Novice revision looks for viewpoint differentiation in voice — in dialect, in sentence rhythm, in a character’s verbal tics. Voice differentiation is real and Chapter 3 treats it. But it is the most expensive place to work, the easiest to overdo, and, in third-person narration, frequently unavailable: a novel with four close-third viewpoints in a single social class cannot make them sound different without making three of them affected.

The multiplying work of a viewpoint grant lives in four humbler places.

1. Selection of detail — what this consciousness registers. Two people enter the shop. One notices that the register drawer sticks and that the stock has not moved; the other notices that the coat still hangs on the hook and that someone has been brushing it. Nothing has been said about either person, and they are now entirely distinct. This is the most reliable instrument in the chapter, because it costs no additional words: the sentences were going to describe something, and choosing which thing is free.

Note that selection also carries the domains. Differential noticing is the primary carrier of contrast (§4.3); the noticing of a clue nobody else registers is access; the noticing of a kindness is sympathy.

2. The idiom of judgement — what appraisal words leak into the narration. Free indirect style is not principally a matter of dialect; it is a matter of evaluation. When a third-person narration calls a room poky, or a decision typical, or a brother impossible, the judgement belongs to the focaliser, and the reader knows whose head they are in before any name appears. Cohn’s narrated monologue (§3.2) is the technical name; the practical instruction is that the adjectives are the viewpoint.

Two characters can share a vocabulary and differ entirely in what they are willing to appraise. One will not permit herself to evaluate her mother; another evaluates everyone. That asymmetry is characterisation performed by the narration itself.

3. The knowledge boundary — what this vantage cannot know, and whether the prose respects it. Every grant draws a line, and the line is a resource rather than a restriction. A chapter that observes its boundary strictly produces inference, misreading and dread; a chapter that leaks produces the failure at §8.7. The instruction that follows is counter-intuitive: when a viewpoint chapter feels thin, the usual fault is that it has been allowed to know too much, not too little. Interiority is generated by uncertainty. A focaliser who understands the situation has nothing to do but report it.

The boundary also carries irony (§4.2) for free: a vantage that misreads a situation the reader has understood from elsewhere is a dramatic-irony engine that costs no additional structure.

4. Naming — what this vantage calls people, places and acts. The cheapest differentiator in the language and the least used. A sister who thinks of him as Dov, a sister who thinks of him as my brother, and a mother who thinks of him by a name nobody else uses have been distinguished at no cost in words. Naming carries relation, distance, era, class and grievance simultaneously, and it operates below the reader’s conscious notice, which is where the most durable effects live.

The same applies to acts. One vantage calls it selling the shop; another calls it what he did; a third calls it the arrangement. Three nouns, three moral positions, no exposition.

The synthesis. Voice differentiation makes viewpoints sound different. Selection, judgement, boundary and naming make them be different. A draft whose viewpoints fail the distinguishability test (§7.1, no. 4) should be repaired at these four levels before anyone attempts a dialect.

5.4 The ceiling: how many vantages a book can carry

There is no correct number, and any figure offered is a convention rather than a discovery. What can be stated is the relation that governs the ceiling, and the symptoms of having exceeded it.

The relation has four terms:

Term Effect on the ceiling
Available length Raises it, roughly linearly, and only if the length is distributed
What the reader is asked to do with each vantage Raises it sharply if the task is triangulation; lowers it sharply if the task is investment
Distinguishability Raises it; fifteen instantly identifiable voices are cheaper than four interchangeable ones
Legibility of the pattern Raises it; a declared architecture converts frame construction into frame recall (§3.4)

The commonest miscalculation in ambitious drafts is to treat only the first term as a variable. A writer with a long book assumes they can afford many vantages, and produces the diluted ensemble (§8.3) — because length distributed across too many centres does not deepen any of them, it merely delays the reader’s arrival at each.

Three symptoms of the ceiling being exceeded:

Symptom What the reader experiences
Re-identification lag Each section opens with a beat of who is this again?, even when the anchor is correct — a failure of memory, not of anchoring
Interchangeable response The reader cannot predict how any given vantage will react to news, because none has a settled temperament
Relief at return The reader is pleased to get back to one particular vantage and tolerates the others. This is the strongest single diagnostic, and it identifies the book’s real protagonist (§8.9)

The limit case, and why it is not a counter-argument. As I Lay Dying has fifteen focalisers in fifty-nine sections and perhaps sixty thousand words. On the arithmetic of page share it is impossible: Vardaman’s total allocation would not fill a short story. It is not diluted, for three reasons that between them constitute the real rule.

  1. The reader’s task is triangulation, not investment. The book is an epistemological structure (§4.6) whose subject is a corpse and a journey seen from fifteen angles. Nobody is asked to become Cash.
  2. The voices are instantly identifiable, by idiom, obsession and syntax. Vardaman’s fish, Cash’s list of thirteen reasons for the bevel, Darl’s uncanny fluency. Identification costs the reader nothing.
  3. The distribution is grossly unequal and the inequality is the design. Darl has nineteen of the fifty-nine sections; several characters have one. This is not an ensemble in which everyone is served; it is a hub with a large number of spokes, and the hub is a man who ends the novel insane and speaking of himself in the third person. §8.9.

The generalisable statement is therefore not how many but:

A book can carry as many vantages as it can make instantly identifiable and as its reader’s task requires. What it cannot carry is many vantages all asking to be inhabited equally.

5.5 Exercise: the page-share audit

A practical procedure, and the most reliable instrument in this chapter for diagnosing a structural problem that feels like a prose problem.

Procedure.

  1. List every viewpoint character.
  2. For each, record: total word count of their sections; number of sections; the page at which they first receive viewpoint; the page at which they last hold it; and the longest gap between two of their sections.
  3. Convert word counts to percentages of the whole.
  4. Plot the sections in book order as a strip — one letter per section — so the pattern is visible as a shape.
  5. Beside each name, write in one sentence the domains the grant serves (§4).

Interpretation.

Pattern Diagnosis First move
One vantage above 60%, others in single figures A single-viewpoint novel with decorations. Possibly correct — but the minor grants should be examined for necessity Convert the small grants to report, or make them bounded and deliberate (§6.3, no. 6)
Shares approximately equal across five or more An ensemble governed by fairness rather than need. The commonest cause of dilution Identify the real protagonist by the “relief at return” test (§5.4) and rebuild the proportions around them
A vantage whose first section falls after the 60% mark A late grant. May be right; must be defended §7.1, test 7
A vantage with one long gap exceeding a quarter of the book The reader will re-enter as if to a stranger Either add a bridging section or accept the gap and re-anchor heavily (Chapter 5 §4.5)
The strip is a strict alternation for the whole book The metronome (§4.5). The pattern has stopped meaning anything Vary the interval according to each thread’s state
A vantage that stops entirely two-thirds through Either an unannounced bounded grant or an abandoned thread Check whether the reader is left waiting; abandonment is felt even when the plot does not require the vantage
The domains column repeats “access” The book’s viewpoint structure is a delivery mechanism §5.1

A refinement worth the extra hour. Repeat step 5 for the characters who have no viewpoint, recording for each: how many scenes they appear in, whether their motives are ever explained, and whether the explanation comes from them or from someone judging them. That is the character-compression test (§7.1, no. 10), and its output is the part of the audit most likely to change the book.
