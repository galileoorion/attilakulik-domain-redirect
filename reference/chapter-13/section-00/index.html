The Viewpoint Shift

Chapter Abstract

A viewpoint shift is a change in whose consciousness the narration is filtered through. The mechanics of executing any single crossing — the handoff, the anchor, the cost of a frame switch — belong to Chapter 5 (The Transition). This chapter sits a level above them and asks the prior question: who is granted a consciousness at all, how many such grants a book can carry, and when in the book each is made.

It proceeds in four movements:

  1. Theory — four independent bodies of work that together convert a craft intuition into an analytic. Genette’s separation of who speaks from who perceives, refined by Bal, Stanzel, Cohn and Booth, supplies the vocabulary. Alex Woloch’s account of character-space and the character-system supplies the rigour: narrative attention is a scarce, distributed resource, and the distribution is itself a formal and a political fact. The cognitive literature on theory of mind and situation models supplies the processing account, and Suzanne Keen’s scepticism supplies the necessary corrective to it. A historical strand establishes that the single limited third person is a recent and local dominance.
  2. Taxonomy — the five domains a viewpoint grant can serve (access, dramatic irony, contrast, sympathy engineering, structural pacing), each anatomised in terms of its textual carriers and its characteristic under- and over-delivery, expanded here with a proposed sixth, the epistemological domain: a vantage granted not to show what happened but to demonstrate that no single vantage can know it.
  3. Method — the principle of multiplicity applied to viewpoint allocation across a whole book; a taxonomy of thirteen viewpoint architectures; the distinction between the first grant and the later grant; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol.
  4. Application — twelve failure patterns, each with a published counterexample; eight close readings; a revision case drawn from the household narrative that runs through this book; notes on other forms; and a full apparatus.

The chapter’s governing claim is that viewpoint is an allocation, not a technique — a distribution of a finite resource across a cast, whose shape is legible, arguable, and revisable. Its principal corrective is historical: what contemporary workshops call head-hopping names the violation of a convention that Fielding, Eliot and Tolstoy did not hold. And its principal extension is Woloch’s: the question is not only who receives a viewpoint, but what happens to the people who do not.

A note on the examples. The novels analysed here are chosen because each displays a mechanism clearly. A concordance mapping each concept to the novel that best displays it appears at §10.6.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Distinguish a structural viewpoint shift from head-hopping, from a change of narrator, from a change of narrative distance, and from a transition, and explain why the distinctions carry different remedies.
  • Deploy the vocabulary of focalisation accurately — zero, internal and external; fixed, variable and multiple — and say what it can describe that “point of view” cannot.
  • Analyse a novel’s character-system in Woloch’s terms, identifying which characters are granted space, which are compressed, and what the compression produces.
  • Identify which of the six domains a given viewpoint grant serves, and diagnose grants that serve none.
  • Classify a novel’s viewpoint architecture against the thirteen-part taxonomy at §6.3, and conduct substitution tests against the alternatives.
  • Apply the ten-test diagnostic protocol to a draft and produce a prioritised revision plan, including a page-share audit.
  • Historicise the prohibition on head-hopping, and state which of this chapter’s cautions describe narrative and which describe Anglo-American workshop practice since roughly 1960.
  • Evaluate the empathy-budget metaphor itself, including the scarcity assumption it imports and the cases in which the assumption is false.

How to Use This Chapter

Reader Route Purpose
Student in a seminar §§1–6 in order, then §9, then §12 exercises Conceptual grounding, worked demonstration, guided practice
Writer in revision §7 (diagnostics) → §8 (failures) → §11 (checklist), returning to §6.3 when the architecture itself is in question Fast triage of a live manuscript
Teacher or workshop leader §3.3, 3.5, 8.1 and 9 for material; §12.1 for assignments; §12.3 rubric A teaching sequence built around the chapter’s two arguments: allocation, and the historicity of the head-hopping rule

A caution before beginning. This chapter contains taxonomies, tests and checklists. None of them is a formula. They are instruments for noticing, and their proper output is a question, not a verdict. The chapter’s own §16 argues against its central metaphor. A student who finishes able to apply the ten tests but unable to say why Middlemarch fails nearly all of them and is nonetheless the chapter’s central authority has read the apparatus and missed the argument.
