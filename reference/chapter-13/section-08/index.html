Chapter 13 · The Viewpoint Shift

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern below is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what actually goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and — mandatorily — the exception: a published work that commits the “error” and succeeds.

The exceptions are not decorative. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults and not writers who can make choices. In every case the pattern fails because of a mechanism, not because of a surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless.

The first pattern is the clearest case in this textbook of a rule that names a convention rather than a mechanism, and it is treated at proportionate length.

8.1 Head-Hopping

Symptom. Within a single continuous scene, the narration reports the interiority of more than one character — moving from what she thinks to what he thinks, sometimes across a paragraph break, sometimes within a sentence — without a structural boundary.

Mechanism, as usually described. The reader, having bound their model of the scene to one consciousness, must rebuild it around another with no warning. In Emmott’s terms (§3.4) the contextual frame’s viewpoint slot is silently overwritten; in Zwaan and Radvansky’s, the protagonist dimension shifts without a marker. The result is a beat of uncertainty about whose perceptions are being reported, and a diffuse loss of intimacy: a reader who has been in nobody’s head in particular has been in nobody’s head.

Mechanism, more accurately. The description above is true of a specific configuration and false as a general account, and the difference is the discrimination this chapter depends on.

What produces the reader’s uncertainty is not movement. It is mismatch between the narration’s behaviour and the convention the book has established. A novel that has spent eighty pages confined to one consciousness has taught the reader that confinement is the rule; the ninety-first page’s excursion into a second mind is therefore read as an accident, and the reader’s response is not confusion about whose thought it was but a small loss of confidence in the book. A novel that has established from its first page a narrating intelligence with the whole world in view has taught the reader something different, and the same excursion is read as that intelligence doing what it always does.

Two further distinctions sharpen this.

(1) Governed movement versus opportunistic movement. The nineteenth-century mobile narrator moves on a principle: to compare, to correct, to widen, to judge. The workshop error moves when the plot requires a fact the current focaliser cannot supply. Readers detect the difference without being able to name it, because opportunistic movement correlates with information delivery and governed movement correlates with meaning. The test at §7.1, no. 9 formalises the distinction: does the narration move where it has something to say, or where it has something to convey?

(2) The strength of the narrating voice. Free access requires a narrator substantial enough to be the reader’s constant. Eliot’s narrator, Tolstoy’s, Thackeray’s and Fielding’s are all continuously audible presences with a temperament, a vocabulary and a set of opinions. The reader’s stability comes from them, not from any character. A contemporary novel written in transparent, self-effacing close third has no such presence, and when its narration moves there is nothing for the reader to hold. Omniscience is not free access; it is free access underwritten by a narrator. A draft that has the access without the narrator has taken on the liability without the asset.

Persistence. Three causes, of which only the first is a fault of skill.

  • Information pressure. The scene needs the reader to know what the other character is feeling, and the fastest route is to say so.
  • The writer’s own vantage. A writer knows what everyone in the room thinks and does not experience the restriction as a restriction. Limitation is an artificial discipline, which is why it must be taught and why it decays under pressure.
  • Reading history. Writers who have read a great deal of nineteenth-century fiction have absorbed a mobile convention and reproduce it inside a limited one. This is a mismatch, not an incapacity, and it is repaired by choosing an architecture rather than by learning a rule.

Remedy. Not “stay in one head”. The remedy is a decision at the level of architecture (§6.3), enforced consistently:

  1. Decide the architecture — limited or mobile — and write it down.
  2. If limited, replace every marked sentence (test 8) with what the focaliser could observe or infer. The scenes almost always improve, because inference is dramatic and knowledge is not.
  3. If mobile, establish the right in the first pages and exercise it throughout, including where it is not needed. A narrator who only moves when convenient has not established a convention.
  4. Do not do both. The commonest live manuscript is one that is limited by default and mobile at crisis points, and it fails in both directions at once.

The exception — and it is very large.

Three of the novels this chapter relies on most move between minds inside a single scene constantly, deliberately, and to effects unavailable by any other means.

Middlemarch (1872). Eliot’s narration moves within scenes as a matter of course. In the Lydgate and Rosamond marriage, the reader is handed the same exchange from both sides in consecutive paragraphs — his exasperation and her serene incomprehension, each entirely reasonable from inside — and the marriage’s tragedy is constituted by the reader’s ability to hold both. No sequence of alternating chapters could produce this, because the effect depends on simultaneity: the reader must know what each is thinking at the moment the other speaks.

Eliot also theorises the practice. The pier-glass parable at the opening of Chapter 27 — a scratched mirror on which a candle produces the illusion of concentric circles, an illusion that moves with the candle — is a statement about focalisation offered as a parable about egoism. Every consciousness organises the same scratches around itself. A narration able to move the candle is a narration able to demonstrate that the circles are not there. This is not a novel that would be improved by discipline; it is a novel whose subject requires the mobility that a workshop would call an error.

Anna Karenina (1878). Tolstoy’s mid-scene movement is more rapid than Eliot’s and less announced. At the races, the narration passes among Vronsky riding, Anna watching, and Karenin watching Anna being watched — a triangulation that is the whole social catastrophe in miniature, executed in a few pages, and impossible to construct from any single vantage. At Anna’s sickbed, the movement among Karenin, Vronsky and Anna produces a scene in which three people are simultaneously transformed and none of them can be sure what has happened. Cutting the scene into three chapters would destroy it: the transformation is mutual and instantaneous.

Tolstoy’s most extreme instance is also the most instructive for a theory of focalisation. During the woodcock shooting, the narration enters the setter Laska — her scent-world, her impatience with the men’s slowness, her judgement of her master. Every definition offered in §3.1 is tested by the passage, and the reason it is not a stunt matters: the dog’s vantage is epistemically superior to the men’s on the only question the scene cares about, which is where the birds are. Tolstoy is not being whimsical. He is demonstrating that consciousness is not co-extensive with reason, which is the argument Levin spends the novel failing to articulate. (§9.4.)

War and Peace (1869). The largest instance and the one that most clearly exposes the prohibition’s provinciality. Tolstoy moves within a single battlefield scene from a general’s calculation to a private’s confusion to a horse’s stumble to a narratorial disquisition on the impossibility of any of them knowing what is happening. That last move — from character consciousness to a narrator who exceeds every character — is the manoeuvre a modern editor would flag most confidently, and it is the book’s thesis. The novel argues that history is not made by the people who believe they are making it; a narration restricted to any participant could not make the argument, because every participant believes otherwise.

The discrimination to carry away. What Eliot and Tolstoy do differs from workshop head-hopping in four respects, and a manuscript can be tested against all four:

Nineteenth-century mobile narration Workshop head-hopping
Establishment The right is declared on the first page and exercised throughout The narration is limited by default and moves under pressure
A narrator A continuous, audible intelligence the reader can hold Transparent narration with nothing to hold
Motive for each move Comparison, correction, irony, judgement Delivery of a fact
Predictability The reader learns that movement is the book’s mode The reader cannot tell whether the next paragraph will move

The rule as it should be taught. Not never change viewpoint within a scene, but: do not exercise a right the book has not established. The prohibition on head-hopping is a prohibition on inconsistency masquerading as a prohibition on a technique.

8.2 The Information Mule

Symptom. A viewpoint character whose sections exist to deliver plot information the protagonist could not access — a colleague who overhears, a servant who witnesses, an official who has the file — and who has no independent want or complicating interiority.

Mechanism. The most expensive instrument in the kit (§5.1) applied to the cheapest problem. The reader is asked to enter a mind and finds it furnished only with the plot. Because inside access carries an implicit promise of significance (Rabinowitz’s rules of notice, Chapter 9 §3.2), the mule also generates an unredeemed promise: the reader assumes this person will matter and keeps a slot open for them.

Persistence. It solves a real problem, quickly, at a stage of drafting when structural costs are invisible. It is also invisible in isolation: any single mule chapter reads acceptably.

Remedy. Test 1, then one of three moves: reassign the scene to an existing vantage; convert the content to report, document or inference; or give the mule a want that the information costs them something to acquire, which converts an access grant into an access-plus-sympathy grant.

The exception. Wilkie Collins’s The Woman in White (1860) is built almost entirely of information mules and is one of the great popular novels in English. Collins’s minor narrators — the housekeeper, the cook, the doctor, the lawyer’s clerk — appear once each, testify, and vanish. They have no arcs. The structure works because Collins has framed the whole book as a legal deposition in which each witness speaks only to what they can attest, so that narrowness is the form’s virtue rather than its defect: a witness with an independent emotional life would be a worse witness. The lesson generalises. An information mule fails in a novel of consciousness and succeeds in a novel of testimony, because in the second the reader has been told what a narrator is for.

8.3 The Diluted Ensemble

Symptom. Five, six, eight viewpoint characters in a book without the length to develop any of them. Coverage is total; investment is nil. Readers report that the book is “hard to get into” and cannot say why.

Mechanism. The coverage–depth trade (§1.1) resolved by default rather than by decision. Each additional vantage adds not one item to the reader’s load but a new set of relations (Zunshine, §3.4), so the cost grows faster than the cast. The compounding symptom is re-identification lag (§5.4): the reader spends the opening of each section remembering rather than reading.

Persistence. Fairness. Writers who have imagined a family or a crew fully find it invidious to grant three of them interiority and not the rest, and the ensemble grows by conscience rather than by design. Genre convention reinforces it: readers of epic fantasy and large-cast thrillers expect coverage, and the habit migrates into books that cannot support it.

Remedy. The page-share audit (§5.5), then conversion to hub-and-spokes (§6.3, no. 5). Most diluted ensembles are hubs whose author will not admit the hub. The second remedy, less often considered, is to shorten the sections: an ensemble becomes tractable when each visit is two pages rather than twenty, because the reader is asked to sample rather than to settle.

The exception. As I Lay Dying (1930): fifteen focalisers, fifty-nine sections, one short novel. §5.4 gives the three reasons it is not diluted — the reader’s task is triangulation rather than investment, the voices are instantly identifiable, and the distribution is grossly and deliberately unequal. The Grapes of Wrath (1939) is a second and different exception: Steinbeck alternates a family narrative with intercalary chapters focalised through a collective (§6.3, no. 9), so that the “ensemble” is not a set of individuals competing for attention but a relation between one family and a people. Both books show the same principle: an ensemble is dilute when its members compete for the same reader function, and robust when they perform different ones.

8.4 The Unearned Late Arrival

Symptom. A new viewpoint character appears at the two-thirds mark or later, holds several chapters, and pulls attention from vantages the reader has invested in.

Mechanism. The three charges of §6.6 incurred together: formal surprise, attention withdrawal, and an investment cold start at the point in the book where the reader is least willing to begin again. The reader’s response is characteristically not confusion but impatience — they skim to get back.

Persistence. It is a plot solution. The third act requires information or presence that existing vantages cannot supply, and a new one is the fastest fix.

Remedy. Bound the grant (§6.3, no. 6); or plant the vantage earlier, even briefly, so the arrival is a return; or solve the underlying plot problem, which is usually that the climax has been placed where the protagonist cannot reach it.

The exception. Two, and they are among the most admired final movements in the modern novel.

Wide Sargasso Sea (1966) opens its Part Three in the voice of Grace Poole — a servant who has not narrated before, who narrates for a couple of pages, and who never returns. By every criterion above this should fail catastrophically: a brand-new vantage in the closing movement of a very short book. It succeeds because it performs the novel’s argument formally. Rhys’s subject is who is permitted to speak about a madwoman, and the last thing the reader is given before Antoinette’s final section is the voice of her keeper — the person Jane Eyre used to explain her. The grant is legible as the book’s meaning rather than as its author’s convenience, which is §6.6’s success condition.

The Sound and the Fury (1929) does something structurally comparable in reverse: after three first-person sections, the fourth abandons the device entirely for a third-person narration centred on Dilsey. A reader trained across three sections to expect an interior monologue is handed, at the end, an outside. The effect is of air entering a sealed room, and it is unavailable to any book that had not sealed itself first. Faulkner then added, seventeen years later, an Appendix in yet another mode — a genealogical register — which some editions print first and which reopens the question of where the novel’s authority resides.

8.5 The Redundant Vantage

Symptom. Two viewpoint characters who notice, judge and process events so similarly that granting both adds coverage but no new information, irony or contrast — indistinguishable interiorities wearing different names.

Mechanism. The distinguishability failure of §7.1, test 4. Its deep cause is almost always that the two characters were differentiated by circumstance rather than by mind: different ages, jobs and problems, but the same attention, the same appraisal vocabulary, the same theory of causation.

Persistence. Circumstantial difference feels like difference while writing. It is also the kind of difference a synopsis can record, so a draft can look varied on a plan and read uniform on the page.

Remedy. §5.3’s four levels, in order: differential noticing first, because it is free. If the repair fails, the two characters should probably be one, and the merge is nearly always an improvement — a single character with two problems is more interesting than two characters with one each.

The exception. Dracula (1897) is frequently and correctly criticised for the sameness of its narrators: Jonathan Harker, Mina, Dr Seward and Lucy write a common late-Victorian epistolary prose, and readers routinely lose track of which journal they are in. The novel is nonetheless a masterwork of viewpoint structure, and the redundancy is part of why. The book’s evidentiary logic requires corroboration, and corroboration requires witnesses who agree. Four sharply differentiated observers producing four idiosyncratic accounts would have produced doubt; four decent, similar, credible people producing convergent accounts produce a case. Stoker’s redundancy is doing the work that differentiation would have undone. The generalisable point: redundancy fails in a novel of consciousness and can succeed in a novel of testimony (compare §8.2).

8.6 The Omniscient Slip

Symptom. A book that is otherwise limited produces, every twenty or thirty pages, a sentence no focaliser could have generated: a fact about a character’s future, a description of the focaliser’s own face, a generalisation in a register the character does not possess.

Mechanism. Stanzel’s circle (§3.2) in action. The narrative situations shade into one another, and a book drifts from figural towards authorial without any identifiable moment of change. The reader does not consciously notice individual instances; the cumulative effect is a slight loss of the sense that the book is inside anyone, which is usually misdiagnosed as a prose problem.

Persistence. These sentences are frequently the best in the draft. The authorial register is where a writer’s intelligence is most comfortable, and suppressing it feels like suppressing quality.

Remedy. Test 8, then a decision rather than a deletion. If the marked sentences are the book’s best, the book may want a mobile-omniscient architecture and be fighting it. That is a legitimate finding, and acting on it — establishing the narrator properly from page one — is a better outcome than sanding the sentences down.

The exception. To the Lighthouse (1927), Part II, “Time Passes”. Woolf’s novel is otherwise the roving third at its most refined, moving among consciousnesses at close range. The middle section abandons human focalisation altogether: an empty house, ten years, the wind in the passages, and the deaths of three of the novel’s people delivered in square brackets as parenthetical announcements. It is the most extreme viewpoint slip imaginable — from inside minds to no mind at all — and it is the greatest thing in the book, because the subject of the section is the indifference of duration to consciousness. Chapter 5 §9.1 treats it as an ellipsis; the allocation-side observation is that Woolf withdraws the grant from everybody, and the withdrawal is the meaning.

War and Peace supplies the second exception in its historiographical passages, where the narration lifts clear of every character to argue about causation. Modern editors have proposed cutting them since the book appeared. They are the reason the novel is about what it is about.

8.7 The Vantage That Knows Too Much

Symptom. A viewpoint character reports events they did not witness, thoughts they could not have read, or dialogue they were not present for — usually at the moments the plot most needs conveying.

Mechanism. A knowledge-boundary breach (test 8) clustered rather than scattered. Where the omniscient slip is a drift, this is a raid: the writer needs a fact delivered and borrows the vantage’s credibility to deliver it. The cost is specific and severe. The knowledge boundary is what makes a limited vantage dramatic — it generates inference, misreading and dread — and a boundary that yields under pressure yields where those effects were most available.

Persistence. It is invisible from inside, because the writer knows the fact and the sentence reads smoothly. It is also encouraged by the retrospective first person, where a narrator may legitimately know things the experiencing self did not, and the licence is easily overdrawn.

Remedy. Replace knowledge with its shadow — the inference, the wrong guess, the thing half-heard. Where the fact is indispensable, supply it by the cheap instruments of §4.1 rather than by narratorial fiat.

The exception. The Great Gatsby (1925). Nick Carraway narrates, in intimate detail, scenes at which he was not present: Gatsby and Daisy’s first kiss five years before Nick met either of them; Wilson’s movements on the afternoon of the shooting; Gatsby’s boyhood as James Gatz. Fitzgerald partly covers the breach by attributing some of it to later testimony — Jordan’s account, Michaelis’s — but the coverage is patchy and the most famous passages are unattributable. Critics have argued about this since the 1940s, and the honest verdict is that the novel commits the fault and is unharmed. The reason appears to be that Nick’s project is explicitly the construction of Gatsby: he tells us on the first page that he has been turning something over ever since, and a narrator engaged in making a legend is licensed to imagine.

Wuthering Heights (1847) supplies a second: Nelly Dean reports verbatim conversations from decades earlier, including scenes she cannot have attended, and Emily Brontë makes no serious attempt to cover it. The frame architecture (§6.3, no. 7) absorbs the strain, and readers who notice tend to file it under Nelly’s unreliability rather than under the author’s carelessness — which is itself a finding: a vantage with a known interest in the story can overreach more safely than a neutral one.

8.8 The Corpse’s-Eye View

Symptom. A viewpoint that cannot exist within the book’s own rules: the dead narrating, a character reporting their own unconsciousness, a vantage that persists past the moment it was extinguished.

Mechanism. A breach not of the knowledge boundary but of the ontological one. Where a knowledge breach costs credibility, an ontological breach costs the world’s rules — and the world’s rules are what a reader has been using to calibrate every threat in the book. If death does not end a vantage, the reader must ask what else the book’s stated conditions do not mean.

Persistence. It solves the hardest access problem in fiction — the interiority of a death — and it is emotionally irresistible to write.

Remedy. Establish the licence early or not at all. This is a formal-contract problem (Chapter 9 §4.6): a novel in which the dead speak must be a novel in which the dead speak from the beginning, or the first instance will read as a cheat.

The exception. Three, and they exhaust the ways of doing it.

  • As I Lay Dying (1930). Addie Bundren’s single section is placed roughly two-thirds through, long after her death, and is the pivot of the novel. Faulkner earns it by having established from the first pages a formal convention — named sections, no narratorial frame — that makes no claim about who may hold one, and by Vardaman’s refrain that his mother is a fish, which has already put the ontology of the corpse in question. The section is a bounded grant (§6.3, no. 6) to the one consciousness the book has been circling, and it retroactively reorganises everything.
  • The Posthumous Memoirs of Brás Cubas (1881). Machado de Assis declares the device in the dedication and the first chapter: the narrator is a dead man writing. Nothing is breached because the rule was stated first.
  • Pedro Páramo (1955). Juan Rulfo makes the reader’s discovery that the speakers are dead into the novel’s central experience. The ontological breach is not a licence taken but the plot itself.

The discrimination: the corpse’s-eye view fails when it is an exemption and succeeds when it is a premise.

8.9 The Ensemble with a Secret Favourite

Symptom. A book presented as an ensemble in which one vantage is manifestly the author’s, and the others are visibly waiting. Readers describe skimming to get back.

Mechanism. A mismatch between declared and actual architecture. The book’s signals (named chapters, regular rotation) promise architecture 4; its page share, its density of interiority and its emotional investment deliver architecture 5. The reader has been asked to distribute attention that the book has already concentrated.

Persistence. Writers rarely know which vantage is their favourite. The audit at §5.5 reveals it; introspection does not, and the “relief at return” symptom (§5.4) is felt by readers long before it is admitted by authors.

Remedy. Not to suppress the favourite. Either declare the hierarchy — convert to hub-and-spokes, and let the proportions be visible — or raise the others by giving them what the favourite has, which is a want the reader can feel and a knowledge boundary that generates trouble.

The exception. As I Lay Dying again, from the other side. Darl holds nineteen of fifty-nine sections; several Bundrens hold one. The book is unambiguously a hub-and-spokes wearing an ensemble’s clothing, and the imbalance is its argument: Darl is the family member who sees, and the novel’s action is the family’s collective decision to have the seer committed. The favourite is secret to the characters and not to the form, which is the condition under which the pattern becomes a technique.

Wide Sargasso Sea is the second and more troubling instance. The husband holds the largest share of a novel written to give Antoinette the vantage Jane Eyre denied her. Critics disagree about whether this is a flaw, a concession to the source text, or the book’s sharpest move — the demonstration that even a novel written to restore a voice must contend with the volume of the one that displaced it. It is set as a seminar question (§12.2).

8.10 The Antagonist Chapter That Spoils Its Own Suspense

Symptom. A book built on a mystery grants viewpoint to the person who holds its answer, and the reader is thereby told what the book was going to be about.

Mechanism. The irony trade of §4.2, made without noticing. Sternberg’s curiosity is converted into suspense, and the exchange rate is unfavourable when the book’s other machinery was built for curiosity: an investigation plot whose reader already knows the answer must find something else for its investigators to do, and frequently has not.

Persistence. Antagonists are enjoyable to write and their chapters are dramatic in isolation. The cost is structural and therefore invisible at the scene level, which is where drafting attention lives.

Remedy. Decide which engine the book runs on — curiosity or suspense — and allocate accordingly. If the antagonist’s vantage is retained, the book must supply a replacement question, and the replacement must be at least as strong: how will they be caught, what will it cost, what will the protagonist become.

The exception. Crime and Punishment (1866). Dostoevsky discloses the murderer in the opening chapters and focalises the entire novel on him. The curiosity is spent immediately and completely, and what replaces it is larger than what was lost: not who but what is happening to this man, a question no whodunit can pose. The form has a name — the inverted detective story — and a hundred and fifty years of descendants.

Anna Karenina offers a subtler version. Tolstoy grants Karenin, the character a lesser novel would keep outside as an obstacle, enough interiority that he cannot function as an antagonist at all. The “spoilage” here is not of a plot secret but of a moral convenience, and the book is made by the loss.

8.11 The Viewpoint Granted Only to Be Killed

Symptom. A character receives viewpoint for one section and dies in it, or shortly after. The grant is a mechanism for extracting emotion from a death or for showing the reader a threat.

Mechanism. Two failures, usually together. The first is transactional: the reader detects that the interiority was issued in order to be cancelled, and responds to the manipulation rather than the death. The second is structural: the grant creates an expectation of continuation (Rabinowitz’s rules of notice again), and the expectation’s abrupt termination reads as a trick rather than as a loss.

Persistence. It works in the moment. A death from inside is more affecting than a death reported, and the effect is available cheaply and repeatedly, which is why series fiction leans on it hardest.

Remedy. Ask what the section would be worth if the character survived. If the answer is “nothing”, the grant is an effect rather than a vantage, and the material should be delivered from a survivor’s position — where it will also carry consequence, which the dead cannot.

The exception. Two, and they define the success condition.

Dracula (1897): the log of the Demeter’s captain. A man the reader has never met, in a document nobody survives to explain, recording the disappearance of his crew one by one and ending with his decision to lash himself to the wheel. It is a viewpoint granted solely to be extinguished, and it is the most frightening passage in the novel. It works because the vantage is not there to make the reader mourn; it is there to establish what the antagonist is, and a witness who survives could not establish it. The grant serves access and epistemology, not sympathy.

War and Peace (1869): Petya Rostov. Tolstoy takes the reader inside the boy on the night before his death — the half-dream in which the sounds of the camp become an orchestra he is conducting — and kills him the next morning in a single sentence, reported from outside, without emphasis. The manoeuvre is the prohibited one, and it is devastating because Tolstoy declines the transaction: he does not focalise the death. The reader is given the interior and then denied the moment for which the interior was supposedly being prepared.

The discrimination. A vantage granted to be killed fails when the death is the payoff, and succeeds when the death is not narrated from inside — or when the vantage exists to establish something the survivors could not.

8.12 The Eye Without a Mind

Symptom. A viewpoint character who observes everything and wants nothing. Their sections are full of other people; they have no project, no error in progress, and no stake. Readers describe them as “a bit of a blank”.

Mechanism. A grant of position without a grant of interiority. The book has placed a camera where a person should be. Because the reader has been given access, they expect a mind, and the expectation goes unmet in a way that is hard to articulate and easy to feel. Palmer’s continuing-consciousness frame (§3.4) is the theoretical form: readers maintain a running model of a whole mind, and a focaliser who supplies only perceptions leaves the model unfurnished.

Persistence. It is the natural shape of a witness-narrator, and witness-narrators are useful: they let a book be about a character more interesting than the person telling it. It is also the natural shape of an author-surrogate, who is by definition the person in the room with the fewest problems.

Remedy. Give the observer a want that conflicts with observing. A witness who needs something from the subject — money, forgiveness, permission, a story — is a witness with a mind, and their reporting becomes evidence about them.

The exception. The Great Gatsby (1925), which is the pattern’s most celebrated instance and its most complete answer. Nick Carraway spends much of the novel watching, and announces early that he is inclined to reserve judgement; the book’s continuing critical argument is about whether he is a moral centre or an evasion. What saves the vantage is that Fitzgerald supplies the conflicting want prescribed above: Nick is snobbish, susceptible, dishonest about his own attraction to money, and involved with Jordan Baker in a way that indicts him. The novel’s last pages are a judgement, delivered by a man who claimed he would not deliver one.

Wuthering Heights’s Lockwood is a second and more comic instance: an observer so vain and so wrong that his blankness becomes the joke and the frame’s justification.
