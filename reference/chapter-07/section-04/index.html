Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

4. The Domains of Purpose

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The domain model asks a single question of any cutaway: what does this buy? It converts a vague instruction — tighten your backstory — into a set of separable questions with separable remedies, and it gives a revising writer a vocabulary for the difference between a cutaway that is doing one job adequately and one that is doing three at once.

Its ancestry in this series is set out in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene), which establishes four domains — plot, character, theme, texture — as the base taxonomy for any unit of narrative. Chapter 1 also proposes a fifth, the relational domain, which Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) adopts; Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) adds the world. This chapter audits a cutaway’s relational work under character, as Chapter 4 does, adds reader knowledge as its own fifth domain, and proposes a sixth, structure, at §4.6.

Reader knowledge earns its place because a cutaway can change the sequence in which the reader learns about events, as distinct from the sequence in which they happened. Chronological scenes can also withhold or reveal information and create dramatic irony. The cutaway’s useful option is to revisit an earlier period after the reader has formed an understanding of later consequences.

The model’s danger is mechanical application, and it takes a specific form in this territory. A writer who has learned that a good cutaway serves several domains will begin loading cutaways — adding a thematic echo here, a sensory shift there — until the passage is visibly performing rather than happening. §4.8 and §5.4 both address this; the short form of the warning is that multiplicity is a property of well-chosen material, not a specification to be met.

For each domain below: what it is, why it matters, its textual carriers, discussion, what under- and over-delivery look like, and illustrations from published fiction.

4.1 Plot — the cutaway explains what is happening now

What the reader gains: the causal history behind a present situation, in a form that changes how the present situation is read.

Carriers

Carrier What it does Characteristic form
The causal antecedent Supplies the because behind a present state the reader has been wondering about A completing analepsis (§3.3), often short
The origin Shows where a present object, relationship or conflict began Frequently a single dramatised moment; the object is the trigger (§6.6)
The plant Establishes a fact whose significance arrives only when the present catches up Deliberately underweighted at the moment of delivery
The precedent Shows that this has happened before, converting an event into a pattern Often iterative rather than singular (§6.4, register 4)
The correction Overturns a causal account the reader had accepted A repeating analepsis; requires the first account to have been given

Discussion

Plot is the domain most often served and least often served well, because it is the one a writer can identify a need for in advance. A drafting writer knows that the reader will need to understand why the shop is failing, and writes a cutaway to explain it. The result satisfies a requirement and rarely does anything else, which is the thin, single-domain case the principle of multiplicity is designed to catch (§5).

Three refinements are worth carrying into revision.

The plot function is usually the one most easily discharged in another register. Causal information is, by definition, information. It compresses. If the whole return on a cutaway is the reader now knows the shop had been losing money for years, that is a sentence, not a scene, and the compression check at §6.3 will say so in one operation.

The precedent is the underused member of the set. Establishing that a present event belongs to a pattern is one of the most efficient things a cutaway can do, because a pattern is worth more than an instance and costs little more to establish. It is also the plot function best suited to the iterative register — every year he did this, and every year it ended the same way — which delivers a history at a fraction of the price of a scene.

The plant is the highest-skill member of the set, because it must be delivered at a weight that does not announce itself. A plant that the reader recognises as a plant has become a promise (Chapter 9 §3.2 on the rules of notice), and an unredeemed promise is worse than a missing one. Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) treats the general mechanism under proposition and payoff; the cutaway version has the additional difficulty that a fact delivered in the past tense inside an already-past passage is doubly buried and easily lost altogether.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the missing antecedent. The present depends on a causal fact the reader has never been given, and the narrative behaves as though they have it. Readers experience this as a feeling that they have skipped a page.
  • Over: the explained present. Every present development is preceded by its own historical justification, so nothing in the book is ever allowed to be simply true. This produces the characteristic rhythm of over-explained fiction: action, explanation, action, explanation, with the reader never permitted to hold an unresolved impression.

Illustrations

Faulkner, Absalom, Absalom! (1936) — the origin as hypothesis. Sutpen’s boyhood at the Virginia plantation door, where he is sent round to the back by a slave in livery, is offered as the origin of the entire design: the land, the house, the dynasty, the ruthlessness. It is the purest available example of a plot-origin cutaway. What makes it more than that — and what a student should notice — is that Faulkner delivers it at several removes, as something Sutpen told General Compson, which Mr Compson told Quentin, which Quentin is now relaying to Shreve. The origin is not established; it is reported as having been offered, by a man explaining himself. The plot function is discharged and simultaneously placed in question.

Ford, The Good Soldier (1915) — the correction as method. Dowell’s account of the Ashburnhams supplies causal antecedents continuously, and revises them almost as continuously: what he presents as an established fact in one chapter is reopened three chapters later on new information, or on a new interpretation of the same information. The plot domain here is served not by any single cutaway but by the accumulating friction between successive versions.

4.2 Character — the cutaway explains who someone is now

What the reader gains: access to the history that produced a present behaviour, in a form that makes the behaviour legible without making it excusable.

Carriers

Carrier What it does Characteristic form
The formative moment Shows, rather than states, the origin of a present fear, belief or habit Dramatised; a scene with a value shift
Then and now Lets the reader measure the distance a character has travelled, or failed to Requires a present detail against which the past can be measured
The withheld justification Supplies the reasoning a character cannot or will not state in the present Often placed immediately after the unexplained present behaviour
The recolouring Reveals a past action that changes how present behaviour reads Frequently late; requires the present behaviour to be already established
The habitual Establishes what this person’s ordinary life was Iterative; cheap; badly underused

Discussion

The character domain is the one for which the flashback register is most often correct, because character is the domain in which the difference between knowing and feeling is largest. A reader told that a man was humiliated at fourteen has a fact. A reader who has watched the humiliation has an experience they will apply, unprompted, to everything the man subsequently does. This is the strongest available argument for the dramatised cutaway, and §6.3’s compression check is designed to find the cases where it holds.

But the domain is also the site of the chapter’s most persistent bad habit, which §8.9 treats at length: the assumption that a character is explained by an origin. The assumption is a hydraulic model of personality — this happened, therefore that follows — and it is both false to experience and destructive of the effect it is trying to produce, since a behaviour with a stated cause becomes a solved problem and stops being interesting.

The more useful formulation is a distinction between two things a character cutaway can do:

Explanation answers why is he like this? and closes the question.

Illumination shows the reader something about the character that makes the present behaviour legible without making it determined — a relation the reader must construct rather than receive.

The second is almost always the better goal, and the practical difference is small: it usually amounts to declining the sentence in which the connection is drawn. A cutaway that shows a father insisting on the good pen for a stock count, and then returns to a daughter holding that pen months after his death, has performed an illumination. A cutaway that adds and that was when she first understood that he had always been preparing for something has converted it into an explanation, and cost itself most of its power.

The recolouring deserves separate mention, because it is the character function with the largest effect on reader sympathy and the one with the strictest ordering requirement. To recolour a behaviour, the behaviour must already be in place. This means the cutaway must come late, and every draft instinct pushes it early, where it would arrive as an excuse in advance. Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) makes a related point about the empathy budget; the ordering rule here is its temporal form.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the unexplained refusal. A character behaves in a way the reader cannot make sense of, and the narrative supplies nothing at all. Note that this is a real failure but a rarer one than its opposite, and that a certain quantity of it is what makes characters feel like people.
  • Over: the psychological receipt. Every present behaviour is issued with its documentation. The extreme form — a single childhood scene offered as the key to an entire personality — is §8.9.

Illustrations

Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — illumination without explanation. Stevens’s recollection of his father’s decline, and of the evening on which the old man died upstairs while Stevens continued serving at a conference below, is delivered without a single sentence connecting it to Stevens’s present condition. The connection is left entirely to the reader, who is by that point equipped to make it. The novel’s characteristic move is to let Stevens narrate the material that condemns him in the register of professional satisfaction, so that the character work happens in the gap between what he says and what the reader sees. Chapter 9 §9.6 analyses the same effect at the level of the first sentence; here it is operating at the level of the retrospective structure.

Morrison, Beloved (1987) — the recolouring held back. The reader learns early that something terrible happened at 124, learns progressively who was involved, and is given the full weight of Sethe’s act only after having been inside her ordinary competence, her exhaustion, her love for Denver, and her relation to Paul D. Every earlier approach makes the eventual account land differently. Had the material been delivered at the outset, it would have functioned as a label.

4.3 Theme — the cutaway restages the argument across time

What the reader gains: evidence that the book’s central concern is not particular to one moment, delivered by a second instance under different conditions.

Carriers

Carrier What it does Characteristic form
The restaging Puts the book’s central question under different circumstances A past scene structurally parallel to a present one
The ironic parallel The past choice and the present choice rhyme, or invert Requires the reader to notice; usually cued by a repeated object or phrase
Compounding cost Shows that a pattern accumulates consequences rather than resetting Often iterative, or a series of short returns to the same period
The refuted lesson Shows a character learning something in the past that the present disproves Depends on the reader remembering the earlier scene closely

Discussion

Theme is the domain most vulnerable to being claimed rather than demonstrated. A cutaway does not acquire a thematic function because the writer intends one; it acquires one when a reader, without prompting, reads the past scene and the present scene against each other. The practical test is whether the rhyme survives the deletion of any sentence that points it out.

The strongest thematic cutaways exploit a structural property peculiar to this device: the past and the present can be made to occupy the same page. No other narrative unit can place two temporally remote instances of the same problem in immediate adjacency and let the reader perform the comparison. A subplot can rhyme with a main plot (Chapter 14, The Subplot), but at the cost of many pages of separation; a cutaway does it in a paragraph.

This is also where the repeating analepsis (§3.3) is at its most defensible. A narrative that returns three times to the same past event, at three different points in the present timeline, is not repeating itself if each return is read against a different present. The material is constant; the comparison is new each time. This is a large part of what Beloved and The Good Soldier are doing, and it is a technique available at much smaller scale to any novel with a recurring object.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the decorative past. A cutaway that is atmospherically pleasant and thematically inert — a warm childhood scene in a cold book, connected to nothing.
  • Over: the illustrated thesis. The past scene is so closely parallel to the present one that it reads as a diagram. The symptom is that the reader can predict the parallel’s terms before the scene finishes. The remedy is asymmetry: let one term of the rhyme be wrong, or let the parallel be closer than the character notices.

Illustrations

Tolstoy, The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886; trans. Louise and Aylmer Maude) — the whole retrospect as thematic argument. The novella’s long analepsis exists to demonstrate a proposition stated in a single sentence at its outset: that Ivan Ilyich’s life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible. Every episode of the retrospect — the career, the marriage, the house, the curtains — is an instance of the same claim. The structure is unusually explicit about its own thematic function, which is why it is the clearest teaching case available (§9.8).

Salih, Season of Migration to the North (1966; trans. Denys Johnson-Davies) — the restaging as structural rhyme. Mustafa Sa’eed’s account of his years in London is, among other things, a second instance of the encounter between the narrator’s Sudan and the England he has just returned from. The novel does not state the parallel; it puts two migrations side by side and allows the second to contaminate the first. The narrator’s growing inability to keep Mustafa’s story separate from his own is the thematic work being done.

4.4 Texture — the cutaway shapes the reading experience

What the reader gains: a change in the rhythm, register or sensory palette of the reading, deployed against the present timeline’s pressure.

Carriers

Carrier What it does Characteristic form
Rhythmic contrast A deliberate change of pace set against present-tense pressure Often a slower, longer-sentenced register
Sensory shift Another time and place with different weather, light, sound, smell Concrete detail, densely placed at the entrance
Deliberate delay Withholds the present’s resolution to intensify anticipation Short; double-edged (see below)
Register change A different diction — a child’s, a formal document’s, a dialect’s Cheap and strong; see the registers at §6.4
Relief A break in an unremitting present Used sparingly, and only where the present is in fact unremitting

Discussion

Texture is the domain craft pedagogy most distrusts, because it can be invoked to justify anything: this flashback is here for the rhythm is the last refuge of an unearned cutaway. The distrust is warranted but should not become a prohibition, because rhythm is real and a book that never varies its pressure exhausts its reader.

The discriminating question is whether the textural effect could be obtained more cheaply. A change of pace can be produced by a short present-tense scene of a different kind, by a paragraph of description, by a chapter break (Chapter 6, The Chapter Break), or by a sequel in the sense given in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) — all of which cost far less than a temporal relocation. A cutaway justified on textural grounds alone is nearly always the most expensive available solution to the problem it is solving. This is why texture appears in almost every strong cutaway as a secondary function and almost never as a sufficient one.

Deliberate delay requires its own warning. Cutting away at the height of present tension in order to intensify it is a real technique, and it is also the mechanism of the failure at §8.3. The difference lies entirely in the ratio: an interruption of a few lines at a peak can sharpen it, and an interruption of six pages at the same peak dissipates it, because sustained attention to other material does not preserve tension — it replaces it. Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) treats the general principle of deferral; the rule of thumb specific to the cutaway is that the length of a delay at peak tension should be measured in sentences, not pages.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the flat cutaway. The past is rendered in the same voice, pace and sensory register as the present, so the reader gains nothing from having travelled. This is common and rarely noticed, because nothing is obviously wrong; the diagnostic is to read the passage aloud alongside a page of the present and ask whether they sound different.
  • Over: the postcard. The past scene is so much more sensually vivid than the present that the present begins to look under-written by comparison — which is a real and specific danger discussed as a failure in its own right at §8.8.

Illustrations

Proust, In Search of Lost Time (1913–27) — sensation as a central effect. The madeleine passage’s return to Combray develops a sensation and the attempt to understand it; it also bears on the novel’s themes of memory and art (§9.1). Its rhythm and diction vary among Scott Moncrieff, the Kilmartin–Enright revision and Lydia Davis’s Swann’s Way. Claims about verbal texture should identify the translation used.

Morrison, Beloved (1987) — register as boundary marker. The novel’s shifts between the narrative present, the past at Sweet Home, and the unattributed interior monologues are marked as much by changes in syntax and diction as by any explicit signal. Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) treats the monologues as a viewpoint phenomenon; considered as texture, they demonstrate that a change of register can carry the whole burden of a temporal relocation with no announcement whatsoever.

4.5 Reader Knowledge — the cutaway manages what is known, and when

This is the fifth domain specific to this chapter’s unit.

What the reader gains: not information as such, but information timed — the arrangement of what they know relative to what the characters know.

Carriers

Carrier What it does Characteristic form
Dramatic irony, created Gives the reader knowledge a present character lacks The cutaway placed before the present scene it bears on
Dramatic irony, resolved Explains a present behaviour the reader has been misreading The cutaway placed after the behaviour
The corrected assumption Overturns something the reader believed about the past Requires the false belief to have been planted, not merely absent
The reframed detail Makes an already-given present detail mean something new The most economical member of the set; costs no new information
Calibrated suspense Lets the reader see the trap the character is walking into Depends on precise placement relative to the present scene

Discussion

This domain is what distinguishes the cutaway from every other unit in the series, and it deserves a plain statement of its mechanism.

A cutaway can revise what the reader believes about an earlier event; its placement can change the meaning of both that event and the present scene.

The point is Sternberg’s (§3.4) and Ohtsuka and Brewer’s (§3.6) from two directions: the sequence of disclosure produces suspense, curiosity or surprise depending on where the gaps fall, and readers reconstruct chronology from the order of the telling. A writer who has both facts in hand has a powerful instrument, and one whose effects are almost entirely a matter of placement rather than content.

The clearest way to teach this is by moving a fixed cutaway through three positions relative to a present scene:

Position Reader’s state during the present scene Effect
Before Knows what the character does not Dramatic irony; dread; the reader watches the mistake approach
During (interrupting) Learns it as the scene runs Recontextualisation in real time; the highest-risk placement
After Reinterprets a scene already read Retrospective correction; the reader is made to feel their own misreading

None of these is superior. They are three different books. But a draft that has placed the cutaway by accident — because that was where the writer thought of it — has usually chosen the second, which is the least effective of the three and the most disruptive.

The reframed detail is the cheapest and best member of the set. It supplies no new information at all: it takes a detail the reader already has and makes it mean something else. In the series’ running example, the reader has known since Chapter 10’s material that the father put the good pen in his coat pocket on the last ordinary day. The four-line cutaway at §9.9 adds no new fact about the pen; it supplies the occasion on which he once said why the pen mattered, and the already-known detail changes from a habit into a decision. A cutaway that reframes rather than informs has the highest possible return on the smallest possible extent, and looking for one is among the most productive things a writer can do in this territory.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the flat reveal. Information arrives that changes nothing about how earlier material reads. It was a fact the reader lacked; now they have it; nothing is re-coloured.
  • Over: the reversal treadmill. Successive cutaways each overturn the previous account, until the reader stops forming beliefs at all — which destroys the mechanism, since a correction requires a belief to correct. Surprise is a non-renewable resource, and a narrative that spends it repeatedly trains readers to hold everything provisionally.

Illustrations

Ford, The Good Soldier (1915) — knowledge as the novel’s whole subject. Dowell’s celebrated protestations of ignorance — his repeated I don’t know — are the instrument by which the reader is made to know more than the narrator. The reader assembles a chronology and a set of judgements that Dowell never quite assembles himself, and the assembly is the reading experience (§9.3).

Dickens, Great Expectations (1861) — the corrected assumption at book scale. Pip’s entire construction of his own history — that Miss Havisham is his benefactor, that Estella is intended for him — is a false account of the past that the novel plants, sustains, and demolishes. The demolition is delivered by a cutaway in the strictest sense: Magwitch’s account of the years since the marshes. It is worth noticing how much the reveal depends on the reader having been given a specific false belief rather than merely being kept in the dark. Chapter 16 (The Ending) treats the two endings of this novel in detail; the point here is only about the architecture of the reveal.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Structural Domain

This section is a proposal of this chapter’s own. Chapter 8, Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture, rejects it as a domain of the cutaway and keeps what it names as the threshold of a strand: past material that recurs in a declared pattern with a question of its own is a strand, judged by that chapter’s tests, and material that recurs without one fails as an explaining strand (its Full Treatment, section 3.6).

The five domains above are all content domains: they describe what the past material delivers. But some cutaways are not chosen for what they contain. They are chosen for what they do to the shape of the book.

The structural domain. A cutaway serves the structural domain when its principal function is architectural — when it establishes, maintains or completes a pattern in the book’s design, and would be required at approximately that point even if its content were somewhat different.

Three recognisable forms:

Form Description Exemplars
The braided timeline Two temporal strands alternated on a declared rhythm; each break in one is an entry into the other Kate Morton, The Forgotten Garden (2008); Delia Owens, Where the Crawdads Sing (2018)
The returning frame An opening frame situation the book departs from and comes back to, so that the retrospect is bracketed Wuthering Heights; Frankenstein; Season of Migration to the North
The recurring approach A single withheld event circled repeatedly, each approach coming nearer Beloved; Absalom, Absalom!; The Good Soldier

The argument for admitting it

First, placement in these structures is determined by the design, not by the content. In a braided novel, the reader learns after two or three alternations that a chapter of the present will be followed by a chapter of the past. From that point onward the cutaway is expected, and the deficit model’s central mechanism has been suspended: there is no unmotivated departure, because departure is the rule. A model that can only assess each cutaway against the present-tense question it interrupts has no vocabulary for this at all.

Second, the structural function has its own failure modes, and they are not the failure modes of any content domain. A braided novel whose two strands progress at incompatible rates; a frame that opens and never returns; a recurring approach that arrives at its centre too early and leaves the last quarter with nothing to withhold — these are architectural faults, diagnosable in the table of contents, and no amount of improvement to the individual cutaways will repair them.

Third, it explains a phenomenon the five-domain model gets wrong. Consider a chapter of the past strand in a well-made braided novel which, taken alone, serves the plot thinly, the character moderately and the theme not at all. The content model marks it weak. Readers do not experience it as weak, because it is doing the work of maintaining an architecture whose payoff is elsewhere. The model should be able to say that a cutaway is load-bearing without being locally interesting.

Fourth, the structural domain is where the cutaway connects to the whole book, and therefore to Part III of this textbook. Chapter 14 (The Subplot) treats the braiding of parallel strands in the present; Chapter 10 (The Prologue) treats the frame that opens; Chapter 16 (The Ending) treats the return that closes. A dual-timeline novel is a subplot structure whose second strand happens to be in the past, and admitting the structural domain is what makes that visible.

The argument against

The case against is respectable and should be stated at its strongest, because it may be right.

It is a species of texture. The pleasures of a well-made braid — the rhythm of alternation, the anticipation of the return — are pleasures of the reading experience, and texture is already the domain for those. Alternation is a rhythmic effect at book scale rather than at paragraph scale, and scale is not usually thought to change a category.

Or it is a species of reader knowledge. A recurring approach to a withheld event is an elaborate mechanism for controlling the timing of disclosure, which is what the fifth domain describes. What looks like architecture may be the fifth domain viewed from far enough away.

And admitting it weakens the model. The five domains are all answers to what does this passage deliver to the reader here? The structural domain answers a different question — what does this passage do for the book? — and mixing the two produces a taxonomy whose members are not comparable. A domain that can be satisfied by a passage that delivers nothing locally is, on this view, not a domain at all but a separate axis of assessment.

The chapter’s position

This chapter adopts the structural domain for diagnostic purposes, on the pragmatic ground that it names something writers get wrong in a distinctive way and that no other category catches. But it adopts it with two qualifications.

  1. A structural justification is not available to a cutaway in a book without a declared structure. If the pattern has not been established, the individual cutaway cannot claim to be maintaining it. In practice this means the structural domain is unavailable before the pattern’s second or third instance, which is why the braided novel must declare its architecture early — the point Chapter 9 §4.6 makes about the formal contract, in its temporal form.
  2. Structural justification is the most frequently abused of the six, because it is the easiest to assert and the hardest to check. It’s a dual-timeline novel, so this chapter belongs here is a claim about the table of contents, not about the chapter.

Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class: is the structural domain a sixth category, or the fourth and fifth categories observed at book scale?

4.8 Must every domain be served at once?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications, each of which has a practical consequence.

  1. Two is the working target; three is strong; five is rare and often a sign of strain. The purpose of the multiplicity principle (§5) is not maximisation. It is to catch the single-domain cutaway that should have been a sentence.
  2. The domains are not equally weighted, and the weighting is book-specific. A crime novel’s cutaways will serve plot and reader knowledge heavily; a novel of consciousness will serve character and texture. A cutaway that serves the two domains its book actually runs on is doing more than one that serves four at random.
  3. Some domains are served by sequence rather than by content, and cannot be assessed on the passage alone. The reader-knowledge domain in particular is a property of placement: the identical paragraph moved forty pages serves a different function or none.
  4. A cutaway may serve a domain by omission. Declining to dramatise an event the reader expects to see is itself a reader-knowledge move, and one of the strongest in the repertoire — the unreachable past at §6.4, register 12. The domain model, which asks what a passage delivers, has some difficulty describing a passage that pointedly does not deliver, and a student should notice the limitation rather than working around it.
