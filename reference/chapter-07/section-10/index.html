Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

10. The Cutaway Across Forms

Continue in Core

The apparatus above was developed for the novel. It transfers to adjacent forms with adjustments that need stating, because writers frequently import novel-scale habits into forms that punish them.

10.1 The short story

The story has proportionally less room and a much less forgiving arithmetic. Two consequences follow directly from §3.9.

The deficit does not amortise. In a novel, an expensive cutaway can be paid off over the following fifty pages. A story has no following fifty pages, which means the return must arrive almost immediately — usually within the same paragraph as the cutaway itself.

The dramatised flashback is often unaffordable. A story of four thousand words that spends eight hundred on a past scene has given a fifth of itself to material that is not the present situation. The registers that suit the form are 1, 2, 4 and 13 — the clause of implication, the participial aside, the iterative, and the corrected assumption — because all four deliver a past without stopping the story.

Difference from the novel Consequence
No room to amortise a deficit The return must be immediate
A single situation, often a single scene Cutaways are usually internal analepses within a short span
The reader holds the whole text in memory A repeating analepsis is more visible, and therefore more expensive
The ending recolours everything The corrected assumption (register 13) is disproportionately powerful

The last row is one possible advantage of short form: a final revelation about the past can recolour much of what preceded it while the text is still readily recallable. Novels can also produce large retrospective revisions. This is one reason to test whether a story’s past is strongest at the end rather than in the middle.

10.2 The novella

The novella has a novel’s tonal amplitude and a story’s word count, and its characteristic problem in this territory is proportion. A single dramatised flashback in a thirty-thousand-word text is a structural event, not a passage.

The form’s distinctive solution is the one Tolstoy uses (§9.8): make the retrospect the whole design rather than an interruption of it. A novella can afford to be almost entirely analepsis in a way a novel usually cannot, because the reader’s investment in the first narrative has not had time to become the thing they are protecting. Where a novel that leaves its present for two hundred pages has abandoned something, a novella that leaves its present after twenty has simply declared its shape.

10.3 Series and sequels

Series fiction faces a version of the problem that no other form has: the past is a previous book, and the reader may or may not have read it.

Three principles, all of which are versions of the rules already stated.

  1. Recap is backstory, and the compression check applies to it. A returning reader needs a clause; a new reader needs a paragraph; neither needs a scene. A dramatised flashback to the previous volume’s climax is the form’s characteristic error, and it is worse than it looks: it is redundant for one audience and unearned for the other.
  2. Deliver the past as present-tense consequence rather than as summary — a character behaving in a way the previous volume explains and this one demonstrates. Chapter 9 §10.4 states the same rule for series openings.
  3. The iterative is the series writer’s best instrument. They had been doing this for two years now, and it had stopped being remarkable delivers a whole prior volume’s worth of relationship in a sentence, and characterises while doing it.

10.4 The screen comparison, and where it misleads

Film and prose both tell stories out of chronological order, but a direct derivation of the prose cutaway from film has not been established (§3.8). The comparison helps identify what each medium can signal readily.

What film can signal visually.

  • Entry cues. A dissolve, colour change or change of aspect ratio can mark a move into the past quickly. Such cues are optional, and viewers may still need to infer when and where a scene occurs.
  • Visible context. A frame can show clothing, setting and apparent age together. It does not automatically specify a date, social position or the relation of the new scene to the previous one; prose can also establish several facts in a phrase.
  • Return cues. A cut back to a familiar face can re-anchor a viewer, but the return’s meaning still has to be earned by the film, just as it does in prose.

What prose can signal grammatically.

  • Grammatical tense. English can place an event earlier with an auxiliary verb inside an ongoing sentence. Film usually uses editing, dialogue, voice-over or other cues to convey the same relation.
  • The iterative. Prose can write every Sunday in two words. Film can convey repetition through montage, recurring scenes, narration or a calendar cue.
  • Uncertainty about the past. Prose can say perhaps or it must have been. Film can also present incompatible or unreliable accounts: Kurosawa’s Rashomon (1950) dramatises conflicting versions of one event in flashback.

The practical warning. A writer accustomed to dramatised screen flashbacks may reach first for register 5. The shorter prose registers are also available, and comparing them in revision can reveal a more fitting way to tell the past.

10.5 Translated fiction

Two observations, both of which follow the series’ standing distinction between structural and textural claims.

Broad structural claims often survive translation. The order of large narrative sections and the placement of major cutaways are usually retained, but temporal cues, sentence boundaries and local emphasis can change. Claims about reach, extent and type should be checked against the particular edition used, especially where they depend on a short passage.

The grammatical marking of the past can change in translation. This is a sharper problem here than in most chapters, because English often marks a cutaway by tense, and tense systems differ.

  • English can use the past perfect to signal a step behind the narrative past. One prose convention is to open a retrospect with past-perfect forms and then use simple past once its temporal position is clear. Other languages also have ways to mark anteriority; their conventions need checking case by case.
  • Languages with grammatical aspect can distribute the work differently; Russian, for example, marks habituality and completion through aspect as well as tense, which an English translator may render with lexical cues such as would or used to.
  • Languages without an obligatory pluperfect may signal the shift by adverbials, by particles, or by nothing at all, leaving the reader to infer the temporal relation from context.

The consequence for a student: a claim about how conspicuous a cutaway feels is a claim about the language it is read in. The observation at §8.10, that italics substitute for construction, is a claim about a typographic convention in anglophone publishing. The observation at §5.3, that tense is the cheapest carrier available, is a claim about English.
