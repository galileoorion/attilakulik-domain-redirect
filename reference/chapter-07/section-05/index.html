Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

5. Multiplicity: The Economy of the Cutaway

Continue in Core

5.1 The principle, stated as a constraint

Multiplicity — the practice of making a single unit of text discharge several narrative functions at once — is a general virtue in this series and a specific arithmetical requirement here.

The reasoning is a constraint problem, and it differs from the version stated in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages). There, the constraint is space: the opening has the fewest words and the most simultaneous obligations. Here the constraint is debt:

  • the cutaway begins by spending attention rather than by soliciting it (§3.9);
  • the spend is incurred before the material delivers anything;
  • and the reader can, at any point during the cutaway, resolve their discomfort by skipping to the return — an option not available in a present-tense scene, because there is nothing to skip to.

That last point deserves emphasis, since it is rarely stated and is the practical mechanism behind most complaints about flashbacks. A reader in a cutaway knows where the exit is. They can see, typographically or grammatically, that this passage will end and the story will resume. A reader in a present-tense scene has no comparable escape, because the scene is the story. This makes the cutaway uniquely vulnerable to skimming, and it is why the cost of a thin cutaway is not merely a slow page but a page the reader learns they can skip — after which they will skip the next one too.

The discipline is not compression; it is selection. A cutaway that serves one domain has not failed by being short. It has failed by being the wrong material. The remedy is rarely to add functions to the passage you have; it is to find the past moment that already carries several, because it is the right moment.

The single-domain test is the most useful first move in revision, because it finds the cutaways that should have been sentences. In its operational form:

Name the domains this cutaway serves. If the honest answer is one, ask whether the material would do the same work as backstory — a sentence rather than a scene — thereby freeing the flashback budget for a past moment that needs the full dramatised treatment.

The phrase flashback budget is not decorative. Most novels can support a small number of fully dramatised past scenes before the reader begins to feel that the present timeline is not carrying the book. Spending one of them on material that a clause would have delivered is not a local inefficiency; it is a structural misallocation.

5.2 Anatomy of a multiplying sentence

The clearest available demonstration is a single sentence that opens a forty-page analepsis and does the work of an entire chapter of preparation. Tolstoy, at the head of Chapter II of The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886), in the Louise and Aylmer Maude translation:

Ivan Ilych’s life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible.

The novella’s first chapter has shown the funeral, the colleagues calculating their promotions, the widow enquiring about her pension, and Peter Ivanovich escaping to a game of cards. The reader now stands at the entrance to a retrospect covering forty-five years. This sentence is the whole of the preparation.

Textual element Functions discharged
Ivan Ilych’s life Reader knowledge (the retrospect’s scope is declared: not an episode, a life); Structure (the first narrative is being left, and the extent announced)
had been Texture, and the formal signal — the pluperfect is the grammatical turn on which the whole cutaway depends; Structure (this is analepsis, and the reader is told so in two words)
most simple and most ordinary Character (the man in five words); Plot (nothing exceptional will happen; the reader is warned off looking for event)
and therefore Theme — the entire argument of the novella is carried by a conjunction. Not but; not and yet. The ordinariness is the cause of the horror
most terrible Reader knowledge (a verdict delivered before its evidence, so that the reader reads the forty-five years as a prosecution); Texture (the tonal register for everything that follows)

Fourteen words; all six domains touched; nothing spent on transition, orientation or apology. Note in particular what the sentence does not do. It offers no trigger — nobody in the present is remembering. It offers no bridging language. It does not explain that we are going back. It relies entirely on the pluperfect and on the reader’s willingness to be told.

And note the effect of the verdict’s position. By placing most terrible before the evidence, Tolstoy converts the whole analepsis from a chronicle into an argument the reader is invited to test. This is the reader-knowledge domain at its most economical: the same forty-five years, delivered without that clause, would be a biography.

What resists teaching here is that the sentence does not feel efficient. It feels blunt, almost crude — three superlatives in a row, joined by and. Successful multiplicity in this territory has the same signature it has in openings: the reader experiences plainness while the text performs density.

5.3 Where the multiplying work actually lives

Novice revision looks for multiplicity in the content of the past scene — a more dramatic memory, a more revealing incident. In practice the cutaway’s multiplying work is done in four humbler places, none of them a matter of what the past scene contains.

1. The choice of the trigger object. The object that motivates a cutaway is doing double duty by construction: it exists in the present scene and in the past one. Choose it well and it carries plot (it is evidence), character (whose object it was, and how they used it), theme (what it means to keep things), and reader knowledge (it was already on the page, and now means something else). Choose it badly — a photograph, a smell of baking — and it does nothing but open a door. The most productive question in revising a cutaway, because it finds triggers already doing other work, is: which present-tense objects could motivate this, and which of them is already load-bearing elsewhere in the book?

2. Tense and aspect. The pluperfect, the iterative would, the simple past used against a present-tense frame, the conditional of a remembered promise: English marks temporal relation grammatically, and grammar is the cheapest possible carrier. Chapter 9 §5.3 makes the same observation about openings, and it is even truer here, because a change of tense can perform a whole transition that would otherwise cost a paragraph. He had always kept it by the till does in eight words what a scene would take three hundred to establish, and it also characterises, because always is a claim someone is making.

3. What the return sentence does with the present. The most consistently underused position in the whole structure is the first sentence after the cutaway ends. It is the only sentence in the passage that can operate on both timelines at once, and in most drafts it is spent on re-anchoring — She blinked. The shop was still there. — which Chapter 5 (The Transition) shows can usually be done in four words or none. A return sentence that instead performs the change the cutaway bought is worth more than any three sentences inside the past scene.

4. The sentence that is not written. A cutaway that ends without stating its own significance leaves the reader to construct the connection, and a constructed connection is held more firmly than a supplied one. The deletion of the explanatory sentence is the commonest single improvement available to a draft cutaway, and it costs nothing.

Note what is absent from this list: better past material. That is not because past material does not matter but because it is the thing writers already attend to. The four items above are the ones that go unrevised.

5.4 The ceiling, and the limit case

Multiplicity has an upper bound, and exceeding it produces a recognisable defect. Three symptoms:

Symptom What the reader experiences
The significant memory Every remembered detail is meaningful; nothing in the past was ever merely true
The over-motivated trigger The object that launches the cutaway is so neatly apt that the machinery shows
The tidy return The present scene resumes having been changed in just the way the cutaway was designed to change it, no more and no less

The third is the most damaging and the least often diagnosed, because it is a consequence of following good advice. A writer told that the present must be changed on return will change it, visibly and to the letter. The result is a cutaway that reads as a mechanism: insert past, receive alteration. What is missing is surplus — the sense that the past scene contained more than the present needed, which is the sense that produces the impression of a real history rather than a supply of relevant background.

The remedy is the same as Chapter 9’s remedy for the overstuffed page, and equally counter-intuitive: a novel needs some past material that is not doing anything. A detail in a flashback that is merely true, unconnected to any present function, is what makes the rest of the flashback credible. Barthes’s argument in “The Reality Effect” (1968) applies directly: the apparently useless detail signifies reality itself, and its uselessness is the mechanism.

The limit case runs in the other direction, and it is instructive. Proust’s Combray does not multiply in the sense described above at all. The extended treatment of the hawthorns, the church steeple, the two ways, the goodnight kiss, has an enormous ratio of extent to plot function; almost none of it is doing the work this chapter’s taxonomy would recognise. It succeeds because the book’s proposition is that this is what a past is — not a set of causes for a present, but a texture recoverable only in fragments and only involuntarily. Where the accumulation itself is the argument, the efficiency criterion has no purchase. The same is true, differently, of Melville’s cetology chapters and of Hugo’s digressions: a book may decide that its subject requires excess, and the domain model can only note that it has done so.

5.5 Exercise: the cutaway audit

A procedure, and one of the more reliable teaching instruments in this chapter.

  1. List every cutaway in the manuscript, in order of appearance, on a single sheet. For each, record five things: page, reach (how far back), extent (how much time it covers), register (which of the fourteen at §6.4), and domains served.
  2. In a sixth column, record the present-tense question that was open at the moment the cutaway began. If you cannot name one, write none.
  3. Do not assess any individual cutaway. Read the sheet.

Interpretation:

Pattern in the sheet Diagnosis
Most entries show one domain The manuscript is using cutaways to deliver information. Most should be compressed to backstory (§6.3), freeing budget for the two or three that need dramatising
Most entries show the same register The book has one gear for the past. The substitution exercise at §6.5 is the remedy
The reach column is uniform All the past material lives in one period. Ask what the intervening years contained, and why the book has decided they were empty
Many entries show “none” in column six Cutaways are arriving on the writer’s schedule rather than the reader’s. Test 1 (§7.1)
Cutaways cluster in the first quarter Front-loaded exposition. Sternberg’s asset is being spent early (§3.4)
Cutaways disappear after the midpoint Often correct, and worth confirming as a choice; a book whose past stops mattering halfway should ask whether it ever did
Total past extent exceeds present extent Not a fault, but a declaration. The book is a retrospective novel, and should be structured as one (§4.6) rather than as a present-tense novel with a memory problem

The sixth row is worth dwelling on. A manuscript in which cutaways stop at the midpoint has usually finished its expositional work and moved into consequence, which is the healthy shape. A manuscript in which they increase towards the end is either doing something ambitious — the recurring approach converging on a withheld centre, as in Beloved — or is delaying a revelation past the point at which the reader has stopped caring. The audit will not tell you which. It will tell you that you have made a large structural choice, which is more than most drafts know about themselves.
