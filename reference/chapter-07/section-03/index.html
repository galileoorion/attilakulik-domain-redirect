Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

3. Why the Past Is Expensive

Continue in Core

Section 1 asserts that a cutaway begins in debt. The assertion is correct, but it is a metaphor, and a metaphor is not an argument. This section assembles the argument from six directions — formal, rhetorical, philosophical, cognitive, psychoanalytic and historical — because a writer who understands why a backward movement costs revises it more intelligently than one who has merely been told that it does. The section ends by restating the deficit principle and then qualifying it, since the qualification is where the more interesting teaching lies.

3.1 Genette: the first narrative, and the measurement of anachrony

The indispensable apparatus is Gérard Genette’s, set out in Narrative Discourse (1972; English translation by Jane E. Lewin, 1980) — the work of narratology this chapter draws on most, and the one whose vocabulary craft teaching reproduces in a rough and unacknowledged form.

Genette’s opening move is to separate the order in which events occur in the story from the order in which the narrative presents them. Any discordance between the two he calls anachrony. Anachrony is not an occasional ornament; it is, as Genette observes, so nearly universal in Western narrative that a strictly chronological telling is the marked case rather than the default. The Iliad opens in the tenth year of the war. The Odyssey opens with Telemachus, reaches Odysseus in Book V, and has him narrate Books IX to XII himself, in the past, at a feast.

The decisive concept for a writer, and the one most often skipped in craft summaries, is the first narrative (récit premier). Anachrony is not measured against absolute chronology, or against the reader’s clock. It is measured against the temporal level the narrative has established as its principal one — the level to which the telling returns and against which everything else is felt as a departure. Without a first narrative there is no anachrony, only a sequence.

Three consequences follow immediately, and all three are practical.

  1. A narrative must have a stable first level before a cutaway can be felt as a cutaway. This is the mechanism underlying the failure pattern at §8.6: a flashback in chapter two arrives before the reader has consolidated a first narrative to depart from, so the departure is not registered as a departure. It reads instead as the book still deciding what it is.
  2. What counts as a departure depends on where the narrative has said its home is. In The Remains of the Day the home is a six-day motoring holiday in July 1956; in Great Expectations the home is Pip’s life from childhood forward. Identical retrospective material occupies opposite structural positions in the two books.
  3. A narrative can change its first level, and this is a large structural decision rather than a local one. Wuthering Heights effectively transfers its first narrative from Lockwood’s residence at Thrushcross Grange to Nelly’s chronicle, and then transfers it back for the ending (§9.2). The transfer is what makes the novel’s shape unusual, and it is invisible to any analysis that treats the whole book as a single flat sequence.

Genette’s two species of anachrony are analepsis — the evocation, after the fact, of an event earlier than the point in the story at which we currently stand — and prolepsis — narration in advance of an event that will come later. The term flashback nowhere appears in Genette’s system, and its absence is not fastidiousness. Analepsis names a relation between two positions in a narrative; flashback names a device with a particular twentieth-century shape (§3.8). The wider term catches things the narrower one misses: the single clause in the pluperfect, the parenthetical aside, the reference in dialogue to something that happened before the book began.

3.2 Reach and extent

Genette’s two measurements of an anachrony are the most immediately usable part of his apparatus, and they are the part craft pedagogy most conspicuously lacks.

Reach (portée) is how far the anachrony goes from the present moment of the first narrative — the size of the leap.

Extent (amplitude) is how much story time the anachrony itself covers — the size of the visited territory.

The two are independent, and every combination has a different profile.

Short extent Long extent
Short reach The recent single moment: yesterday’s argument, dramatised. Cheap; often the best available option, and underused Recent history summarised: the last six months of a marriage. Cheap but flattening; the natural home of summary
Long reach The distant single moment: one afternoon, thirty years ago. The most expensive unit in the repertoire, and the one requiring the strongest justification The distant era: a childhood, a war, a family’s founding. Structurally a second book; usually requires the architecture of §4.6

Two observations that a working writer can use at once.

The distant single moment is the costly case, and it is the one drafts reach for by reflex. A long reach requires the reader to build a whole context — different ages, a vanished place, a superseded set of relations — and a short extent gives them only one scene’s worth of material to build it from. The context is expensive and immediately discarded. This is the arithmetic behind the widespread and otherwise mysterious workshop suspicion of the childhood flashback (§8.9). The suspicion is not about childhood; it is about the ratio of construction cost to occupancy.

Reach can change during an anachrony, and the direction it changes in is a structural signal. An analepsis with a long reach and a long extent shortens its own reach as it proceeds, since it moves forward while the first narrative waits. Nelly Dean’s narrative in Wuthering Heights begins about thirty years before Lockwood’s present and closes the distance until it merges with it. A reader feels this as approach, and the feeling is one of the most reliable sources of momentum available inside a long cutaway — the past visibly coming to meet the present. Its absence is one reason a static, non-progressing flashback drags: nothing is closing.

3.3 The three distinctions that determine cost

Genette subdivides analepsis further, and three of the subdivisions do real diagnostic work.

First: external, internal and mixed.

Type Definition What it can do
External analepsis Lies entirely before the starting point of the first narrative Supplies antecedents. May still revise earlier reports or assumptions about this period
Internal analepsis Falls inside the span of the first narrative Fills or revises material the narrative has already passed through. Risks redundancy, and can produce interference with what was already told
Mixed analepsis Begins before the first narrative and continues into it The characteristic shape of the long embedded chronicle; must manage the overlap

The distinction is not academic. An external analepsis covers time before the main action, but it can still challenge a prior account of that time. An internal analepsis works in time the main action has already crossed and may repeat or revise what the reader was shown. A writer should know which kind they are writing and what the reader already believes about the period.

Second, and within the internal case: completing versus repeating.

A completing analepsis fills a gap — an ellipsis — that the narrative previously left open. It supplies something the reader knew was missing.

A repeating analepsis returns to material the narrative has already covered, in order to recolour it.

This distinction is the sharpest instrument in the chapter, and craft teaching rarely has a name for it.

  • A completing analepsis is met by a reader who already has a slot for it. The question what happened in the eleven years between them? has been open; the material arrives as an answer. The deficit is small, because the reader was already carrying the question. In Sternberg’s terms (§3.4), the narrative has been generating curiosity and is now discharging it.
  • A repeating analepsis has no slot waiting. The reader has been told this already, and is being asked to accept a second telling — which is only worth their time if the second telling differs: a new vantage, a new fact, a new emotional weight, a revealed lie. The deficit here is large, and the payment must be visible.

The practical form of the distinction is test 9 in §7.1. Its diagnostic value is high because it converts a vague complaint — this flashback feels redundant — into a determinate question with a determinate remedy. If the cutaway is completing, the remedy for a flat one is usually placement: bring it closer to the moment the reader most feels the gap. If it is repeating, the remedy is difference: identify what the second pass adds, and if nothing does, cut it.

It also names the achievement of the two most ambitious novels in this chapter’s allocation. The Good Soldier and Beloved are both built almost entirely of repeating analepses — the same events returned to again and again — and both survive the enormous deficit that implies, because each return supplies a visibly different colouring. Ford’s Dowell revisits the Ashburnhams’ history a dozen times and each revisit corrects the last; Morrison’s narrative approaches the events at 124 repeatedly, from different consciousnesses, at different distances, and never twice in the same terms. Repetition is the most expensive structure in this chapter and the one that produces its greatest novels — which should be enough to warn any student against reading the cost model as a prohibition.

Third: prolepsis, and its asymmetry with analepsis.

Prolepsis is formally the mirror of analepsis. It can create a question about how a foreseen event will occur, just as an analepsis can answer a question about the past. Neither direction has a guaranteed advantage in attention or comprehension. García Márquez’s opening sentence in One Hundred Years of Solitude (trans. Gregory Rabassa) invokes a later firing squad and generates a question about how the narrative will reach it.

An advance reference to a later event can open a question that a subsequent passage answers. Tolstoy achieves a related effect by a different structure in The Death of Ivan Ilyich: Chapter I begins after Ivan’s death, and Chapter II turns back to his life. Relative to the funeral frame, that long retrospect is an analepsis, not a prolepsis (§9.8).

3.4 Sternberg: distribution, and curiosity as an engine

Meir Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978) is the second pillar. Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) uses Sternberg at its own §6.2 to argue that exposition is a distribution problem rather than a location; this chapter takes the argument further into the territory Sternberg himself was most interested in.

Sternberg’s three modes — preliminary (antecedents delivered up front), delayed (action first, antecedents later), and distributed (antecedents dispersed throughout, never gathered) — describe where a book’s expositional mass sits. The cutaway is the primary instrument of the second and third.

The concept that matters most here is Sternberg’s distinction between the interests a narrative can generate:

Interest Uncertainty about Generated by Discharged by
Suspense The future An open situation moving towards outcome The outcome arriving
Curiosity The past A visible gap in what the reader has been told Exposition supplied
Surprise The past, retroactively A gap the reader did not know existed The gap opened and filled at once

The consequence for this chapter is fundamental and reframes the whole deficit model:

A well-placed cutaway does not merely spend interest. It discharges an interest of a different kind — one the narrative deliberately created earlier by withholding.

This is why the deficit metaphor, taken alone, is too pessimistic. It treats the past as a pure cost against a present-tense credit. Sternberg’s account shows that a novel can manufacture the appetite its cutaway will satisfy, so that the cutaway arrives as payment rather than interruption. The reader who has been wondering since the drawer, half a book earlier, why a looser hand takes over the ledger on 9 March is not being interrupted when Dov’s chapter finally goes back to that evening; they are being paid.

Three operational rules follow, and they are among the most useful in the chapter.

  1. Open the gap before you fill it. The cheapest cutaway in any novel is the one the reader has already asked for. A single earlier sentence establishing that something happened, without saying what, converts a later analepsis from an imposition into a discharge.
  2. Backstory withheld is an asset; backstory delivered is an expense. Chapter 9 states this rule for openings. Applied to the whole book, it becomes a scheduling principle: the question is not how much past a novel contains but when it spends it.
  3. Curiosity and suspense can be run simultaneously, and the strongest cutaways do. A flashback that answers a standing question about the past while raising a new question about the present is generating both interests at once. The worked example in §9.9 is of this kind: a four-line cutaway that answers why does she care about this pen and opens what did he think anyone would need to check.

3.5 Ricoeur and Mendilow: configured time and the novel’s discovery of it

Genette describes the mechanism and Sternberg the economy. Paul Ricoeur’s Time and Narrative (three volumes, 1983–85; English translation by Kathleen McLaughlin and David Pellauer) supplies the reason the mechanism exists at all.

Ricoeur’s argument, in the form relevant here, is that narrative is the mode in which human time becomes intelligible. Mere succession — one thing after another — is not yet a story; emplotment is the operation that draws a configuration out of a succession, converting a sequence of events into a whole that can be grasped as a unit. Ricoeur takes from Augustine the observation that the present is where the past and the future are held: the mind stretches itself, in Augustine’s phrase, across memory and expectation, and this stretching is what makes duration experienceable at all.

For a writer, this yields a claim that is more than a metaphor:

A narrative can configure time while presenting events in chronological order. Backward movement is one further way to make an arrangement of events meaningful; chronology alone does not determine whether a sequence has been emploted.

That is a deliberately strong statement, and it stands in productive tension with the deficit model. The deficit model treats the present as the story and the past as a visit. Ricoeur treats the mutual determination of the two as constitutive of narrative itself. Both are true at different scales: locally, in a scene, the interruption costs; globally, in a book, the arrangement of past against present is the book’s meaning. A student who holds only the first will write efficient, forgettable fiction. A student who holds only the second will write two hundred pages in which nothing happens now.

The historical dimension is supplied by A. A. Mendilow’s Time and the Novel (1952), the earliest systematic English treatment of the subject and still a useful one. Mendilow’s central concern is the novel’s fictive present — the moving now that the reader occupies — and the ways novelists have manipulated the relation between the time of the telling, the time of the told, and the time of the reading. His historical account tracks the movement, across the eighteenth, nineteenth and twentieth centuries, from a broadly chronological convention, in which antecedents are delivered as preliminary matter or in a character’s spoken history, towards the modernist rearrangement of time as a psychological rather than a calendrical medium.

The lesson to draw from Mendilow is that conventions of narrating the past change over time. Retrospective scenes and sensory prompts predate cinema; particular combinations of marked entry, sustained past scene and marked return became prominent in modern fiction and film. Their precise literary genealogy should not be asserted without historical evidence.

3.6 The cognitive strand: what a backward shift costs a reader

The empirical literature on discourse comprehension supplies the sharpest available version of the interest deficit, and — used carefully — converts a metaphor into something closer to a measurement.

Rolf Zwaan and Gabriel Radvansky’s synthesis (Psychological Bulletin, 1998) describes readers building situation models: representations of the state of affairs a text describes. Research on narrative comprehension examines continuity in protagonist, space, time, causality and intentionality. Temporal discontinuities can slow reading or make earlier information less accessible. In the studies reviewed, longer forward shifts did not always produce proportionally greater disruption; the specific cue and situation matter.

Two further findings bear on the cutaway specifically.

Readers can reconstruct chronology distinct from the text’s order. In Keisuke Ohtsuka and William Brewer’s experiment (Discourse Processes, 1992), participants listened to five versions of an event sequence and answered questions about its temporal order. Comprehension was best for the canonical version; backward, flashback and embedded versions were harder, and the flash-forward version was hardest. The result concerns accuracy on that task, not reading speed or general recall, and does not establish that backward moves cost more than forward ones. Test 7 in §7.1 turns the finding into a practical chronology check rather than a claim of universal difficulty.

Frames may be switched and recalled. Catherine Emmott’s Narrative Comprehension (1997) describes a reader’s contextual frame — the running record of who is present, where, and in what configuration. A flashback may require access to an earlier frame and a later return to the interrupted situation. That makes frame management a useful craft concern, but Emmott’s account does not by itself rank the cost of a flashback against an equally large forward transition.

On the asymmetry between backward and forward shifts, a caution is required. A backward cutaway can suspend an open present question, but forward shifts also require model updating. Ohtsuka and Brewer’s flash-forward condition was hardest in their temporal-order task, so their result cannot support a general backward-greater-than-forward claim. What the literature supports is narrower:

  • Temporal discontinuities of any direction impose measurable processing costs.
  • In Ohtsuka and Brewer’s experiment, noncanonical arrangements reduced accuracy on questions about event order relative to the canonical version.
  • Comprehension involves maintaining a situation model whose updating is effortful, and whose components must be held across intervening material.

These findings give a limited cognitive basis for asking readers to track chronology. The broader interest deficit remains this chapter’s craft model, not a measured cognitive quantity. Difficulty is not a defect: the experiment does not tell us whether Absalom, Absalom! should have been written differently.

3.7 Trauma and the unavailable past

There is a class of narrative for which everything above is inverted, and it requires its own frame.

Cathy Caruth’s Unclaimed Experience: Trauma, Narrative, and History (1996) develops, from Freud and from her readings of literary and filmic texts, an account of traumatic experience as characterised by belatedness: an event so overwhelming that it is not fully registered at the moment of its occurrence, and which therefore returns — intrusively, repetitively, and with a literality that ordinary memory lacks. On this account the traumatic past is not stored as narrative and cannot be told in sequence; it possesses the present rather than preceding it.

The consequence for narrative form is direct, and it is why this strand belongs in a chapter on the cutaway rather than in a chapter on character.

For a narrative representing traumatic experience, the fragmented, repetitive, resistant approach to the past is not a failure of construction. It is the represented condition. The past does not stay behind to be visited; it arrives, uninvited, and interrupts.

This inverts the chapter’s economics completely. The deficit model assumes the writer chooses to leave the present and must justify the choice. In Beloved the past is not chosen and cannot be refused; Sethe’s rememory is explicitly a past that persists as a place in the world, which another person can walk into. The novel’s repeated, veering approaches to the events of 1855 are the form its subject takes. To ask whether Morrison has earned her cutaways is to have misidentified which is the interruption. Here the cutaway is not a detour from the story; it is the story, and the present timeline at 124 Bluestone Road is the interruption.

Absalom, Absalom! presents the same inversion under a different sign. The Sutpen material is not traumatic in Caruth’s clinical sense so much as historically unavailable: no living witness saw the decisive events, the surviving accounts conflict, and the novel’s method is the reconstruction of a past that resists being reconstructed. The retrospection is not a means of telling the story; it is the story of an attempt to tell it.

Caruth’s model is contested, and a textbook should say so. Ruth Leys’s Trauma: A Genealogy (2000) mounts a detailed critique of the literalist account of traumatic memory that Caruth’s argument relies on, tracing its intellectual genealogy and challenging both its clinical foundation and its theoretical coherence. Dominick LaCapra’s Writing History, Writing Trauma (2001) argues for a distinction between acting out and working through that Caruth’s model tends to collapse, and warns against the conflation of trauma with the sublime. Stef Craps’s Postcolonial Witnessing (2013) argues that the canonical trauma-theory paradigm is narrowly Eurocentric in its models and its examples, and inadequate to collective and continuing traumas that do not fit the single-event structure.

For this chapter, the contest matters in a specific way. What is at issue is whether the formal features — fragmentation, repetition, belatedness — reflect a psychological truth about how traumatic memory works, or a set of literary conventions for representing it. For a critic, the difference is large. For a writer, the practical upshot is the same either way: the conventions exist, readers recognise them, and a novel that adopts them takes on the obligations they bring. What a writer must not do is borrow the form without the subject — the fragmentation of trauma narrative applied to material that is merely withheld, which is the mechanism behind the failure at §8.10. Seminar question 4 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class.

3.8 The historical strand: literary retrospection and cinematic convention

The last strand is the one most often omitted and the most immediately liberating for a student, because it shows that the object under study has a date.

The term has a film history. “Flashback” was used in discussion of cinema by the silent era, although its early meanings were not fixed. Maureen Turim’s Flashbacks in Film: Memory and History (1989) studies how films came to mark transitions into and out of earlier events. This does not show that prose borrowed the retrospective scene itself from film: literary analepsis has a much longer history, and paths of influence require separate evidence.

Earlier narrative had a rich repertoire for the past. Its instruments overlap with later film and literary practice, even where framing conventions differ.

Form Description Governing rules Examples
The epic excursus A digression into antecedents, delivered by the narrating voice No perspectival hierarchy; the past is illuminated as fully as the present The scar of Odysseus, Odyssey XIX
The character’s recital A long spoken account of the past, delivered to an audience within the story Governed by the rules of speech: a listener, an occasion, a motive for telling Odysseus at Alcinous’s court; Aeneas to Dido, Aeneid II
The interpolated tale A complete separate narrative inserted into the main one Formally detachable; may have no causal relation to the frame at all “El curioso impertinente” in Don Quixote I
The found manuscript A document presented as authentic, containing the past Authenticated by an editorial apparatus; the past arrives as evidence Cervantes’s Cide Hamete; The Castle of Otranto’s preface
The oral chronicle A servant, retainer or witness narrating at length across many sittings The listener’s interruptions and the teller’s biases are part of the form Nelly Dean in Wuthering Heights
The marked screen flashback A dramatised past scene, sometimes entered from a trigger and returned from with a visual or sound cue Entry, immersion and return may be marked in several ways A common twentieth-century film convention

Erich Auerbach’s reading of the scar of Odysseus in the first chapter of Mimesis (1946; English translation by Willard Trask, 1953) is the indispensable text here, and it makes the historical point with unusual force. When Eurycleia recognises Odysseus by the scar on his thigh, Homer breaks off for some seventy lines to narrate the boar hunt in which the scar was acquired. A modern reader trained on flashback conventions expects this to be retardation — suspense generated by delay at the crisis. Auerbach argues it is nothing of the kind. Homer’s excursus does not stand in shadow behind the present; it is brought fully into the foreground, uniformly illuminated, and while it is being told there is no suspended present at all. There is no “background” in Homer, and therefore nothing is being held in reserve.

This is not a difference of skill. It is a different theory of what narrative time is for. Auerbach’s contrast with the Elohist’s Abraham — a narrative “fraught with background”, in which the unexpressed is the essential thing — sets out the two poles between which everything in this chapter operates. The modern cutaway belongs entirely to the second pole: it depends on there being a foreground that is suspended and a background that is withheld, and neither of those is a natural feature of narrative.

Two conclusions follow, and they should be stated plainly rather than implied.

  1. The prohibitions in §8 are calibrated to a particular craft model. Motivated triggers, marked returns and suspicion of long recitals appear in some modern screen and workshop practice, but the chapter cannot establish that this model has a single cinematic origin or a precise start date. These are useful tests for some contemporary novels, not universal laws of narrative.
  2. Reading historically is a practical skill, not a scholarly ornament. A writer who knows that the interpolated tale, the found document and the oral chronicle are all live options has a repertoire of fourteen registers (§6.4) rather than one. The commonest technical poverty in draft fiction in this territory is not bad flashbacks; it is the assumption that the dramatised scene-with-trigger is the only way a novel can hold its past.

3.9 The interest deficit, restated — and qualified

The chapter’s first principle can now be stated with the precision the theory allows.

The interest deficit. At the moment a cutaway begins, the reader is holding an open question about the present narrative. The cutaway suspends that question, requires a contextual frame to be held in memory while a second is constructed, imposes the processing cost of a temporal discontinuity, and obliges the reader to maintain a chronology distinct from the order of the telling. These costs are incurred before the past material has delivered anything. The deficit is discharged when the return leaves the present measurably changed — in the reader’s knowledge, in the character’s situation, or in the meaning of what was already known.

Four refinements the theory has supplied:

  1. The deficit is variable, not fixed. It scales with reach and extent (§3.2), with the pressure of the suspended present-tense question, and with whether the material is completing or repeating (§3.3).
  2. The deficit can be pre-funded. A narrative that opens a gap early converts the eventual cutaway from an expense into a discharge of curiosity (§3.4).
  3. The deficit is positional. The same material at a different point in the present timeline may cost a fraction as much. In revision this is usually the first thing to try (§7.1, test 8).
  4. The deficit is a claim about attention, not about value. Every one of the works in §9 runs deficits that no diagnostic protocol would license.

And now the qualification, which is not a hedge but a limit on the model’s domain.

The deficit metaphor assumes that the present-tense question is the primary source of the reader’s engagement. It assumes a reader leaning forward into what happens next, whose forward lean is interrupted by the past. That assumption holds for most plot-driven fiction, and it is a fair description of the reading experience for the majority of published novels. It is false, and misleading, for a substantial and distinguished body of writing in which:

  • The past is the subject, and the present is a device for approaching it. The Remains of the Day has almost nothing happening in 1956; the car goes west, the radiator overheats, a man walks up a hill. The present timeline is deliberately the thinner of the two, and the interest lives in the retrospect.
  • The past cannot be told in sequence, so its interruptions are the represented condition rather than a structural choice (§3.7).
  • The reader’s engagement is with the act of reconstruction itself. In The Good Soldier and Absalom, Absalom! the pull is not what happens next but what actually happened, and can this teller be trusted about it. This is Sternberg’s curiosity operating not as a subsidiary interest but as the engine of the whole book.
  • The book’s proposition is about time. Proust’s narrator is not delayed by involuntary memory; involuntary memory is the subject the novel exists to examine.

For these books the deficit model does not merely fail to apply; it runs backwards. In them, forward motion is the interruption. §16 develops the point as the chapter’s closing argument, and §7.0 states the procedural consequence: the protocol is a revision instrument for books whose present timeline carries the engine, and a writer whose book is not one of those should say so before applying it, rather than discovering it test by test.
