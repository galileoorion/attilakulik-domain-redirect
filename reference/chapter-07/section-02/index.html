Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion in this territory is unusually loose. “Flashback” is applied indifferently to a two-page dramatised scene, to a sentence in the past perfect, to a character’s spoken reminiscence, and to a whole novel’s retrospective design. Since these have different lengths, different costs and different failure modes, the chapter begins by separating them.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A cutaway is any passage in which the narrative leaves the temporal position of its present action to present material from an earlier point in the story’s chronology. It has two principal registers. Backstory is the past told — conveyed by summary, implication, report or dialogue, without the reader being required to live through it. Flashback is the past dramatised — a scene in its own right, rendered moment by moment, and subject to every rule that governs a scene.

Four elements of that definition need emphasis.

  • “The temporal position of its present action.” Genette’s term for this is the first narrative (§3.1), and the chapter uses it throughout. Everything called past here is past relative to that reference point, not relative to the reader or to history.
  • “Register.” The distinction between backstory and flashback is not a distinction between two kinds of content. It is a distinction between two ways of delivering the same content, and it is the chapter’s central craft decision (§6).
  • “Earlier point in the story’s chronology.” Movements in the other direction — prolepsis, the flash-forward — are the cutaway’s formal mirror, and are treated at §3.3 and as register 14 in §6.4, but they are not this chapter’s main subject.
  • “Any passage.” The definition deliberately includes the single subordinate clause. A great deal of a novel’s past is delivered in units too small to be noticed as cutaways at all, and one of this chapter’s practical arguments is that those units are usually underused (§6.4, registers 1–4).

2.2 Scale and extent

Cutaways range over four orders of magnitude, and advice calibrated to one scale is frequently misapplied to another.

Scale Typical length Reader’s experience Governing question
The implication A clause; a phrase Barely registered as a departure Is the fact needed here?
The report A sentence to a paragraph A brief change of pitch Told or shown? (§6.3)
The scene 300–3,000 words A full temporal relocation Does it earn the suspension?
The structure A whole strand of the book The past as a second timeline Is the architecture declared? (§4.6)

The chapter treats the third scale as primary, because it is where the craft decisions carry the most weight and where drafts most often go wrong. But the arithmetic of the other three matters. A novel that delivers its past exclusively at the third scale has, in effect, decided that everything the reader needs about the past deserves a scene — which is almost never true, and produces the bloat diagnosed at §8.4.

A useful rule of thumb, offered as a starting position and not a law: the past should be delivered at the smallest scale that does the job, and the burden of proof rises with the scale. A clause needs no justification. A paragraph needs to be the right paragraph. A scene needs an argument. A second timeline needs a structural reason (§4.6).

2.3 The term most often confused: exposition

Backstory and flashback are frequently equated with exposition, and the equation causes trouble in both directions.

Exposition, in Sternberg’s precise usage (§3.4), is the category of information a narrative must convey about its antecedents in order for the present action to be intelligible: who these people are, what the situation is, what has already happened. It is a category of content, distributed across a book, and it is not confined to the past — exposition can concern a present state of affairs (the layout of the house, the rules of the world) that no cutaway is needed to convey.

Backstory and flashback are two of the several delivery mechanisms available for expositional content. The others include dialogue, description, the character’s habitual behaviour, documents, and the reader’s inference from an unexplained detail — this last being the mechanism Chapter 9 §4.4 calls normalisation.

The practical consequence can be stated as a rule, because it removes a great deal of confusion in revision:

Not all exposition is backstory, and not all backstory is exposition. A cutaway can deliver material the plot does not require at all — an ironic rhyme, a change of texture, a recalibration of sympathy — and much of the past a reader needs is best delivered without any cutaway whatsoever.

The second half of that rule is the more useful. Writers who have been told their backstory is heavy typically respond by compressing it, when the more effective move is often to relocate it out of the cutaway register entirely: into an object, a habit, a way of speaking, an unremarked assumption. Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) treats this at length under the heading of connotation; the ledger in the series’ running example carries eleven years of a man’s history without a single sentence of retrospect.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the cutaway
Anachrony Genette’s general term for any discordance between the order of the story and the order of the telling The genus; analepsis and prolepsis are its two species
Analepsis Narration, after the fact, of an event earlier than the present point of the narrative The formal name for what this chapter calls a cutaway
Prolepsis Narration in advance of an event that will occur later The mirror manoeuvre; §6.4, register 14
Ellipsis A stretch of story time passed over in silence What a completing analepsis later fills (§3.3)
Paralipsis The omission of information a narrative’s own vantage should have supplied A lateral gap rather than a temporal one; typically the material of a late revelation
Frame narrative A first narrative whose principal function is to occasion and contain another The limit case of the embedded tale (§6.4, register 6); see also Chapter 10
Retrospective narration A whole narrative told from a position after its events Not a cutaway: the past is the first narrative here
Memory A character’s mental act of recalling A frequent motivation for a cutaway, and not a synonym for one
Exposition Information about antecedents needed for the present to be intelligible A category of content; the cutaway is one delivery mechanism (§2.3)
Flashforward Popular term for prolepsis Avoid in analysis; it obscures the reach/extent distinction

Two of these deserve a further sentence. Retrospective narration is the case that most often confuses students. Great Expectations, The Remains of the Day and The Good Soldier are all narrated from a position later than most of their events; but in Great Expectations the childhood is the first narrative and the older Pip’s vantage is a layer of commentary upon it, whereas in The Remains of the Day the 1956 motoring trip is the first narrative and the 1920s and 1930s are analepses within it. The distinction is not a technicality. It determines what counts as an interruption of what, and therefore where the deficit falls.

Memory is the other. A character remembering is a psychological event that a narrative may report; it is not the same as the narrative departing to render the remembered thing. A novel can say she remembered the argument about the pen — an event in the present, taking one clause — or it can leave the present and stage the argument. The first is backstory about a memory; the second is a flashback. Conflating them is the source of the drafting habit whereby every cutaway must be introduced by a character visibly remembering, which §6.6 identifies as the weakest available trigger.

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • The mechanics of crossing the gap — the anchor, the drift, the orientation budget, the technique-fit question — belong to Chapter 5 (The Transition), and this chapter cross-refers rather than duplicating. §6.6 handles only what is specific to a backward crossing: what motivates it, and what state the present must be in on return.
  • Separated units preceding the main narrative belong to Chapter 10 (The Prologue). §8.11 treats only the failure that occurs when such a unit is relocated inside the narrative without being rewritten.
  • Shifts of viewpoint belong to Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift). A cutaway to a past scene the present viewpoint character did not witness is doing two things at once, and the viewpoint half of the problem is Chapter 13’s.
  • Whole retrospective structures at book scale — the frame narrative, the memoir novel — are treated here only insofar as they bear on the question of the first narrative (§3.1) and on the structural domain (§4.6).
  • Endings that reinterpret the past belong to Chapter 16 (The Ending). The line quoted in the series’ running example is planted in Chapter 10’s material and lands in Chapter 16’s; this chapter owns the middle term.
