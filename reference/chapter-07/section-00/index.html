Backstory and Flashback

Chapter Abstract

A narrative that leaves its present moment to visit an earlier one performs the most conspicuous manoeuvre available to prose fiction. It suspends a question the reader wanted answered, asks them to hold that question in memory, transports them backwards across a temporal gap, and then requires them to return. Every part of that sequence costs something. This chapter formalises the cost, names what can be bought with it, and sets out the choices available once the decision to leave has been made.

It proceeds in four movements:

  1. Theory — why a backward movement in narrative time is structurally and cognitively expensive, drawing on Gérard Genette’s analysis of anachrony (the chapter’s spine), Meir Sternberg’s account of expositional distribution and of curiosity as distinct from suspense, Paul Ricoeur’s and A. A. Mendilow’s treatments of narrative time, the psychology of temporal shift in discourse comprehension, the trauma-studies literature on a past that cannot be narrated in sequence, and a historical account of the flashback as a partly cinematic import into prose.
  2. Taxonomy — the five domains a cutaway can serve (plot, character, theme, texture, reader knowledge), each anatomised in terms of its textual carriers and its characteristic under- and over-delivery, together with a proposed sixth: the structural domain, in which the cutaway is used as an architectural device rather than for its content.
  3. Method — the principle of multiplicity applied to the past; the backstory/flashback fork and the one-sentence compression check that resolves it; a taxonomy of fourteen registers of the past, from the single subordinate clause of implication to the reconstructed testimony of Absalom, Absalom!; a taxonomy of triggers and returns; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol for revision.
  4. Application — twelve failure patterns, each paired with a published work that commits the fault and succeeds; eight close readings of published cutaways; a full revision case worked on the series’ running example; and an apparatus of exercises, glossary, sources and further reading.

The chapter’s governing claim is stated as a debt:

Every cutaway begins in deficit. Leaving a present-tense question spends interest the narrative has already accumulated, and the past material must buy that interest back — not merely be interesting in itself.

The claim is deliberately harsh, and §3.9 and §16 both qualify it, because it assumes that the present-tense question is the primary source of a reader’s engagement. That assumption is accurate for most plot-driven fiction and false for a substantial tradition of admired writing — Proust, Faulkner, Ford, Morrison, Ishiguro — in which the past is not an interruption of the story but its subject. A chapter that cannot say so has mistaken a local convention for a law.

A note on the examples. The illustrative works are chosen for what they demonstrate. A concordance mapping each concept to the novel that best displays it appears at §10.6. The Core chapter adds post-2000 fiction and screen cases, listed in §14.6.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Distinguish backstory from flashback as two registers of the same material, and explain why the choice between them is felt by readers who could not name it.
  • Apply Genette’s apparatus of anachrony — analepsis and prolepsis, reach and extent, external, internal and mixed, completing and repeating — to any passage of retrospective narration, and explain what each distinction predicts about cost.
  • Explain, with reference to at least three theoretical or empirical sources, why a backward movement in narrative time imposes a processing cost that a forward movement does not.
  • Identify which of the five domains a given cutaway serves, assess whether it serves more than one, and argue for or against the proposed sixth.
  • Select an appropriate register of the past from the fourteen tabulated in §6.4, and defend the choice against at least two alternatives by substitution.
  • Diagnose a draft cutaway using the ten-test protocol, producing a prioritised revision plan rather than a verdict.
  • Recognise the failure patterns by mechanism rather than by surface feature, and articulate the conditions under which each supposed fault is the correct choice.
  • Evaluate the limits of the deficit model, including its assumption that forward motion is the default state of narrative, and the traditions of fiction for which that assumption is inverted.

A caution before beginning, and it matters more in this chapter than in most. The instruments below — six domains, fourteen registers, ten tests, twelve failure patterns — are calibrated against a particular and recent convention: the scene-based flashback with a motivated trigger and a clean return, which is a twentieth-century form with cinematic ancestry (§3.8). Applied to In Search of Lost Time, to Absalom, Absalom!, or to the interpolated tale in Don Quixote, most of them return the answer fail. That is information about the instruments, not about the books.

The proper output of every test in this chapter is a question, not a verdict.
