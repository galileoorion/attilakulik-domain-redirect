Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

1. Introduction: The Cost of Leaving the Present

Continue in Core

1.1 Two temporal movements

Consider two movements in narrative time, each of one paragraph’s length, in the same novel.

In the first, the narrative moves forward: a scene ends, white space intervenes, and the next paragraph begins three weeks later. The reader adjusts to a new time and may have to update the situation model; pending questions can remain open, be answered, or lose their urgency.

In the second, the narrative moves backward: mid-scene, with a question hanging — will she open the drawer, will he answer, does the key fit — the paragraph steps eleven years into the past. Now the reader must do three things at once. They must suspend the pending question rather than resolve it, holding it in memory at some cost. They must build a second situation — a different place, different ages, different people present — while the first remains live. And they must file the new material against the old, since past material acquires its meaning only in relation to the present it explains.

These movements can create different narrative obligations, depending on what each passage interrupts and promises. A backward cutaway may suspend one situation and open another before returning; a forward jump can also require substantial reorientation. Call the craft question of managing that interruption the cutaway problem. Backstory and flashback need a chapter of their own because they offer distinct ways to present antecedents, not because a general cognitive ranking of backward and forward shifts has been established.

There is also a useful revision question. A cutaway to 1971 invites the reader to ask why this earlier material matters here. A forward gap can invite a comparable question when it skips an event the reader expected to see. In either direction, the placement should reward the attention it asks for.

1.2 The claim in its strong form

Every cutaway starts in debt. Leaving a present-tense question spends the interest the narrative has accumulated, and the past has to buy it back. Being interesting is not the same as being owed.

The metaphor of interest as an account will be used throughout, and it is worth being exact about what it does and does not claim.

  • It does claim that reader attention at any given moment is a finite quantity, unevenly distributed across a book, and that its local supply is a function of the questions currently open.
  • It does claim that the debt is positional as well as qualitative. The same past material, moved to a lower-pressure moment in the present timeline, may cost almost nothing. A great deal of what is diagnosed in workshops as a bad flashback is a well-made flashback in the wrong place. This is the model’s most useful practical consequence, because it turns a rewrite into a move, and it is developed as test 8 in §7.1.
  • It does not claim that the past is unwelcome, or that fiction should minimise it. Some of the most durable novels in the language are almost entirely retrospective.
  • It does not claim that the debt must be repaid immediately. A cutaway may run a deficit for pages, and settle it in one line on the return; a novel may run a deficit for its whole length and settle it in the last chapter.
  • It does not apply at all in the form stated to books whose present timeline is deliberately the thinner of the two. For those, the metaphor inverts, and §3.9 and §16 say how.

1.3 Where this chapter sits in the series

Three neighbouring chapters share a border with this one, and the borders need marking, since the commonest error in teaching this material is to run all three together.

Chapter Owns This chapter owns
Chapter 5 (The Transition) How a gap is crossed and the reader re-anchored: the orientation budget, hard cut and time-stamp, anchor and drift Whether to leave the present at all, and which register the past is delivered in once you have
Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) Where the gap is placed in the book’s segmentation The internal architecture of the material on the far side of a backward gap
Chapter 10 (The Prologue) A separated unit before the main narrative, held to a higher bar because it precedes investment A separated passage inside the main narrative, held to a different bar because it interrupts investment

The relation to Chapter 5 is the one students most often blur. A flashback requires a transition into it and a transition out of it, and both of those are Chapter 5’s material: the anchor after the gap, the drift when the anchor is deliberately delayed, the orientation budget spent on getting the reader from one side to the other. This chapter begins one step earlier and ends one step later. It asks whether the crossing should be attempted, what the far side should look like when you get there, and what condition the present must be in when you come back.

The relation to Chapter 10 is a formal symmetry. A prologue and a flashback often contain the same material — an earlier event, dramatised, in another time. The difference is entirely positional, and the costs invert. A prologue arrives before the reader has invested in anything, and must therefore be interesting without accumulated context, as Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) describes. A flashback arrives after the reader has invested, and must therefore be worth more than the accumulated context it interrupts. The same two pages, moved from the front of the book into chapter nine, change from a unit that must generate interest to a unit that must justify spending it. This is why the manoeuvre catalogued at §8.11 — cutting a prologue on advice and reinserting it later as a flashback, unchanged — fails so reliably: the material has been moved across a boundary where the criteria reverse.

Two further dependencies carry forward from earlier chapters:

  1. A flashback is a scene, and Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) still applies to it in full. Someone wants something, meets resistance, and the situation is different at the end. A cutaway that is called a flashback but contains no want, no resistance and no change is not a flashback; it is backstory with scenery, and §6.3 will show how to detect this in one operation.
  2. The four domains of Chapter 1 — plot, character, theme, texture — are the base taxonomy of this book, and Chapter 1 proposes a fifth, the relational domain. Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) adopts it; Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) adds the world and audits relations under character, as this chapter also does; this chapter adds reader knowledge, because the cutaway’s most distinctive power is control over what the reader knows and when.
