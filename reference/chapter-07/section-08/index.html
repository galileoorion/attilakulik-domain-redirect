Chapter 7 · Backstory and Flashback

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern below is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what actually goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and — importantly — the exception: a published work that commits the fault and succeeds.

The exceptions are not decorative. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults and not writers who can make choices. Every pattern below fails because of a mechanism, not because of a surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless.

One exception in this section is unusually strong: Absalom, Absalom! builds a major part of its design from the apparently reductive childhood origin described in pattern 8.9. The madeleine episode in In Search of Lost Time is discussed under 8.1 because it clarifies the distinction between an involuntary memory and an absence of a trigger.

8.1 The Unmotivated Cutaway

Symptom. The flashback simply starts. Nothing in the present scene occasioned it; the reader can locate no object, phrase, sensation or question that opened the door.

Mechanism. The departure is legible as an authorial act rather than a narrative one. In close third-person or first-person narration this is a breach of the narration’s own contract, which has promised to remain inside a consciousness; a consciousness does not relocate to 1994 because the manuscript needs a fact delivered on page 112. The reader does not usually formulate the objection in these terms. They experience it as a loss of confidence — the sudden visibility of a writer making arrangements.

Persistence. The information really is needed, and the writer really has reached the point where the reader needs it. The diagnosis is right and the prescription is wrong: the need is expositional, and the response is a scene.

Remedy. Either supply a trigger (§6.6) or move the cutaway to a location where a trigger already exists. Test 8. In practice the second is more often correct, because a trigger invented to justify a placement tends to be over-apt (§5.4).

The exception. In Search of Lost Time (1913–27) does not lack a trigger. The taste of a madeleine dipped in tea recalls a taste the narrator knew as a child at Combray. He cannot at first identify what the sensation is offering him and tries to recover it. The useful exception is narrower: a concrete sensory cue can occasion a memory without the character willing the recollection or knowing its meaning in advance. Involuntary memory differs from deliberate recollection, not from triggered recollection (§9.1).

The general principle the exception establishes: the trigger requirement is a device for keeping a close, character-bound narration honest. Where a narration has other sources of authority — a philosophical first person, a confident omniscience, a declared retrospective frame — the requirement lapses. Tolstoy needs no trigger; neither does Eliot; neither does the narrator of The Death of Ivan Ilyich, who simply announces that the life had been most ordinary and proceeds.

8.2 The Redundant Flashback

Symptom. A dramatised past scene that tells the reader something they already knew, or could have inferred without difficulty.

Mechanism. The passage delivers no new information and no new feeling, so the deficit is incurred against nothing. Note the “or new feeling”: a scene that confirms what the reader inferred can still be worth its place if it supplies weight the inference lacked. The failure is not repetition as such; it is repetition without difference (§3.3).

Persistence. Writers write the flashback before they write the present material that makes it redundant, and then cannot see the redundancy because they wrote the scene first and it feels foundational. This is the same mechanism as Chapter 9’s throat-clearing: composition apparatus retained in the finished text.

Remedy. Test 9. If the cutaway is repeating, name what the second pass adds. If nothing can be named in one sentence, cut. If something can, ensure it is visible — the difference must be perceptible on a single reading.

The exception. The Good Soldier (1915). Ford’s Dowell returns to the same handful of events — the visits to Nauheim, Florence’s behaviour, Edward’s affairs, the four-day sequence in which everything collapses — a dozen times or more. Almost every return is formally redundant: the reader has been told. The novel survives because each return is differently wrong. Dowell revises, retracts, discovers he has misunderstood, and lets slip something he had previously suppressed. The material is constant and the narrator is not, which converts repetition from waste into the book’s principal instrument (§9.3).

8.3 The Momentum Killer

Symptom. The cutaway arrives at the story’s most tense present moment and stops it, with a payoff that does not match the interruption.

Mechanism. Tension is not preserved during an interruption; it is replaced. A reader given six pages of other material does not spend those pages holding the previous tension in reserve — they build a new engagement with the new material, and the old tension has to be restarted from a lower level on return. The suspension described at §3.6 is a cognitive fact about the frame, not an emotional fact about anticipation.

Persistence. The technique of cutting away at peak tension to intensify it is real, taught, and works — at the right length. Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) and Chapter 5 (The Transition) both treat deferral as a legitimate device. The failure is a misjudgement of scale rather than of kind.

Remedy. At peak tension, measure the interruption in sentences, not pages. If the material requires more than that, it is in the wrong place: test 8.

The exception. The scar of Odysseus, Odyssey XIX. Eurycleia’s hand is on the scar; the recognition is a heartbeat away; Homer breaks off for some seventy lines to narrate the boar hunt that made it. This is the most conspicuous momentum killer in European literature, and Auerbach’s reading in Mimesis (1946; trans. Willard Trask, 1953) argues that it is not a suspense device at all — that Homer’s world has no background, that the excursus is fully illuminated rather than held in shadow, and that nothing is suspended while it runs. The exception therefore establishes something stronger than a licensed deviation: the momentum test presupposes a narrative mode in which foreground and background exist, and in a mode that lacks the distinction, the test has no object.

A second and more modern exception: Hugo’s Waterloo in Les Misérables (1862), which opens the novel’s second part, in the middle of Jean Valjean’s story, with an extended historical account and is defensible on the ground that the book’s subject is the relation between individual lives and historical forces. Chapter 14 (The Subplot) treats the Hugolian digression as the limit case of its own device; considered here, it is a reminder that a book may decide its interruptions are its argument.

8.4 The Info-Dump Backstory

Symptom. Paragraphs or pages of compressed history with no scene-level engine, read as a report rather than felt as consequence. A particular risk wherever worldbuilding or family history tempts a writer to deliver a whole system at once.

Mechanism. Two failures, usually simultaneous, and both are the same as Chapter 9 §8.3 diagnoses for openings. First, per Sternberg (§3.4), delivering exposition early spends an asset that would have generated curiosity. Second, information is not memorable without a container: a reader given a family’s four generations before they care about anyone in it has nowhere to store the material and will retain almost none of it, so the dump fails even on its own terms.

Persistence. The writer knows the history intimately and fears the reader will be lost without it. The fear is real; the remedy is misdirected. The reader does not need to know the history; they need to believe the writer knows it, which is achieved by confident specificity rather than by explanation.

Remedy. Convert into implication. Ask of each expository sentence: which character would say this, to whom, and why? Then attempt the material at registers 1 and 4 (§6.4) — the clause of implication and the iterative habitual — before allowing it any more space.

The exception. Père Goriot (1835). Balzac’s long accounts of the Maison Vauquer, of the pension’s economics, of each lodger’s antecedents, are info-dumps by any contemporary standard, and they are causal rather than decorative: the novel’s argument is that the lodgers are what the rent makes them, so the rent must be specified before the people can be understood. Chapter 9 §4.4 reads the same material as world-building in an opening; considered as backstory, it demonstrates that a novel with a determinist thesis needs the determinants on the page.

Moby-Dick (1851) supplies the more extreme case. The cetology chapters are expositional mass with no scene-level engine whatever, placed at intervals through a chase narrative, and they are the reason the book is what it is rather than an adventure story.

8.5 The Untethered Return

Symptom. The cutaway ends and the present scene resumes as though nothing had happened — no acknowledgement, no consequence, no visible change in what the character does next.

Mechanism. The deficit is never discharged. The reader has paid the cost of the departure and receives nothing at the point where the payment was due. Note that the cutaway may have been perfectly good: an untethered return can follow an excellent flashback, and the fault lies entirely in one sentence.

Persistence. The return is written last, when the writer’s attention has already moved to the next scene, and it is treated as a re-entry problem rather than as the cutaway’s payoff. Chapter 5’s vocabulary makes this worse rather than better: a writer thinking about anchors will produce a competent anchor and stop.

Remedy. Test 5. Rewrite the first sentence after the cutaway to carry one of the four changes at §6.6 — preferably a changed question, which is available in nearly every case and requires no plot event.

The exception. “El curioso impertinente”, the interpolated novella in Don Quixote, Part I. A complete separate story, read aloud at the inn, occupying three chapters, and returning the narrative to the state it was in — nothing in Don Quixote’s situation is altered by it. Cervantes was criticised for the interpolations in his own lifetime, replied to the criticism inside Part II, and largely abandoned the practice there. The case is instructive because it is contested: the untethered return was a recognised convention that ceased to be one, and Cervantes’s own change of practice is the clearest available evidence that this prohibition is historical rather than structural.

8.6 The Second-Chapter Flashback

This pattern is added to the original five.

Symptom. The novel leaves its present for an extended visit to the past before the end of the second chapter.

Mechanism. Per §3.1, anachrony is measured against a first narrative, and a first narrative that has not been consolidated cannot be departed from. The reader has not yet formed the model that the cutaway would interrupt, so the departure does not register as a departure — it registers as the book not having decided what it is. Worse, the reader has no basis for evaluating the past material, since its whole function is to bear on a present they do not yet possess.

Persistence. The writer knows the past material is essential and delivers it at the first opportunity. This is also an artefact of drafting order: a writer who wrote the backstory first to understand their characters tends to place it early.

Remedy. Delay. As a rule of thumb, the first substantial cutaway should follow the point at which the reader could describe the book’s present situation in a sentence. That is usually later than it feels.

The exception. The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886). Chapter I is the funeral; Chapter II opens the retrospect and the retrospect is the rest of the novella. The pattern is committed in its most extreme available form and the book is a masterpiece, for a reason that can be stated: Tolstoy consolidates the first narrative in a single chapter by making it complete. The funeral has a beginning, a middle and an end, a protagonist in Peter Ivanovich, a want (to get away to his card game), and a conclusion. There is a first narrative to depart from because Chapter I is a finished thing. Compare the usual draft version of this pattern, in which chapter one is an unfinished fragment of a scene and chapter two goes back — and note that the difference is not the location of the cutaway but the completeness of what precedes it (§9.8).

8.7 The Nested Flashback

Added.

Symptom. A cutaway inside a cutaway: a character in a flashback remembers an earlier thing, which is dramatised.

Mechanism. The reader must track two nested temporal levels (§3.6) and eventually return through both. This can become confusing if the intermediate level is not established clearly before another begins. A possible symptom is that the return from the inner cutaway goes unnoticed and the reader mislocates the following passage. No measured “worse than double” cost is established here.

Persistence. Nesting arises naturally from association: one memory produces another. The writer, who knows where they are, does not feel the load.

Remedy. Flatten. Nearly all nested material can be delivered as a single-level cutaway, or as a clause of implication (register 1) inside the outer one. If both levels must be dramatised, mark the layers structurally — a section break, a change of register — rather than relying on tense alone, since the pluperfect cannot go deeper than one step in English without becoming unreadable.

The exception. Wuthering Heights (1847) nests three and sometimes four deep: Lockwood’s diary contains Nelly’s chronicle, which contains Isabella’s letter and Zillah’s report, which contain further reported speech. Frankenstein (1818) does the same — Walton’s letters contain Victor’s narrative, which contains the creature’s, which contains the creature’s account of the De Lacey family’s history. Both are among the most durable novels in English.

The mechanism of their success is identifiable and teachable: each layer is marked by a change of voice. Nelly does not sound like Lockwood; the creature does not sound like Victor. The reader tracks the level by the diction rather than by the tense, which removes the load entirely. This is a general lesson worth extracting: nesting fails when the layers are indistinguishable, not when they are numerous (§9.2).

8.8 The Better Past

Added.

Symptom. The flashbacks are more vivid, more urgent and more interesting than the present timeline. Readers report that they liked the book but skimmed the modern sections.

Mechanism. The writer’s imaginative investment is in the past material — which is often where the novel’s originating idea lived — and the present has been constructed as a delivery mechanism for it. Two consequences follow. Every cutaway now pays a positive return, so the diagnostic protocol reports success while the book fails. And the reader, having learned that the past is where the value is, begins reading the present as an obstacle.

Persistence. It is invisible from inside, because each individual cutaway tests well. This is the failure the domain model is least able to detect, and the reason test 10 exists.

Remedy. Two options, and they are two different books. Either strengthen the present until it can carry the book — which usually means giving the present timeline its own value shifts rather than only its own occasions for remembering — or accept the diagnosis and restructure: make the past the first narrative, and let the present become an explicit frame.

The exception. The Remains of the Day (1989). Ishiguro’s present timeline is deliberately the thinner of the two, and its thinness is the point. Six days in July 1956 contain a broken radiator, a walk up a hill, a misunderstanding about a butler’s identity, a parting from Miss Kenton at a bus stop in the rain, and an evening on a pier — set against Lord Darlington’s appeasement, the dismissal of the Jewish maids, the death of Stevens’s father during a conference, and twenty years of a refused attachment. The disproportion is the novel’s argument: a life spent in service to another man’s importance has very little present left in it. The book is the definitive counter-case to this pattern, and the discriminating question it establishes is not is the present thinner? but does the book know it, and is the thinness saying something? (§9.6).

Wuthering Heights is the second case, and cruder: Lockwood is a fool, and his fatuousness in the frame exists so that the passion in the chronicle can be measured against it.

8.9 The Childhood-Explains-Everything Cutaway

Added.

Symptom. A single childhood scene, usually of humiliation, loss or a parental failure, offered as the origin of an adult character’s defining trait.

Mechanism. Three faults at once. Structurally, it is the expensive cell of §3.2 — long reach, short extent — requiring the reader to build a whole vanished context for one scene’s occupancy. Psychologically, it proposes a hydraulic model of personality that readers do not believe, because it does not match their experience of anyone including themselves. And narratively, an explained character is a closed question: once the reader has the key, the lock stops being interesting.

Persistence. It is what the craft phrase character motivation is usually taken to mean, and it is reinforced by a century of popular psychology and by screen conventions in which a single traumatic memory is the standard shorthand for interiority.

Remedy. Prefer illumination to explanation (§4.2). Move the material nearer: a recent memory usually makes the same point at a fraction of the construction cost, and does not carry the determinist implication. Where the childhood material must stay, deliver it in a register that does not claim causal authority — the iterative (register 4), which describes what was normal, or the clause of implication (register 1).

The exception. Absalom, Absalom! (1936). Thomas Sutpen’s whole design — the land, the house, the sons, the ruthlessness — is traced to a single childhood scene in which a boy sent to the front door of a Virginia plantation is told by a liveried slave to go round to the back. This is the childhood-explains-everything cutaway in its purest and most schematic form, and the novel is one of the twentieth century’s great achievements.

Two features make the difference, and both are transferable. The origin is reported, not narrated: it comes to the reader through Sutpen’s account to General Compson, relayed through Mr Compson to Quentin and thence to Shreve, so that what the reader receives is not the boy at the door but a man’s explanation of himself, at four removes, from people with reasons to believe it. And the novel treats the explanation’s very neatness as evidence — a design so completely accounted for by one afternoon is itself a symptom. Faulkner makes the reductiveness of the device the device’s subject, which is the only reliable way to use it.

8.10 The Italicised Memory

Added.

Symptom. Cutaways marked by typography rather than by construction: italics, indentation, a different font, a row of asterisks — for material that is otherwise indistinguishable from the surrounding narration.

Mechanism. The typography is doing work the prose declined to do. Two costs follow. The reader is trained to recognise a category of passage rather than to feel a change in the narrative, which converts the past from an experience into a labelled section; and sustained italics are physically harder to read, so the marked passages are the ones the eye learns to skip. There is a third cost, subtler: typographic marking removes the writer’s incentive to solve the transition, so the entrances and exits go unwritten.

Persistence. It is easy, it is unambiguous, and it looks like design. It is also common in published fiction, which supplies the writer with precedent.

Remedy. Attempt the passage with the marking removed. If it is then unclear where the past begins, the prose has a real problem which the italics were concealing; solve it with tense, with a shift of register, or with a trigger. Restore the typography afterwards only if it is doing something the prose cannot.

The exception. The Sound and the Fury (1929). Faulkner’s Benjy section uses italics among its cues for shifts between periods that Benjy experiences without a stable chronology. Typography helps the reader distinguish layers; it is not literally the only available signal, since scene content and recurring people also orient the reader. The useful craft question is whether the marking helps the reader follow a viewpoint whose temporal associations differ from the narrator’s.

Beloved offers the more radical alternative: Morrison marks her deepest temporal and vocal shifts largely by changes in syntax and diction rather than by typography, and the shift is unmistakable without a single italic.

8.11 The Prologue Relocated

Added.

Symptom. A dramatised past scene, usually of two to four thousand words, sitting between chapters in the first third of the book, self-contained and tonally distinct — and identifiable, once you know to look, as a prologue that was moved.

Mechanism. §1.3 sets out the reason. A prologue and a flashback can contain identical material and are governed by inverse criteria. A prologue must generate interest without accumulated context; a flashback must be worth more than the accumulated context it interrupts. Material written to satisfy the first criterion has been optimised for the wrong thing: it is self-sufficient, which means it does not connect; it establishes rather than recolours; and it has no trigger, because it never needed one.

Persistence. The advice to cut a prologue is common and often correct, and a writer who has been given it reasonably concludes that the material should be preserved elsewhere. The move is made without rewriting because the passage is finished, and finished prose resists revision.

Remedy. Do not move a prologue. Rewrite it as a cutaway, which usually means shortening it by two thirds, attaching it to a present-tense trigger, and changing its ending so that it hands something to the scene it is interrupting rather than concluding on its own.

The exception. Season of Migration to the North (1966; trans. Denys Johnson-Davies). Mustafa Sa’eed’s life story — Khartoum, Cairo, London, the women, the killing, the trial, the prison years — is in effect a complete separate novel, and it is delivered not at the front of the book but into the middle of it, as a night-long confession to the narrator. Structurally it is a prologue relocated: self-contained, tonally distinct, longer than any surrounding unit.

It works because the relocation is motivated by the present. The narrator has already been made curious; Mustafa has a reason for telling and chooses his moment; the telling is an event in the frame narrative, with consequences for the man who hears it. The general rule the exception establishes is that relocated material must be given an occasion inside the present — which is what a prologue, by definition, does not have (§9.7).

8.12 The Undeveloped Repeat

Added.

Symptom. The novel returns several times to the same past period, and the returns are the same beat told again: the same tone, the same relationship, the same information at slightly different lengths.

Mechanism. Distinct from §8.2 in that no single return is redundant — each supplies something — but the sequence has no direction. A reader who has been to a past period three times expects the fourth visit to go further, and a fourth visit that does not is felt as the book marking time. The underlying error is treating the past as a place to be revisited rather than as a sequence to be advanced.

Persistence. Writers develop a strong relationship with one past period and return to it because it is where they are comfortable. The audit at §5.5 catches this in the reach column: a uniform reach across a manuscript means one era is doing all the work.

Remedy. Order the returns and give the sequence a direction — chronological approach, escalating disclosure, or increasing proximity to a withheld centre. Then check that each successive return is closer to something than the last.

The exception. Beloved (1987). Morrison returns to the events at 124 and to Sweet Home repeatedly, from Sethe, from Paul D, from Denver, from Stamp Paid, from Baby Suggs, at varying distances and in varying registers. The novel is built almost entirely from this manoeuvre. It works because the returns converge: each approach comes nearer to the centre than the last, so that the reader experiences a spiral rather than a circle, and the final full account arrives as the completion of a movement the whole book has been making. The distinction between a spiral and a circle is the whole of this pattern, and it is assessable in one operation: list the returns in order and ask whether they get closer to anything (§9.5).
