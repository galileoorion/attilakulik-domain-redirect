Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Eight close readings and a revision case. The apparatus is the same throughout: identify the beats, identify the chain structure (§6.3), and assess the compression (§5).

9.1 Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment (1866) — the recursive sequel

This is the chapter’s central case, and the one that either breaks the model or reveals its deepest application.

The structure is easily stated. Raskolnikov’s goal is formed and rehearsed in Part One; the conflict is his own body, the staircase, the porter, the axe, Lizaveta’s unplanned return; the disaster is the murder itself, at the end of Part One — a compounded failure of the most complete kind, since he kills two women instead of one, takes almost nothing, and immediately discovers that the theory the act was meant to prove has not been proved. Five parts and an epilogue remain.

Nothing in those five parts is a scene in the narrow sense that terminates the chain. Raskolnikov forms goals — evade Porfiry, find out what Razumikhin knows, discover what Svidrigailov overheard, get Sonya to read the Lazarus passage — and every one of them is a goal generated by his reaction to the murder rather than by a decision that closed a completed sequel. The book is one enormous Sequel with scenes inside it.

Why this does not stall. Apply the test at §5.4: does the expansion yield? Each of the five parts introduces a new consideration into the same dilemma.

Part New consideration entering the sequel
Two The body will not obey the theory; illness as refutation
Three Porfiry: the theory stated aloud, in public, as an article
Four Sonya, and the possibility that suffering is not a defeat
Five Katerina Ivanovna’s death; the spectacle of another’s ruin
Six Svidrigailov as the theory lived out to its conclusion, and his suicide

Five parts, five new terms. The dilemma — confess or persist — is stable throughout, and it is never re-weighed in the same words twice, because each part changes what confession would mean.

The hinge, and where it is. The decision to confess arrives at the end of Part Six. But the criterion change (§4.7) does not happen there; it happens earlier and by degrees, and locating it is a genuine critical problem worth setting to students. The candidates are Sonya’s reading of the raising of Lazarus, the confession to Sonya, and — the strongest case — Svidrigailov’s suicide, which shows Raskolnikov the only other available future. What is not in doubt is that the confession is made on grounds Raskolnikov would have rejected in Part One, and that the epilogue insists the real change occurs after the novel ends.

The verdict. Crime and Punishment does not break the model; it runs one iteration of it across six hundred pages. Every beat is present, correctly ordered, and correctly caused. What the novel demonstrates is that the six beats say nothing whatever about duration — that a structure devised to organise a magazine story will describe, without modification, one of the least commercial novels ever written, provided one abandons the assumption that a sequel is a chapter.

It also demonstrates the model’s limit. What the six beats cannot tell you is why five hundred pages of a stable dilemma are gripping rather than tedious. For that one needs Brooks on desire and delay (§3.2), or Bakhtin on the unfinalised consciousness, or simply the observation that the prose is doing something the diagram cannot record.

Structural claims here survive translation; the texture claims do not. Readers working from Garnett, Pevear and Volokhonsky, or Katz will find the same chain and three different Raskolnikovs.

9.2 Brontë, Jane Eyre (1847) — the interrupted wedding

Goal: to be married, that morning. Conflict: none, until Chapter 26, when a solicitor and a stranger interrupt the service. Disaster: the compounded failure, reaching backwards — the marriage is prevented, the household’s year of strangeness acquires an explanation, and Jane’s understanding of her own position is retrospectively rewritten.

What follows is a model sequel of unusual proportions.

  • Reaction occupies the remainder of Chapter 26 and is rendered almost entirely as sensory withdrawal — the room, the light, the physical incapacity — and by a shift into a register of dissociation.
  • Dilemma occupies most of Chapter 27, and Brontë does the thing most drafts will not: she gives the wrong option the better speeches. Rochester’s case is materially sound and emotionally overwhelming, and the novel lets it run for pages.
  • The hinge is Jane’s declaration that she cares for herself, and that the more friendless and unsustained she is, the more she will respect herself. This is not a decision; it is the criterion from which a decision can be derived, stated in the open.
  • Decision is executed rather than announced: she leaves before dawn, with almost nothing, and the concreteness of the leaving — the road, the coach fare, the parcel left behind — is what makes it irreversible.

The pair is a demonstration of §4.7’s argument in miniature. Remove the criterion sentence and the beats are all still present, correctly formed; what disappears is any reason to regard the departure as an arc rather than an event.

9.3 Dickens, Great Expectations (1861) — the convict on the marshes

The opening chapters provide a compact scene–sequel sequence, and the first chapter is a teaching text for the smallness of a legitimate goal.

Goal: Pip, in the churchyard, is trying to work out from the shape of the lettering what his dead parents were like. It is an objective of almost no magnitude, and it is sufficient, because it is specific.

Conflict and disaster arrive together, in a voice — “Hold your noise!” — and a man among the graves who takes the child by the throat and threatens, in the novel’s most quoted line of dialogue, to cut it. The disaster is not the threat; it is the demand: a file and wittles, by morning, or the young man who is hidden with him will find Pip out.

The sequel is chiefly in Chapter 2, with terror rendered in Pip’s dream of the gibbet and his guilty perception of the pantry. His dilemma is real and both options are costly: steal from Mrs Joe, or fail the convict. His decision is executed physically, in the pantry, in the dark, with a pork pie. His guilt continues to colour his perception in Chapter 3, when the cattle seem to accuse him on the marshes.

Two lessons. First, the goal beat may be nearly weightless if the disaster is not. Second, and more useful in revision: much of the sequel is carried by objects — the file, the pie, the brandy — and there is not one sentence of abstract deliberation in it.

9.4 Herbert, Dune (1965) — the goal made physical

The gom jabbar scene is the model’s clearest illustration in twentieth-century fiction, and it repays study because it is so mechanically clean.

Beat Realisation
Goal Keep the hand in the box. Stated as an instruction, with a stated penalty; success is externally visible
Conflict Pain, and the needle at the neck. The resistance is escalating and cannot be negotiated with
Disaster The poisoned success (§4.3). Paul passes, and passing identifies him as what the Bene Gesserit have been breeding for
Reaction Physical: the hand examined and found unmarked, which is more disturbing than injury
Dilemma Not Paul’s, initially, but Jessica’s — and the split is instructive
Decision Compressed almost to nothing, and carried by the departure for Arrakis

The scene’s efficiency comes from a decision made much earlier in the composition: the goal is physical, binary and observable, which allows Herbert to spend the entire unit on unexplained vocabulary and metaphysical exposition without the reader losing the thread. This is the general principle worth extracting: the more difficult the material, the simpler the goal beat must be. A reader who knows what would count as failure will tolerate a great deal of strangeness around it.

9.5 Dumas, The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46) — the clean chain

The novel is the purest surviving demonstration of structure 1 in §6.3, and it earns its place in a university syllabus by being unembarrassed about it.

The opening pair is exemplary. Goal: Dantès, newly promoted, is to be married that afternoon. Conflict: three men with reasons to prevent it. Disaster: arrest at his own betrothal feast. Sequel: the Château d’If, where the reaction beat is expanded to years, the dilemma is despair against endurance, and the decision — supplied by Faria — is to survive, learn, and escape. That decision is the next scene’s goal, as the model requires.

Two features deserve comment.

The engine’s visibility is a virtue here, not a defect. The feuilleton appeared in Le Journal des débats in several runs from August 1844 to January 1846; a reader returning after an interruption needs the goal-state to be recoverable in a sentence. The clean chain is a solution to a distribution problem before it is an aesthetic.

The novel outgrows its own machinery. The last movement introduces dilemmas the revenge engine cannot price — whether the children of the guilty are legitimate targets — and it is at this point that the clean chain stops being sufficient and the book becomes serious. The lesson for a student is the reverse of the one usually drawn: the engine is not what makes the novel good; it is what makes the novel legible for long enough that its one hinge can land.

9.6 Heller, Catch-22 (1961) — the chain broken as argument

Heller’s novel is the strongest counterexample in this chapter, because it fails almost every test in §7 and is not defective.

The chapters are numbered and often titled for characters; events are narrated out of order, repeated with variation, and returned to with new information; the causal relation between units is frequently unavailable. The novel’s governing device is the regulation itself — there is only one catch, and it is Catch-22 — which is a causal loop: a rule whose application produces the condition that makes the rule apply. Where the model asks for descent, Heller supplies circularity.

Three observations.

  1. The broken chain is the thesis. The book argues that the institution destroys causal reason, and a novel that proceeded by therefore would refute its own case in its form.
  2. Something replaces the chain. Not nothing — that would be incoherence. Heller substitutes two other engines: the escalating mission quota, which supplies a crude but genuine forward pressure, and the progressive disclosure of Snowden’s death, a curiosity structure (§6.1) in which the same event is returned to and each return supplies a further detail, until the final telling.
  3. The chain is therefore only apparently absent. The Snowden material is a chain run backwards (§6.3, structure 11), embedded in a novel whose forward chain is deliberately jammed. Heller has not abandoned causal structure; he has moved it into the past, where his subject requires it.

9.7 Atwood, The Handmaid’s Tale (1985) — the sequel-dominant novel

Offred’s narration is very largely reaction and dilemma. Scenes occur — the Ceremony, the shopping expeditions, the Commander’s office, the birth, Jezebel’s — but they are short, and they are frequently reported rather than enacted. The bulk of the text is a consciousness processing what it cannot act on.

The reason is structural rather than stylistic. The decision beat is unavailable to a person without options, and Gilead has removed them. Atwood does not compensate by inventing agency; she builds the book on the absence. Two devices help sustain momentum, while a third reframes what the reader has encountered:

  • Curiosity rather than suspense — the withheld past (the daughter, Luke, the escape attempt), distributed across the novel in Sternberg’s delayed mode.
  • The formal frame — the “Historical Notes”, which appear after Offred’s account and reveal retrospectively that it has survived as a document with a provenance. Chapter 10 (The Prologue) treats this device as the prologue’s structural inverse.
  • The small recovered agencies — Scrabble, butter as hand cream, the message in the cupboard. Each is a decision beat scaled to what remains possible.

The lesson for the model is exact: a beat may be structurally unavailable, and a novel must then pay for it with another engine. What it cannot do is omit it and supply nothing.

9.8 Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939) — the collective sequel

Steinbeck alternates the Joads’ narrative with sixteen intercalary chapters that contain no continuing characters: the turtle crossing the highway, the tractors, the used-car lots, the roadside diner, the growing wrath in the eyes of the hungry.

Read as description, they are digressions. Read structurally, they are the reaction beat given to a population. The Joads suffer a disaster; the intercalary chapter that follows registers it at the scale of a class, in a different register and with a different narrator. The private beat is priced socially, and the two chains — the family’s and the migration’s — are braided so that neither has to carry the other’s work.

Two costs need naming. The chapters interrupt identification with the Joads, and some readers find that interruption frustrating; the collective sequel is a demanding structure rather than a free upgrade. The technique also requires a narrator willing to speak in a register the character chapters do not use, which is a formal contract that must be declared early — Steinbeck declares it in Chapter 1, before any Joad appears.

9.9 A revision case: the household

The textbook’s running example — three siblings, a dead father’s shop, a mother who changed the locks in March — supplies the material. The pair follows directly from the doorway exchange analysed in Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange): Elena learns there that the locks were changed and leaves wanting to know why; Dov gives her the key and withholds the reason.

Version A (draft)

Elena left with the key. She was angry with Dov, but there was no point arguing with him when he got like that, and anyway she had what she’d come for. She got into the truck and drove across town to the shop, thinking about what her mother might say if she asked her directly. She decided it wasn’t worth it. Better to look for herself. She parked outside, let herself in, and started with the register drawer.

Diagnosis

Test Result
1 — Goal Present in the previous scene; fine
3 — Disaster Weakened. “She had what she’d come for” prices the scene as a win. The cost — she trusts her brother less — is asserted (“angry”) and not spent
4 — Cost Fails. No currency is named
5 — Chain Technically intact and entirely carried by connectives: but, anyway, better to
7 — Dilemma False. One option is dismissed in six words on grounds of convenience (“wasn’t worth it”)
10 — Beat weight Reaction: five words, all of them a named emotion. Dilemma: twenty-three. Decision: a subordinate clause
— Patterns present: skipped sequel (§8.3), false dilemma (§8.4), named emotion (§8.12)

Version A contains every necessary item. Its failure is arrangement: the sequel has been summarised rather than performed, so the decision that closes it is a matter of logistics rather than of character.

Version B (the revision)

Reaction — She didn’t cry in the truck, is what she’d tell people later, though she also couldn’t say what she’d done instead: some effortful blank thing with her hands on the wheel, the key already leaving a mark in her palm because she hadn’t unclenched.

Dilemma — She could call their mother and ask outright, and get the same answer Dov had given her, or worse, the silence that meant she’d asked something true. Or she could go through the ledger herself, quietly, and risk finding out Dov’s silence in the doorway hadn’t been protecting their mother. It might have been protecting her.

Decision — She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K.

What the revision did, procedurally

  1. Restored the reaction beat, and rendered it physically. The named emotion (“angry”) is replaced by an unclenched hand and a mark in a palm — §4.4’s rule, and §8.12’s remedy. Note the second move inside it: is what she’d tell people later installs a retrospective narrator who can see what Elena cannot, which is the reliability protocol for the rest of the book.
  2. Priced both options. Calling costs the possibility of the answer; investigating costs the possibility that the silence was protective. Neither is cheap, and — the test at §7.1, test 7 — a sympathetic reader could reasonably prefer either.
  3. Located the real disaster. Version A treats the withheld reason as the cost. Version B locates it in the interpretation of the withholding: Dov may have been protecting Elena, which is worse than obstruction. The costed success of Version A has become the third shape, the poisoned success — the goal achieved and revealed to have been the wrong goal (§4.3).
  4. Made the decision physical and generating. She drove to the shop is the decision and its execution at once (§4.6, “compressed to nothing”), and the register drawer that sticks on the K — planted in Chapter 10 (The Prologue) — supplies the next scene’s opening move without a sentence of setup.
  5. Set the beat weights deliberately. Reaction 44 words, Dilemma 56, Decision 27, against a moderate disaster. Chapter 14’s material — Dov’s sale of the shop — will require a heavier ratio, and the register has now been established at a level that leaves room above it (§5.5).

What Version B has not solved

Three things, and they need stating because the model cannot see any of them.

  • The decision is reversible. Elena could stop at the door, and nothing in the text forecloses calling her mother later. By §7.1, test 9, the beat is weaker than it appears; the passage is carried by momentum rather than by irreversibility.
  • There is no hinge. Elena’s criterion — find out for yourself, do not ask people who will lie — is the criterion she held in the doorway. This is correct for an early chapter, but the sequence is not doing any arc work, and a book made of pairs like this one would produce the treadmill diagnosis at §8.9.
  • The dilemma’s second option is more interesting than its first, which risks the asymmetry the false-dilemma test warns against. Whether It might have been protecting her is a devastating turn or a thumb on the scale is a legitimate editorial disagreement, and a good one to stage in a workshop.
