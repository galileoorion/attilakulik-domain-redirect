Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

6. Interruption, Deferral, and the Shapes of the Chain

Continue in Core

6.1 Historical grounding

Interruption is older than the novel and was industrialised by it. The technique of cutting away from a sequel before its decision lands is the domestic form of a commercial practice with a documented history.

The serial. Dickens’s monthly and weekly numbers, and Dumas’s feuilleton instalments in the Paris press, both required a terminal state that would survive a month or a day of waiting. A deferred choice is one way to carry tension across that interval, because it invites the reader to weigh the options before the character decides. It is one technique among the serial’s varied instalment endings, and Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) treats the placement question in full.

The formalist account. Viktor Shklovsky briefly invokes retardation in “Art as Device” (1917), then develops his account of narrative delay in Theory of Prose (1925), especially in “Stepped Construction and Deceleration”: narrative art works by impeding the straight line, and the impediment is not an obstacle to the story but its substance. Shklovsky’s “stepped construction” — the delay by repetition, by digression, by embedded tale — is the structural ancestor of every technique tabulated below.

The theoretical account. Brooks’s dilatory space (§3.2) supplies the reason the technique works rather than merely irritating: the reader’s desire for the end is what the delay feeds on, and a delay that arrives before desire has been established is simply a slow passage. This is the most useful thing to know about deferral in practice, because it predicts when the technique will fail. Deferral is a tax on desire, and it can only be levied where desire exists. A chapter that breaks off mid-dilemma before the reader has any stake in the outcome has broken off mid-nothing.

The distinction that governs use. Meir Sternberg’s separation of suspense (uncertainty about the future) from curiosity (uncertainty about the past) tells a writer which deferral they are performing. Interrupting a sequel generates suspense; withholding what happened during it generates curiosity. The two are frequently confused in drafting, and they have different payoff obligations: suspense must be resolved by an event, curiosity by a disclosure.

6.2 The core mechanism: the deferred decision

Deferred decision. A sequel whose Reaction and Dilemma are shown but whose Decision is withheld past a chapter or section break, and supplied later — often in a different scene, sometimes by implication rather than statement.

The technique borrows tension from the reader’s own unresolved position. Having watched the weighing, the reader has usually formed a preference, and now wants the decision for two reasons at once: to find out what happens, and to find out whether they were right about this person.

Its one hard condition is this: the deferral must be paid. A decision interrupted and never shown, with a later scene simply assuming a choice was made, is not a deferred sequel. It is a skipped one wearing a deferred one’s excuse — and the reader’s experience is of having been asked to invest attention that was then not collected. §8.10 treats the failure; §7.1, test 5 catches it.

6.3 A taxonomy of chain structures

The clean chain is one option among many, and treating it as the norm is the commonest way of misusing the model. What follows is a working repertoire. The structures are not exclusive; long novels braid several.

# Structure Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 The clean chain Every decision becomes the next goal, on the page, in order The Count of Monte Cristo Maximum legibility and momentum; the reader always knows what is being attempted Predictability; a mechanical feel if sustained without variation
2 The deferred decision Reaction and dilemma shown; decision withheld across a break Dickens’s and Dumas’s serial instalments Tension carried across a gap; the reader’s own judgement engaged Must be paid, and the payment must be visible
3 The interrupted sequel The cut comes earlier still — mid-reaction, before the dilemma has formed Bleak House; most multi-plot Victorian fiction Momentum without abandoning interiority Risks reading as evasion if the reaction is never resumed
4 The compressed sequel All three beats folded into a clause or a verb Thriller and hard-boiled convention; Dune’s transitional passages Pace; a register of competence and speed Characters can flatten into functions; sustained too long, motivation evaporates
5 The braided chain Two or more chains cross-cut, each interrupting the other’s sequel Anna Karenina; Bleak House; War and Peace Each thread’s deferral is filled with another thread’s material, so nothing is dead time Attention tax (Chapter 14); the reader must hold several goal-states at once
6 The displaced sequel The disaster happens to A and is registered by B Mrs Dalloway — Septimus’s death arrives as Clarissa’s sequel Thematic reach; the event acquires a meaning its owner could not give it Requires a viewpoint architecture that permits it (Chapter 13)
7 The sequel-dominant novel Sequels occupy most of the book; scenes are brief and often remembered rather than enacted The Handmaid’s Tale; The Remains of the Day Interiority at maximum; suits constrained or retrospective narrators Momentum must be supplied by something else — usually curiosity about the past
8 The collective sequel The reaction beat is given to a group, or to no one, in a separate unit The Grapes of Wrath’s intercalary chapters Scale; the private disaster is priced socially Interrupts identification; readers frequently skip such chapters
9 The recursive sequel One enormous sequel containing scenes inside itself, each generating further reaction Crime and Punishment, Parts Two to Six The deepest available character work; the disaster is examined from every side Only survives if each stage yields something new (§5.4)
10 The broken chain Causal connection deliberately refused; units follow without descending Catch-22 The absence becomes the argument Requires a governing idea strong enough to replace causality; without one it is simply disordered
11 The chain run backwards The outcome is given first; the chain is reconstructed towards it The Death of Ivan Ilyich Converts suspense into curiosity; every subsequent event is read as cause Forfeits the pull of not knowing what happens
12 The refused decision The sequel completes but produces no commitment; the character declines to choose The Stranger; Goncharov’s Oblomov The refusal characterises absolutely; makes a thesis of passivity Momentum collapses unless the refusal itself escalates

Notes on four of them

The braided chain (5) is the workhorse of the long nineteenth-century novel and remains the standard solution for multi-viewpoint fiction. Its efficiency is that it converts a cost into a benefit: the reader must wait for thread A’s decision, and the waiting is spent inside thread B. The technique has a threshold, treated as the attention tax in Chapter 14 (The Subplot): beyond roughly three or four live chains (an estimate from practice; no study measures it), most readers stop tracking goal-states and begin tracking only characters, at which point the deferrals stop generating tension.

The displaced sequel (6) deserves more attention than it receives. When the disaster belongs to one character and the reaction to another, the event is lifted out of private psychology and given a public meaning. Woolf’s use of it in Mrs Dalloway — a young man’s death, of which Clarissa knows almost nothing, becoming the material of her own reckoning at her party — is the finest example in English, and it is a structure, not an accident.

The recursive sequel (9) is the chapter’s hardest case and is treated at length at §9.1. Its distinguishing feature is that the enclosed scenes are generated by the sequel rather than resolving it: Raskolnikov’s encounters with Porfiry, Sonya, Svidrigailov and Razumikhin are all consequences of his reaction to the murder, and each produces further reaction rather than a decision. The structure is legal within the model; it simply runs one iteration of the model across an entire novel.

The chain run backwards (11) is more common than students expect, and Tolstoy’s The Death of Ivan Ilyich is its purest instance: the death is announced in the first chapter, and everything after is the reconstruction of the chain that produced it. Note what this does to the six beats — it does not remove them, it inverts the reader’s relation to them. Each goal Ivan Ilyich pursues is read, at the moment it is stated, as a step towards a terminus already known. García Márquez’s Chronicle of a Death Foretold (1981) makes the same structure its explicit subject.

6.4 How to use the taxonomy: substitution testing

The taxonomy’s purpose is not classification but substitution. A sequence that is not working is very often the right material in the wrong chain structure, and the productive revision exercise is to ask what it would be as a different one.

Three substitutions that reliably teach something:

Current structure Try instead What it reveals
A clean chain that feels mechanical (1) Defer one decision across a break (2) Whether the decisions are worth waiting for
A stalled recursive sequel (9) Cross-cut with a second chain (5) Whether the interiority is dense enough to be missed
A braided chain the reader cannot follow (5) Collapse to a single chain and give the second thread a collective sequel (8) Whether the second chain has its own engine or only its own material

6.5 Historical drift, and a caution

Practice has moved, and a student should be able to historicise the chapter’s preferences rather than receive them as constants.

Period Characteristic chain practice
Picaresque and early novel Episodic by design; adventures linked by a traveller rather than by causality (Don Quixote, Tom Jones)
Nineteenth-century serial The braided chain and the deferred decision, driven by instalment economics (Bleak House, The Count of Monte Cristo, Great Expectations)
Realist novel of consciousness Sequels expand; disasters are internal and often unmarked (Middlemarch, The Portrait of a Lady)
Modernist The chain loosened or refused; simultaneity and displacement replace succession (Mrs Dalloway, The Sound and the Fury)
Mid-century commercial The clean chain codified and taught; the compressed sequel becomes a house style (Swain’s market)
Post-1960 anglophone literary Sequel-dominant narration; retrospection; the withheld decision (The Remains of the Day, The Handmaid’s Tale)
Contemporary screen-influenced The act-break deferral imported wholesale; the “but/therefore” heuristic (§7.1, test 6) as popular doctrine

Two inferences follow, and they pull against each other, which is the point.

  • The clean chain’s prestige is historically local. It is at its strongest in the middle of the twentieth century, in the commercial fiction market that produced the model, and its dominance in contemporary craft advice is partly an inheritance and partly an effect of screen convention. It is not what the nineteenth-century novel mostly does, and it is not what the modernist novel does at all.
  • The episodic novel is not a primitive stage the form outgrew. Don Quixote is episodic on principle, and the principle is an argument about how experience is actually shaped. A pedagogy that can only say “this would be tightened today” has described a fashion and not a form.
