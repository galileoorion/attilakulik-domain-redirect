Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and its counterexample: a published work that commits the fault and succeeds.

The counterexamples are the section’s intellectual centre. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults; it does not produce writers who can make choices. In every case below, the pattern fails because of a mechanism, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless — or is the whole point of the book.

8.1 The Free Goal

Symptom. A scene opens with the character pursuing something that has no origin in anything previously decided. The reader’s experience is of a book that proceeds by “and then”.

Mechanism. The reader’s causality index (§3.5) is asked to accommodate an unsignalled shift. Because readers generate causal-antecedent inferences automatically, they search for the origin, fail to find one, and pay the processing cost without receiving anything for it. Repeated, this teaches the reader that searching is not worthwhile — after which the book’s genuine connections stop being noticed.

Persistence. Outlining produces it. A writer who has planned the events knows why each is next, and the knowledge feels like it is on the page.

Remedy. Test 2. Where no decision produced the goal, the fault is usually in the previous sequel, not in this scene.

The counterexample. The picaresque tradition is built on free goals. Don Quixote proceeds by encounter: the knight rides out and meets what he meets, and the absence of causal descent is a proposition about the world — that experience is a series, and that the mind which insists on reading it as a chain is the mind under diagnosis. Cervantes’s structural joke is that Quixote himself is the only character who believes in the causal chain.

8.2 The Clean Win

Symptom. The scene ends in unmixed success. The character wanted the thing and got it.

Mechanism. Per Brooks (§3.2), narrative desire is sustained by deferral; a costless satisfaction discharges it. Structurally, the next goal must now be invented rather than inherited, which produces the free goal at 8.1 one unit later. Clean wins are therefore rarely a local problem; they are a problem that surfaces in the following chapter.

Persistence. Writers like their characters, and competence is pleasant to write.

Remedy. Ask what the victory should have cost. The cheapest available conversion is to leave the win intact and charge for it — trust, time, an ally’s good opinion, a fact the character would rather not have learned.

The counterexample. The Count of Monte Cristo runs on clean wins for hundreds of pages. Dantès’s schemes succeed, one after another, more or less as designed, and the novel is not slack. Two mechanisms save it. First, each of his wins is somebody else’s disaster, and Dumas gives the victims sequels — the reader watches Villefort, Danglars and Morcerf react, weigh and decide. The chain is intact; it has simply been handed to the losing side. Second, the moral disaster is deferred across the whole book: Dantès’s method works perfectly and destroys people it should not, and the accumulating cost is the only thing his own sequels are eventually about.

8.3 The Skipped Sequel

Symptom. Disaster, section break, new goal. No reaction, no weighing.

Mechanism. The conversion described at §4.4 never happens: an external event is not turned into an internal state, so the decision that follows has nothing to issue from. Readers report this as characters behaving “randomly” or “coldly”, and misattribute it to characterisation.

Persistence. Sequels are harder to write than scenes, and drafts are written forwards, where the next event is always more attractive than the last one’s consequences.

Remedy. Insert one sequel — a paragraph — after the most costly disaster in the sequence, and re-read. Frequently the surrounding material stops feeling thin without further intervention.

The counterexample. Cormac McCarthy’s Blood Meridian (1985) omits the reaction beat almost entirely, and the omission is the book’s moral argument: violence is narrated without registration because a world that registered it would be a different world. The hard-boiled tradition does the same at lower voltage, where the withheld reaction signals a code of conduct rather than a metaphysics. The distinguishing question is whether the missing beat is legible as missing. McCarthy’s absence is deafening; a draft’s absence is merely absent.

8.4 The False Dilemma

Symptom. Two options are presented and one is obviously correct.

Mechanism. The reader completes the weighing before the character begins it, and every subsequent line of deliberation is read as delay. Worse, the character is diminished: a person who cannot see what the reader sees at a glance has been made slow in order to fill a page.

Persistence. The writer knows which way the plot goes and has unconsciously loaded the option that will be taken.

Remedy. Write the two costs first, in one sentence each, before writing the beat. If one sentence is hard to write, the dilemma does not exist yet.

The counterexample. The Remains of the Day is built almost entirely of false dilemmas, and it is one of the most affecting novels of its period. Stevens weighs options whose correct resolution is obvious to every reader — whether to speak to Miss Kenton, whether to leave his father’s deathbed, whether to question his employer’s politics — and chooses wrongly each time, with immense deliberation. The dilemma is false from outside and real from inside, and the gap between those two positions is the novel. The general principle: a false dilemma fails when the narration cannot see what the reader sees, and succeeds when it can.

8.5 The Stalled Sequel

Symptom. Reaction and dilemma expand until forward motion dies. The book reads as brooding.

Mechanism. Expansion without yield (§5.4). The beat repeats rather than develops: the same considerations are re-weighed in new prose, and the reader — who has understood the position — is waiting for a term to be added.

Persistence. Interior prose is where many literary writers are strongest, and the passage is often the best writing in the draft.

Remedy. Mark every point at which a new consideration enters. If a stretch of three pages introduces none, the stall is located.

The counterexample. An entire tradition. The Remains of the Day stalls for a whole novel; Beckett’s Molloy and The Unnamable make the failure of deliberation to terminate their explicit subject; Goncharov’s Oblomov devotes its first part to a man who cannot get out of bed and is one of the great comic achievements of the Russian nineteenth century. In each case the stall is thematised: the novel is about a person for whom decision is unavailable, and the reader’s impatience is a designed effect rather than a side effect. The test to apply to a draft is whether anyone in the book, including the narrator, has noticed that nothing is being decided.

8.6 The Borrowed Motivation

Added to the original five.

Symptom. The goal is supplied by genre convention rather than by this character’s prior decision: the detective takes the case because detectives take cases; the heir returns because heirs return.

Mechanism. The chain’s causal link is replaced by a generic one. The reader accepts it — genre competence is real, and Jauss’s horizon of expectations does the work — but nothing has been spent, so nothing accumulates. Books built this way often read as competent and weightless.

Persistence. It is invisible from inside, because the goal is motivated: by the genre, which the writer has internalised so thoroughly that it feels like the character’s psychology.

Remedy. Ask what this particular person would lose by declining. If the answer is “the plot”, the motivation is borrowed.

The counterexample. The entire folktale and romance tradition works this way by design. Propp’s function IX is mediation: the hero is dispatched, and the dispatch is the motivation. Motivation in such narratives is structural rather than psychological, and demanding an individual reason for the quest misunderstands the form. The same holds for a great deal of modern genre fiction that is doing what it means to do. Borrowed motivation is a fault only in books that have promised psychological causation elsewhere.

8.7 The Passive Protagonist

Added.

Symptom. Things happen to the character. Goals are formed rarely, feebly, or not at all; the plot is driven by others.

Mechanism. Without a goal there is no yardstick (§4.1); without decisions there is no chain. In a book that has otherwise promised a chain, this is a genuine structural failure, and it is usually accompanied by a second symptom: other characters become more interesting than the protagonist, because they are the ones deciding.

Persistence. Writers often construct a protagonist as an observer of a situation they find interesting, and observation is not an engine.

Remedy. Give the character one small pursuit per unit — not a change of personality, a change of grammar. A passive character who is trying not to be noticed has a goal.

The counterexample — and a concession. This prohibition is the one in this chapter that comes closest to being a genre preference wearing the clothes of a structural principle, and the honest position is to say so. Meursault in The Stranger forms almost no goals and the absence is the novel’s subject. Oblomov’s passivity is a critique of a class. Offred’s is imposed by a state. Bartleby’s refusal (Melville, 1853) is the founding text of the mode. Stevens’s is a life’s self-erasure. Clarissa Dalloway’s day contains one goal, and it concerns flowers.

The provenance argument of §3.6 applies with full force here. Agency is a value, and a historically and culturally specific one — the value of a mid-century American commercial fiction that assumed a protagonist could act on the world, and of the individualism that fiction took for granted. A great deal of the world’s fiction assumes otherwise, and describes conditions in which acting is not among the available options. The correct formulation is therefore narrow: in a book that runs on the chain, passivity breaks it. In a book that does not, passivity may be the argument, and the model’s job is to help the writer find out which book they are writing.

8.8 The Sequel Without a Decision

Added.

Symptom. Reaction and dilemma are fully delivered and then the sequence dissolves — a section break, a change of subject, and the next scene proceeds as though nothing had been pending.

Mechanism. Burke’s appetite is created and not satisfied (§3.4). The reader has been recruited into a weighing and then released without payment. This differs from the deferred decision (§6.2) in one respect only: nothing is coming.

Persistence. It is a characteristic fault of literary drafts, and it usually indicates that the writer does not yet know what the character decides. The draft is honest; that is why the beat is missing.

Remedy. Write the decision, however provisionally. A wrong decision that can be revised is more useful than an absent one, because it forces the following scene to have a goal.

The counterexample. The Handmaid’s Tale. Offred’s sequels routinely terminate in no decision, and the structure is the argument: a person whose options have been removed by a regime cannot complete the beat, and Atwood makes the missing terminus the reader’s experience of the book. Note how the novel pays for the omission — with curiosity in Sternberg’s sense (§6.1), supplied by the withheld past, and by a formal frame that promises the account will eventually be accounted for. A missing decision must be replaced by another engine, not merely omitted. §9.7.

8.9 The Escalation Treadmill

Added.

Symptom. Each disaster is bigger than the last — more danger, more damage, higher stakes — and the reader’s engagement declines.

Mechanism. Habituation. Magnitude is a relative quantity, and a reader recalibrates within a few iterations. What sustains attention is not increasing size but change in kind: the disaster that alters what the character can want, rather than the one that raises what they can lose. The treadmill is frequently a symptom of the missing hinge (§4.7): the plot escalates because the person is not moving.

Persistence. Escalation is the one structural instruction every writer has received, and it is easier to double a threat than to change a criterion.

Remedy. Test 8. Then replace one escalation with a disaster that costs the character something they wanted rather than something they had.

The counterexample. Catch-22 escalates by pure repetition — the number of missions required is raised, and raised, and raised — and the repetition is the joke and the indictment. Heller converts habituation into meaning: the reader’s numbness to each new increment is the numbness the novel is describing. The difference from the fault is that Heller knows the reader is habituating and has built the effect out of it.

8.10 The Deferred Decision Never Paid

Added.

Symptom. A sequel is interrupted before its decision; the narrative moves elsewhere; a later scene proceeds on the assumption that a choice was made, and it is never dramatised.

Mechanism. The deferral levies a tax on the reader’s desire (§6.1) and never delivers the service. Worse, it damages the reader’s model of the narrator: a text that opens a question and closes it off-page teaches the reader that its emphases are unreliable, which is expensive.

Persistence. In drafting, the decision often was made — in the writer’s head, during the gap — and the writer cannot see the omission.

Remedy. List every deferred decision in the manuscript and, for each, name the page on which it is paid. Any without a page number is a skipped sequel.

The counterexample. Hemingway’s “Hills Like White Elephants” (1927) is a complete sequel whose decision is withheld from the reader permanently, and it is the canonical instance of the technique. It works because the withholding is total and visible: nothing in the story pretends the decision was shown, and the reader’s uncertainty is the story’s final position rather than an oversight in it. The failure pattern is not the withheld decision. It is the withheld decision that the text later behaves as though it had supplied.

8.11 The Free Disaster

Added.

Symptom. The scene’s catastrophe arrives from outside the character’s pursuit — an accident, a coincidence, an unrelated third party.

Mechanism. Aristotle’s requirement (§3.1) that the reversal arise out of the action itself. A disaster the character’s own goal did not produce cannot be reacted to as consequence, only as misfortune, and misfortune generates pity rather than the specific engaged dread that drives a chain.

Persistence. It is the fastest way to make a scene end badly when the writer cannot find an organic cost.

Remedy. Ask whether the character’s pursuit created the conditions for the disaster. If not, either relocate the disaster or make the pursuit responsible for it.

The counterexample. Thomas Hardy built a career on it. The letter pushed under a door and beneath a carpet in Tess of the d’Urbervilles is the most notorious coincidence in Victorian fiction, and it is not a lapse: Hardy’s argument is that human intention is not the governing force in human outcomes, and a plot in which every disaster descended neatly from a goal would refute the thesis the novel exists to advance. Where a book’s subject is the insufficiency of agency, uncaused disaster is not a broken chain but a demonstrated one.

8.12 The Named Emotion

Added.

Symptom. The reaction beat consists of emotional vocabulary: she was devastated, he felt a wave of grief, a terrible anger rose in her.

Mechanism. A named emotion is a summary of the reaction beat, and summary cannot perform the beat’s function of conversion (§4.4). The reader is told a state obtains instead of being placed in it, and therefore does not accumulate the pressure the following decision needs.

Persistence. It is fast, and it is what the writer knows is true.

Remedy. Replace with what the body does and what the attention selects. As a mechanical exercise: forbid the twenty most common emotion words for one pass.

The counterexample. Great Expectations, and most retrospective first-person narration. Pip names his emotions constantly — his shame, his ingratitude, his cowardice — and the naming is the novel’s moral machinery, because it is performed by an older self passing judgement on a younger one. The prohibition applies to close third-person narration in which the naming has no speaker. Where a narrator is a person with a stake in the naming, naming is action.

8.13 Summary: symptom to mechanism

Pattern Underlying mechanism Beats damaged
Free goal Unsignalled shift on the causality index; no antecedent to find Goal, Decision (prior)
Clean win Narrative desire discharged; nothing left for the next goal Disaster

False dilemma Reader completes the weighing first; character diminished Dilemma
Stalled sequel Expansion without yield; repetition mistaken for depth Dilemma, Decision
