Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

4. The Six Beats — and a Proposed Seventh

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The six beats are a functional taxonomy. Each names a job, not a quantity of prose; the same beat may occupy four pages or four words, and a single sentence may discharge two beats at once. What the taxonomy provides is a set of separable questions with separable remedies, which is what converts the useless instruction make this chapter better into something a writer can act on.

Its characteristic misuse is as a composition template. Filled in beat by beat, it produces a recognisable species of competent, inert prose: the goal announced in the first paragraph, the conflict duly encountered, the disaster arriving on schedule, the reaction rendered in the approved physical vocabulary, the dilemma laid out in two balanced sentences, the decision closing the chapter. Everything is in place and nothing is alive. The reason is that the model describes the skeleton of a working scene, and a skeleton assembled first and clothed afterwards has the proportions of a diagram.

Two protections against this, both used throughout §§4.1–4.6.

  • Read the model backwards. Its proper use is on a completed draft, to find out what is already there and what is missing. Swain’s own book is a manual of composition; this chapter converts it into an instrument of revision, which is a change in kind.
  • Attend to the compressed cases. For each beat, §§4.1–4.6 ask not only what full delivery looks like but what the beat looks like compressed to nothing — because in accomplished fiction that is what it usually looks like, and a student who has only seen the expanded version will not recognise the beat when it is doing its work invisibly.

One further piece of Swain’s apparatus is worth carrying into the taxonomy, since it explains what the beats are made of. The motivation-reaction unit (MRU) is his sentence-level pattern: an external, objectively presented stimulus, followed by the viewpoint character’s response. Swain’s prescribed internal order for the response — feeling first, then involuntary reflex, then deliberate action, then speech — is too rigid to be adopted as a rule, and reads today as a period style. But the principle underneath it is sound and is the same principle as the chain: nothing in a narrative should arrive without a visible cause immediately preceding it, at whatever scale one is working. The MRU is the causal chain at the scale of the paragraph; the six beats are the same chain at the scale of the chapter; the plot is the same chain at the scale of the book.

Each of the six sections below follows the same pattern: definition · what it buys · textual carriers · discussion · under- and over-delivery · illustrations · compressed to nothing.

4.1 Goal

Definition. A concrete, immediate objective which the point-of-view character pursues within the unit, legible to the reader before the resistance begins, and specific enough that the reader can tell whether it has been achieved.

What it buys. A yardstick. Without a goal there is no measure against which any subsequent event counts as progress or setback, and therefore — per Barthes (§3.3) — no title under which the reader can file what happens. Tension is not produced by danger; it is produced by an outcome the reader can score. This is why a fight between two strangers is merely fast, and a conversation in which someone is trying not to cry is tense.

Textual carriers

Carrier Example of the form Note
Direct statement of intention She had come to get the key and she was not leaving without it. Cheapest, bluntest; suits commercial registers and first-person retrospect
A stated deadline or condition before he changed his mind Converts a goal into a goal with a clock, doubling its yield
An action already in progress A character who is knocking on a door has a goal The most economical form; see “compressed to nothing” below
A question the character asks Interrogation implies an objective Especially efficient in dialogue-led scenes; see Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange)
Descent from the prior decision The reader already knows what she resolved to do The strongest form: the goal arrives pre-motivated
Selected detail What the character notices is what the character is looking for Characterisation and goal in one operation; the multiplicity principle of Chapter 1

Discussion

The distinction that does the most work in revision is between the scene goal and the story goal. Find out what my father was hiding is a story goal; it cannot be pursued in a room. Get Dov to say why the locks were changed is a scene goal: it has an addressee, an occasion and a criterion of success. Drafts that feel unfocused very often have a perfectly clear story goal and no scene goals at all, and the resulting prose has the odd quality of being about something urgent while nothing urgent is happening.

The second distinction is between a goal and a wish. A goal is something the character is doing something about now. A wish is a state of affairs they would prefer. Wishes are not scene engines, and a draft in which the protagonist wishes for a great deal and pursues nothing is the passive-protagonist problem (§8.7) in its purest form.

A third point, easily missed: the goal need not be admirable, achievable, or even conscious to the character, but it must be legible to the reader. A character who is manifestly trying not to know something has a goal, and it is a good one.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the unstated goal. The scene has an engine the writer can name and the reader cannot. Diagnosed by the goal test (§7.1, test 1) and remedied not by adding a sentence of explanation but by giving the character an action whose object is visible.
  • Under, second form: the diffuse goal — “she wanted things to be better between them”. Nothing can count as failure, so nothing can count as disaster.
  • Over: the announced goal, in which a character states an objective the situation has already made obvious, usually in dialogue and usually to someone who does not need telling. The reader experiences this as being managed.
  • Over, second form: the over-motivated goal, where a paragraph of justification precedes a small objective. Motivation is the sequel’s job, and importing it into the goal beat is the commonest way of stalling a scene’s opening.

Illustrations

Herbert, Dune (1965). Paul’s goal in the gom jabbar scene is stated as a physical instruction and could not be more concrete: keep his hand inside the box while the Reverend Mother holds a poisoned needle at his neck. He must not withdraw. The scene’s entire tension derives from the fact that success and failure are visible from outside — the hand is in the box or it is not — while the experience being resisted is invisible. §9.4 analyses the full sequence.

Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment (1866). Raskolnikov’s goal at the opening of Part One is a masterclass in the goal held just below the surface: he is conducting what he calls a rehearsal, and the reader is permitted to know that an objective exists, and its rough shape, long before its content is named. The scene remains taut because the criterion is legible — he is finding out whether he can — even while the object is withheld.

Dickens, Great Expectations (1861). Pip’s goal in Chapter 1 is almost nothing: he is looking at his parents’ gravestones and trying to work out from the lettering what they were like. It is the smallest possible objective, and it is sufficient, because it is specific and because the convict’s arrival converts it instantly into a different one — survive this man — which is smaller still and completely legible.

Compressed to nothing

The goal beat compresses further than any other, because an action in progress implies its own objective. A character picking a lock has a goal; no sentence is required. Fiction of high accomplishment routinely opens units this way, and the apparent absence of a goal beat is in fact a goal beat carried by a verb.

The test for whether the compression has succeeded is simple: ask what the character would count as success in the next ninety seconds. If the reader could answer, the beat is present. If only the writer could, it is missing.

4.2 Conflict

Definition. The resistance the goal meets: an opposing person, a physical obstacle, a competing obligation, a rule, an absence, or the character’s own reluctance. Its job is to change the shape of the goal, not merely to postpone its achievement.

What it buys. Duration with meaning. Conflict is what allows a scene to occupy pages without the reader feeling that time is being filled, because each encounter with resistance yields information — about the obstacle, about the character, about the cost of continuing.

Textual carriers

Carrier Mechanism Characteristic weakness
An opposing person with their own goal The strongest form; both parties want something and only one can have it Becomes mechanical when the opponent exists only to obstruct
A competing obligation The character wants two incompatible things Frequently under-used; produces the best dilemmas later
A rule or institution Resistance without an antagonist Risks abstraction; needs a human face to be dramatised
A physical obstacle Immediate, legible, cheap Yields little character information unless the manner of overcoming it is characterising
An absence The needed person, object or information is not there Underrated; produces scenes of search, which are naturally goal-shaped
Internal reluctance The character resists their own goal The most sophisticated and the easiest to botch; see below

Discussion

The operative test, and the reason the standard account insists that conflict must “escalate or complicate, not merely delay”, is whether the resistance is informative. Three locked doors in succession are not conflict; they are one obstacle told three times. Three locked doors of which the first is locked, the second is locked from the inside, and the third has been recently oiled constitute an escalation, because each yields a new fact and each narrows the interpretation of the situation.

Internal conflict deserves particular care, because it is where literary and commercial pedagogy diverge most sharply. Swain’s model has room for it — he allows that the resistance may be the character’s own doubt — but the commercial tradition treats it as thin, and prefers resistance with a body. The literary tradition inverts this. Both are right about their own cases: an internal conflict is dramatically weak when it is not embodied in a decision the character must physically make, and dramatically overwhelming when it is. The reason Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day is not slack is that Stevens’s internal resistance is continuously converted into small external choices — whether to take the trip, whether to raise a subject, whether to answer a question — each of which is visible.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the cooperative obstacle, which yields at the first push. Common in drafts where the writer is more interested in the scene after this one.
  • Under, second form: conflict as delay. The character is prevented for four pages and then proceeds unchanged. Nothing was learned; the goal did not alter shape.
  • Over: conflict without traction, in which resistance is overwhelming from the outset and the character has no available move. This produces the experience of watching rather than following. A conflict must leave the character something to try.
  • Over, second form: escalation for its own sake — see the escalation treadmill, §8.9.

Illustrations

Brontë, Jane Eyre (1847). The interrupted wedding at Chapter 26 is a limit case of conflict arriving from outside the frame of the scene’s own struggle. Jane’s goal is to be married; the resistance is not Rochester, not the church, not her own doubt, but a solicitor and a stranger with a fact. The lesson is that resistance may enter the unit from a chain the reader has been following elsewhere — which is what makes the interruption feel both shocking and prepared (§9.2).

Dickens, Great Expectations. In Chapter 1 the resistance is total and immediate: a man who has the child by the throat. What keeps the scene from being merely a threat is that the convict wants something Pip can supply, so the obstacle instantly converts into a transaction, and Pip acquires a goal — get the file and the food — that he can pursue.

Dumas, The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46). Dantès’s conflicts in the first volume are exemplary because they are layered: he must escape a cell, then a fortress, then the sea, then obscurity, then the problem of what to do with a fortune. Each obstacle overcome reveals a larger one, and each revelation is delivered as a consequence of the solution to the last.

Compressed to nothing

Conflict compresses least well of the six beats, because it is the beat that supplies duration. A scene with a goal, no conflict, and a disaster reads as an accident rather than a struggle.

But there is one legitimate compression, and it needs naming because drafts use it accidentally rather than deliberately: the conflict may be located entirely in the reader’s knowledge. If the reader knows the man Elena is asking is the man who has already signed the papers, the resistance is present in every polite sentence, and no visible obstruction is needed. This is dramatic irony operating as conflict, and it is one of the most efficient devices available in fiction. It requires that the reader be ahead of the character, which is a structural decision made much earlier — see Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) on what a viewpoint arrangement buys.

4.3 Disaster

Definition. The unit’s terminal state: the character ends materially worse off than they began, or achieves the goal at a cost exceeding its value, or achieves it and discovers it was not the thing needed.

What it buys. The sequel. This is the beat’s entire structural rationale and the standard account is right to insist on it: without a disaster there is nothing for reaction, dilemma or decision to be about, and the following scene’s goal must be invented rather than inherited.

The three shapes

Shape Formula What it produces in the sequel Characteristic use
The costed success Yes, but… A dilemma about what the cost has made impossible The most useful of the three; sustains long books
The compounded failure No, and furthermore… A reaction of shock, and a dilemma with fewer options than before Thrillers, crime, the middle of most plots
The poisoned success Yes, and that is the problem A reaction of recognition; the dilemma is about the self Literary fiction; the anagnorisis case (§3.1)

The first form is worth dwelling on, because it is the one drafts most often miss. A scene may end with the character possessing what they came for, and still be a disaster, provided the acquisition has cost something that will be missed. Elena leaves the doorway with the key. The scene is a disaster because she has spent trust she did not know she was spending, and the sequel is about that (§9.9).

The third form is the literary tradition’s characteristic disaster and is almost absent from the commercial manual. Its structure is that the goal is achieved and revealed to have been the wrong goal — which does not damage the chain, because a decision beat still follows; it merely means the new goal is directed at a different object than the reader expected. This is the beat in which a novel changes its subject without changing its plot.

Textual carriers

Carrier Note
A refusal, a withholding, a silence The cheapest disaster available and often the strongest; the thing not said
A fact arriving late Anagnorisis; the reader and character learn together, or the reader learns first
A cost extracted Something is given up in the transaction — money, trust, an alibi, a relationship
A door closing An option that existed at the scene’s start no longer exists
A third party’s arrival Resistance entering from another chain, as at the Jane Eyre wedding

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the clean win (§8.2). The scene is a satisfaction, and the chain has nothing to hold onto.
  • Under, second form: the unregistered disaster, in which something costly happens and no character or narrator marks that it was costly. The reader cannot tell whether the scene has turned.
  • Over: the catastrophe inflation pattern, in which each unit ends with a disaster larger than the last until the reader habituates (§8.9). Magnitude is not the point; the change in the character’s option set is the point.
  • Over, second form: the free disaster (§8.11) — a calamity unconnected to the goal, arriving from outside. This is why the disaster must be, in Aristotle’s phrase, out of the action itself.

Illustrations

Brontë, Jane Eyre. Mason’s revelation is the compounded failure at maximum volume: not only is the marriage void, but a further fact about Rochester’s household and Jane’s own year in it becomes retrospectively legible. The disaster reaches backwards as well as forwards, which is the strongest form available.

Herbert, Dune. Paul passes the test. The disaster is that passing it means he is what the Bene Gesserit were looking for, and the news that follows — about his father, about Arrakis, about what is expected of him — makes the success a burden. This is the poisoned success in a commercial register, and it is the reason the scene is more than a set piece (§9.4).

Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939). The Joads’ successive arrivals — at the border, at the camps, at the peach farm — are a sequence of costed successes: work is found, and the finding is itself the trap. The intercalary chapters (§9.8) are where the cost is registered collectively.

Compressed to nothing

A disaster can compress to a single perceived detail, provided the reader can price it. The last line of a chapter which reports only that the coat had been brushed again since the last visit is a disaster if the reader knows what that implies. This is the technique the whole of Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) is concerned with, and it is why the two chapters are natural partners: the compressed disaster is nearly always carried by an object.

What a disaster cannot survive is compression to nothing at all — an ending in which neither the character’s position nor the reader’s knowledge has changed. That is the purposeless scene of Chapter 1, and no amount of beat vocabulary will save it.

4.4 Reaction

Definition. The character’s registration of the disaster: emotional, frequently physical, given room on the page proportionate to the disaster’s weight rather than to the writer’s habit.

What it buys. Three things, and they are usually taught as one.

  1. Permission. The reader takes their emotional cue from the text’s own pacing. A catastrophe passed over in a clause instructs the reader that it did not matter much.
  2. Characterisation under pressure. How a person reacts is more revealing than what they subsequently decide, because reaction is involuntary and decision is composed. This is the single largest source of character information in most novels.
  3. Conversion. Reaction is the beat in which an external event becomes an internal state, which is the only form in which it can motivate a decision. Skip it and the decision that follows is unmotivated, however logical it looks.

Textual carriers

Carrier Example of the form Risk
Physical involuntary response The unclenched hand; the walk taken without deciding to walk The most reliable; becomes cliché at the extremes (trembling, nausea)
Disordered perception The character notices the wrong things, or notices too precisely Excellent for shock; hard to sustain
Displacement activity The character does something irrelevant and does it thoroughly Extremely efficient — carries reaction and characterisation at once
A refused thought The narration circles the fact without naming it Powerful; one step from the withholding tease of Chapter 9
Named emotion She was furious Cheapest and weakest in close narration; legitimate in retrospective first person
Time distortion The passage slows, or an hour vanishes Genette’s territory (Chapter 5); the reaction beat is where duration is most often manipulated

Discussion

The strongest general rule available is that reaction is rendered by what the body does and what the attention selects, not by what the emotion is called. This is not a stylistic preference but a structural one: a named emotion is a summary of the beat, and a summary of a beat does not perform the beat’s function of conversion.

There is an important exception, and it is not marginal. In retrospective first-person narration, the naming of emotion is itself an act of the narrating self, and therefore characterising. Pip in Great Expectations names his shame, his ingratitude and his snobbery constantly and at length, and the naming is the book’s moral engine: the older Pip is doing to the younger Pip what the reader is doing. Any prohibition on named emotion must be qualified by asking who is doing the naming, and when.

The second rule concerns duration. The reaction beat is where the correspondence between page-weight and story-weight is most visible, and where drafts drift most reliably. §5 treats this as the principle of compression.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the skipped sequel (§8.3) in its commonest form — disaster, section break, new goal. The decision that follows will feel arbitrary because the reader has not been given the state from which it issues.
  • Under, second form: the stoic default, in which every character absorbs every blow with the same brief composure. This is a voice habit masquerading as characterisation.
  • Over: the wallow — reaction expanded past the point of new information. The test is not length but yield: a reaction beat may run four pages if each page tells the reader something they did not know about this person.
  • Over, second form: reaction inflation, in which a minor setback receives the physical vocabulary of a catastrophe, which spends the register the book will need later.

Illustrations

Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment. The fever after the murder is the reaction beat at its structural extreme: days of delirium, misrecognition, and a body that will not obey, occupying enough of the novel that it ceases to be a beat and becomes the book’s subject. §9.1 treats this at length; it is the chapter’s central case.

Atwood, The Handmaid’s Tale (1985). Offred’s narration is very largely reaction: the night sections in particular are a consciousness processing an event it cannot act on. Because the character’s capacity for decision has been removed by the regime, the sequel’s terminal beat is structurally unavailable — which makes the novel a demonstration that the missing beat can itself be the argument (§9.7).

Dickens, Great Expectations. Pip’s reaction to the convict is rendered almost entirely as disordered perception — the tilted world when he is turned upside down, the gibbet and the cattle on the walk home — and then as the physical business of the theft. The emotion is never named in the moment; it is named years later by the narrator, and the gap between the two namings is the novel.

Compressed to nothing

The reaction beat compresses more elegantly than any other, and the standard compression is a changed verb. The standard example — she didn’t wait to see if he’d follow — carries reaction, dilemma and decision in a single clause: the reaction is in didn’t wait (a refusal that implies feeling), the dilemma is in see if he’d follow (the option not taken), and the decision is in the leaving.

The requirement is that the reader must be able to reconstruct what was skipped. Compression works where the disaster’s weight is unambiguous; it fails where the reader is left uncertain whether the character noticed.

4.5 Dilemma

Definition. The weighing of two or more courses, each with a real cost, none obviously correct.

What it buys. Respect for the character’s intelligence, and — more importantly — the reader’s own participation. A dilemma is the only beat that invites the reader to arrive at a view before the character does, and a reader who has formed a preference is invested in the outcome in a way no amount of description can produce.

Textual carriers

Carrier Note
Explicit enumeration She could do X, or she could do Y. Blunt, clear, and standard in commercial registers
Free indirect argument The options argued in the character’s own idiom, without narratorial marking
Dialogue with a confidant Externalises the dilemma; risks becoming a briefing (Chapter 1’s expository scene in disguise)
Physical hesitation The character stopped in a doorway; the hand on the telephone
A list of what each option costs The strongest form: the costs, not the options, are what generate the dilemma
An unanswered question The dilemma stated as a question the character cannot answer

Discussion

The load-bearing requirement is symmetry of cost, and it is why the false dilemma (§8.4) is so common: writers construct two options, invest in one, and inadvertently signal which is correct. The remedy is to write the costs before the options. If the writer cannot state, in one sentence each, what the character loses by taking either path, the dilemma is not yet built.

A second requirement, less often noticed: the options must be available. A dilemma between two courses the character cannot actually take is not a dilemma but a lament. This is why the beat is structurally unavailable to characters in conditions of extreme constraint, and why novels about such conditions must find another engine (§9.7).

A third point, which §4.7 develops into the chapter’s own contribution: a dilemma is resolved by a criterion, not by a calculation. Two costly options are compared according to some rule about what matters more, and that rule is where the character actually lives. The six-beat model has a slot for the choice and no slot for the rule.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the false dilemma — one option is plainly correct, and the beat is a delay before the sensible thing.
  • Under, second form: the unpriced dilemma, in which two options are stated and neither cost is named.
  • Over: the stalled sequel (§8.5) — weighing extended past the point at which new considerations arrive, so that the beat repeats rather than develops.
  • Over, second form: the symmetrical essay, in which the two options are argued at equal length in balanced paragraphs. Real deliberation is lopsided: people spend most of their time arguing against the thing they are going to do.

Illustrations

Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884). Huck’s dilemma over whether to send the letter turning in Jim is the most instructive in American fiction, and it is analysed word by word at §5.2. Both options are catastrophic in Huck’s own terms: betray Jim, or be damned. Neither is available at a discount.

Brontë, Jane Eyre. Jane’s dilemma after the failed wedding is a genuine one and Brontë refuses to make it easy: staying is not merely a temptation but a defensible course, given her circumstances, and the novel gives Rochester the better arguments for several pages. That is what makes the departure cost something.

Dumas, The Count of Monte Cristo. The novel’s late dilemmas — whether the revenge should extend to the children of the guilty — are the reason the book outgrows its own machinery. Dantès’s engine has been running flawlessly for eight hundred pages; the dilemma that finally stops it is the one his method cannot price.

Compressed to nothing

A dilemma can compress into a single rejected option, mentioned and dismissed: She could have called their mother. One clause, and the reader reconstructs the whole weighing, because a rejected option implies both the alternative and the reason.

The failure mode of this compression is that it can render a dilemma invisible rather than compressed, so that the decision reads as the only thing the character could have done. Where the reader cannot name a road not taken, the decision has no weight, because a decision is defined by what it forecloses.

4.6 Decision

Definition. A commitment to a new, concrete objective, arising from the dilemma just weighed, and specific enough to function as the following scene’s goal.

What it buys. The chain. This beat is the join, and everything the chapter claims about causal necessity depends on it.

Textual carriers

Carrier Note
An action taken immediately The strongest: the decision and its first execution in one movement — She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead.
A stated intention with an object She would look at the ledger herself. Requires the object to be specific
A refusal Declining is a decision if the reader can see what was declined
A physical commitment Keys taken, a door locked, a number deleted; the body settles it before the mind announces it
A changed address or register The character speaks to someone differently from this point; the decision is legible in the diction
Deliberate withholding The decision made off-page and revealed by its consequences; see §6.4 on deferral

Discussion

Two properties distinguish a decision beat from a mere statement of intent.

Concreteness. She resolved to find out the truth is not a decision, because no scene can be built from it. She would open the register drawer is, because the next unit has somewhere to start. The practical test is whether the sentence names a place, an object or a person.

Irreversibility. The strongest decisions close a door. A decision the character could quietly abandon between chapters at no cost is not load-bearing, and the reversibility test (§7.1, test 9) is designed to catch this. The point is not that characters must be locked into their choices, but that a choice with no consequence for the option set is indistinguishable from a preference.

There is a third property that the model does not name and that §4.7 proposes to add: a decision may or may not be made on the same basis as the character’s previous decisions. Where the basis is unchanged, the decision advances the plot. Where the basis has changed, the decision advances the character. Both are decisions; only one is an arc.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the implied decision, in which the following scene reveals a choice that was never made on the page. This is the deferred decision never paid (§8.10).
  • Under, second form: the abstract decision, which cannot generate a goal.
  • Over: the declared decision, in which a character announces a resolution in dialogue, usually with a summary of their own reasoning. The beat’s work is done twice — once by the dilemma, once by the announcement.
  • Over, second form: the over-determined decision, arrived at with such thoroughness that the reader has been convinced twenty lines before the character. This produces impatience, which is the emotion a decision beat is least able to afford.

Illustrations

Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. “All right, then, I’ll go to hell.” A decision, a criterion change and an irreversible act, in seven words and a torn piece of paper (§5.2).

Brontë, Jane Eyre. Jane’s departure from Thornfield before dawn is a decision executed rather than announced. The famous statement of principle — that she cares for herself, and will respect herself the more the friendless she is — is not the decision; it is the criterion (§4.7) from which the decision follows.

Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment. The confession is the novel’s terminal decision, and it is delayed across five parts of accumulating reaction. §9.1 argues that this is not a violation of the model but its most extreme application: the decision beat is present, correctly formed, and postponed for six hundred pages, and the postponement is the book.

Compressed to nothing

A decision compresses into a verb of motion in the next sentence. She drove to the shop. Where the previous beats have been delivered, the decision needs no announcement at all, because arriving somewhere is deciding to go there.

The one thing that cannot be compressed away is the fact of the decision. A sequel that dissolves into the next scene without any commitment having been made is the pattern at §8.8, and it is a common structural fault in literary drafts — the fault that produces the reader’s report that a book is beautiful and static.

4.7 A Proposed Seventh: The Hinge

This section extends Swain’s six-beat model.

The six beats give an exact account of how a character’s goals change. They give no account at all of how a character’s grounds for choosing change — and that, not the sequence of objectives, is what readers mean by a character arc.

Consider the structure of a dilemma once more (§4.5). Two courses are available, each with a real cost. To resolve it, the character must apply some rule about which cost is the more bearable: loyalty over legality, safety over dignity, the family over the self, the truth over the peace. Call this rule the criterion. Most decisions in most novels are made on a criterion the character already held; the plot advances and the person does not. But at a small number of points in a well-made novel — sometimes only one — the dilemma is resolved by a criterion the character did not previously hold, or held and had never acted on. That moment is where arc happens.

The Hinge. The point within a sequel, between Dilemma and Decision, at which the character’s criterion for choosing changes. The options may be unchanged; what changes is the rule by which they are priced.

Why the six beats cannot name it

The model can register that the decision is surprising, and it can register that the goal which follows is a different kind of goal. It cannot say why, because it has no vocabulary for the standard against which options are weighed. In Swain’s terms a criterion change is invisible: the beats are all present and correctly ordered, and something enormous has happened inside the fifth one.

The practical consequence is a diagnosis the six beats cannot deliver. A manuscript may pass every test in §7.1 for four hundred pages — every goal descends, every scene ends worse, every dilemma is costly — and still produce the complaint that the protagonist “doesn’t change”. The chain is intact; the criterion never moves. The escalation treadmill (§8.9) is this condition: a perfectly working engine attached to a person who is not going anywhere.

Textual carriers

Carrier Form Note
A statement of value, not of intent I care for myself The criterion named directly; rare, and usually reserved for the book’s largest hinge
A previously held term used against its owner Huck applies “wicked” to himself and proceeds anyway The strongest form: the old criterion is not refuted; it is outranked
A refused comfort The character declines the argument that would let them off Legible without any statement of principle
A reversal of who is being protected The decision now serves a different person Very common in family fiction; carries an arc without a word of abstraction
A physical act that contradicts a stated belief The hand that does not sign Dramatises the criterion change without narrating it

Illustrations

Twain, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. Huck decides not by rejecting his moral training but by accepting its verdict and choosing damnation anyway. His criterion moves from what is right to what I will do for Jim, and he has no language for the second, which is why the sentence has to be a swear-word-adjacent blasphemy rather than an argument. See §5.2.

Brontë, Jane Eyre. Rochester’s case for staying is strong and the novel lets it be strong. Jane’s answer is not a better argument but a different standard: self-respect is admitted as the term that outranks love, safety and even usefulness. The decision to leave follows in a line.

Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813). Elizabeth reading Darcy’s letter is the cleanest hinge in English fiction, because Austen puts the criterion change into the sentence itself: until that moment, Elizabeth reports, she had never known herself. What changes is not what she wants but what she now regards as evidence.

Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment. The novel is a five-part search for a hinge that will not arrive. Raskolnikov’s dilemma is stable and his criterion is stable; the entire enormous sequel is the slow demonstration that the criterion cannot survive contact with his own body. §9.1.

The argument against admitting it

The case for a seventh beat is not decisive, and students should be given the objections in their strongest form.

  1. It is not a beat but a property of one. A hinge is a quality that the Decision beat may or may not possess. Taxonomies should not multiply units to describe qualities of existing units, and a model whose virtue is that it fits on a postcard is damaged by an addition that fits nowhere.
  2. It is unobservable. The other six beats are identifiable from the page: a goal is stated or not, a disaster occurs or not. A criterion is inferred, and inferred by the critic. A test that asks a writer to locate their protagonist’s criterion change will return whatever the writer wishes to find.
  3. It imports arc theory into a scene-level model. Swain’s model is deliberately local: it describes the join between two units. Character arc is a whole-book structure, and the correct place to treat it is at that scale — in a chapter on architecture, not in a chapter on the chain.
  4. Most sequels have no hinge and are none the worse. If the addition applies to two moments in a four-hundred-page novel, it is not a beat; it is an event.

The response this chapter offers is partial. Objections 1 and 3 are conceded: the Hinge is best understood not as a seventh beat of equal standing but as a structural qualification — a distinction between two kinds of decision, one plot-bearing and one person-bearing. Objection 2 is answered by the criterion test (§7.1, test 8), which asks not where is the criterion change but the falsifiable question at which decision would the character’s earlier self have chosen differently, and why. Objection 4 is conceded and reframed: the beat’s rarity is the point. A novel needs one, perhaps three. What it must not have is none.

Ruling in the second edition. Chapter 12, Arc and Theme (section 3.1) adopts the hinge at the scale of the book, as the point at which an arc is located, and rejects it as a seventh beat, on objections 1, 3 and 4. It also qualifies the last sentence above: a book whose protagonist has a steadfast or a refused arc has no hinge by design, and the criterion test then asks whether the steadfastness or the refusal is itself chosen under pressure. Objection 2 is conceded for arcs of erosion, in which no single decision is the turn, such as Lydgate’s in Middlemarch.

Seminar question. Is the Hinge a seventh beat, a property of the Decision beat, or a category error — arc theory smuggled into a model of the join between scenes? Argue the case using Jane Eyre, Crime and Punishment and one novel of your own choice in which no criterion ever changes. (§12.2, question 2.)

4.9 Must all six be served at once?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

  1. The beats are functions distributed across units, not slots in a unit. A scene may end in disaster with the reaction supplied three chapters later, by a different character, in a different register. The chain is a property of the sequence, not of any member of it.
  2. A pair may be split across viewpoints. The disaster happens to one character and is registered by another. §6.12 treats this as a chain structure in its own right; Mrs Dalloway is its finest instance.
  3. Some beats are structurally unavailable in some books. A protagonist in conditions of total constraint cannot have a decision beat, because deciding requires options. This is a real limit of the model and a real subject for fiction (§9.7).
  4. The proportions are genre-inflected, and the genre is the choice. A thriller compresses the sequel and expands the scene; a novel of consciousness inverts the ratio. Neither ratio is more correct; the ratio is the register. A book that cannot decide which it is produces the commonest tonal fault in ambitious drafts — catastrophes given a clause and inconveniences given a chapter.

The correct question in revision is therefore never is a beat missing? but is the absence deliberate, and does the reader have what they need to bridge it?
