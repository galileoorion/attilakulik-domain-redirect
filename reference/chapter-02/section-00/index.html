Scene and Sequel

The Six-Beat Engine, the Causal Chain, and the Limits of a Mid-Century Model

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 2. Part I: The Scene and Its Materials.

A companion to Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene), whose note on the sequel (§6.6) this chapter expands, and to Chapter 5 (The Transition), Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break), Chapter 14 (The Subplot) and Chapter 16 (The Ending).

Chapter Abstract

Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) treated the scene as a unit of purpose and asked what each one is for. This chapter treats the join between units and asks a different question: what makes one unit follow another necessarily rather than merely next.

Its spine is a six-beat model inherited from Dwight V. Swain’s Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965): a scene built from Goal, Conflict, Disaster, followed by a sequel built from Reaction, Dilemma, Decision, whose Decision becomes the following scene’s Goal. The model is compact, teachable and unusually durable. Swain codified it in a mid-century American writing manual informed by his experience in commercial fiction, and later trade manuals helped transmit it. The chapter presents it as what it is: a heuristic with a specific provenance, real explanatory power, and limits that exclude a great deal of admired fiction.

The argument proceeds in four movements.

  1. Theory (§3) — the grounding of the chapter’s first principle in Aristotle’s distinction between events that follow one another and events that follow from one another; in Peter Brooks’s account of narrative desire as a dynamic of delay and discharge; in Barthes’s proairetic code, Todorov’s equilibrium schema and Propp’s functions; in the discourse-psychology finding that causally connected events are better recalled than merely sequential ones; and in the institutional history of the model itself.
  2. Taxonomy (§4) — the six beats anatomised individually, each with its textual carriers, its under- and over-delivery, its published illustrations, and — a question the usual account leaves open — what it looks like when compressed to nothing. A seventh unit is then proposed: the Hinge, the point at which a character’s criterion for choosing changes, which is where arc actually happens and which the six-beat model cannot name.
  3. Method (§§5–7) — the principle of compression; a taxonomy of twelve chain structures, from the clean chain to the chain deliberately broken; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol for revision.
  4. Application (§§8–9) — twelve failure patterns, each paired with a published work that commits the fault and succeeds; eight close readings; and a revision case worked on the textbook’s running example.

The chapter’s governing claim is this:

A story is not a sequence of things that happened. It is a chain of things that had to happen next — and “had to” is a relation the reader constructs, not a property the text possesses.

The second half of that sentence is the chapter’s contribution and its principal qualification. Causal necessity is an effect; it can be produced by beat structure, and it can be produced by other means, and it can be deliberately refused. A model that treats it as a checklist has mistaken one route for the destination.

A note on the examples. The illustrative passages are drawn chiefly from the European and anglophone novel; the Core chapter adds post-2000 fiction and screen cases, listed in §14.5. A concordance mapping each concept to the novel that best displays it appears at §10.6. Where a work outside those lists demonstrates something they cannot, it is used and marked.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Distinguish scene from sequel in Swain’s narrow sense, and both from the loose sense of “scene” used in Chapter 1 and in most workshop conversation.
  • Identify each of the six beats in a published passage, name its textual carriers, and say what the passage buys by expanding or compressing it.
  • Explain, with reference to at least three theoretical sources, why causal connection between narrative units produces effects that mere sequence does not — and where that account comes from historically.
  • Trace a causal chain backwards through several scene–sequel pairs and locate the exact link at which necessity fails.
  • Apply the ten-test diagnostic protocol to a draft and produce a prioritised revision plan.
  • Diagnose the recurring failure patterns by mechanism rather than by surface feature, and state the conditions under which each “failure” is the correct choice.
  • Evaluate the six-beat model’s provenance and limits, including the extent to which its prohibitions describe narrative and the extent to which they describe a commercial genre preference.
  • Analyse at least one novel that runs on a different engine entirely, and account for how it generates momentum without the chain.

How to Use This Chapter

Reader Route Purpose
Student in a seminar §§1–4 in order, then §6 and §9; §12.2 for discussion Conceptual grounding, then demonstration on real texts
Writer in revision §7 (tests) → §8 (failures) → §5.5 (the beat-weight audit) → §11 (checklist) Fast triage of a live draft, in that order
Teacher or workshop leader §4, 6, 8, 9 for material; §12.1 for assignments; §12.3 for the rubric Ready-made teaching sequence with worked models and six seminar questions

A caution before beginning is more necessary in this chapter than in most. The six-beat model is the most mechanisable instrument in this textbook, because it alone tells a writer what to write next. It can be turned into a template and filled in; drafts produced that way have a recognisable flatness: correct in structure, dead on the page. The model’s proper use is retrospective. It describes what a working chain looks like from the outside; it does not tell a writer what should happen next, and a writer who asks it to will get a plausible answer and a mediocre book.
