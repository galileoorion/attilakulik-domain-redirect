Chapter 2 · Scene and Sequel

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

The vocabulary of this chapter is unusually treacherous, for a specific reason: two of its central terms — scene and sequel — already have widely used meanings that are not the ones intended here. A student who does not distinguish the senses early may conflate them throughout §§4–9.

2.1 Working definitions

Scene (narrow sense). A unit of dramatised action in which a point-of-view character pursues a concrete, immediate goal, meets conflict, and ends in disaster — a state materially worse than the one in which the unit began, or a success whose cost exceeds its value.

Sequel. The unit that follows a scene, in which the same character registers the disaster emotionally (reaction), weighs a set of options none of which is good (dilemma), and commits to a new concrete objective (decision) — which becomes the following scene’s goal.

Four elements of those definitions carry weight.

  • “Point-of-view character.” The model is focalised. It describes the experience of one consciousness at a time, which is why multi-viewpoint novels run several chains in parallel (§6.5) and why the model has little to say about narration that is not anchored in a person (§8.7).
  • “Concrete, immediate.” The goal is scene-sized, not book-sized. Get the key back before he changes his mind is a goal; understand my father is a theme.
  • “Materially worse.” The disaster is defined by its structural function — it must generate a sequel — and not by its magnitude. A disaster can be a raised eyebrow.
  • “Becomes the following scene’s goal.” This is the load-bearing clause and the whole reason the model exists. Without it, the six beats are a description of one unit; with it, they are an account of how units connect.

2.2 Scale and extent

Neither half has a characteristic length, and one of the more damaging misreadings of the model is that a sequel is a chapter type.

Unit Shortest observed Longest observed Note
Scene A sentence A hundred pages (the Waterloo chapters of Vanity Fair; the ball in Anna Karenina extended across several chapters) Length tracks the density of resistance, not the importance of the goal
Sequel A single clause — she did not wait to see whether he would follow Five of the six parts of Crime and Punishment See §9.1
A complete pair A paragraph A volume Serial fiction often runs one pair per instalment

Two consequences follow. First, the beats are functions, not paragraphs: a beat is a job done, and the job may be done in a subordinate clause. Second, the model does not predict chapter architecture. A chapter may contain three complete pairs, half a pair, or a sequel alone. Where the breaks fall is Chapter 6’s subject, and the two decisions — where the beats fall, where the breaks fall — are independent variables that a great many drafts confuse.

2.3 The term most often confused: “scene”

The word “scene” is used in at least four distinct senses in ordinary craft conversation, and the differences matter.

Sense Meaning Used in
Loose / workshop Any continuous stretch of dramatised action, rendered moment by moment Chapter 1, and most of this series
Narrow / Swain Specifically the goal–conflict–disaster half of the pair This chapter, §§4–9
Genettean A relation of duration — narrative time approximately equal to story time, as against summary, ellipsis and pause Chapter 5 (The Transition)
Dramaturgical A division of an act, marked by entrance and exit Screen and stage practice; §10.4

This chapter uses the narrow sense throughout and signals the loose sense explicitly where it is needed. The Genettean sense is the one to keep most carefully separate, because it is a claim about rate rather than about function: a Swain scene may contain summary, and a Genettean scene may contain no goal at all.

A second, cruder confusion is worth disposing of at once. “Sequel” here has nothing to do with a follow-up volume. Swain’s usage is idiosyncratic and, in retrospect, unfortunate; some later manuals substitute “aftermath” or “reflective unit”. This chapter retains “sequel” because it is the term in which the model is transmitted and because the alternatives are worse — “aftermath” implies passivity, and the sequel’s job is to produce a decision.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to this chapter’s units
Beat Smallest unit of dramatic change; in screen usage, an exchange in which the transaction between characters turns A Swain beat is one of six named functions, which is narrower and more specific
Motivation-reaction unit (MRU) Swain’s sentence-level unit: an external motivating stimulus, then the character’s response in the fixed order feeling, action, speech The same causal principle at paragraph scale; see §4.0
Kernel / satellite Chatman’s distinction between events that open alternatives and events that elaborate without branching A disaster is nearly always a kernel; reaction is usually satellite material
Function Propp’s unit: an act defined by its significance for the course of the action An earlier analogue of a “beat”; see §3.4
Proairetic code Barthes’s code of actions — the reader’s naming of a sequence in progress The reader-side description of what a scene is; §3.3
Peripeteia Aristotle’s reversal: a change of fortune arising from the action itself The classical ancestor of “disaster”; §3.1
Turning point Loose critical term for any structurally consequential moment Overlaps disaster and decision without distinguishing them
Obligatory scene The confrontation a plot has promised and must deliver A scene the chain has made necessary far in advance; see Chapter 16
Dénouement The unwinding after the climax Structurally, an extended terminal sequel; §6.9
Dilatory space Brooks’s term for the middle of a narrative, which resists the end it desires The theoretical form of the deferral techniques in §6

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • What a scene is for — the value shift, the five domains and the principle of multiplicity are the subject of Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene), and are used here as established equipment.
  • How the gap between units is crossed — Chapter 5 (The Transition).
  • Where the gaps are placed, and what a break does to a deferred decision — Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break).
  • Whose chain the reader is following after a shift — Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift).
  • Chains that run in the past — analepsis, and the cost of interrupting a chain to visit its antecedents, are Chapter 7’s (Backstory and Flashback).
  • Parallel and subordinate chains — Chapter 14 (The Subplot). Braiding is treated here only as a deferral device (§6.5); how a second chain earns its page space is Chapter 14’s question.
  • The chain’s two boundaries — Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) and Chapter 16 (The Ending).
