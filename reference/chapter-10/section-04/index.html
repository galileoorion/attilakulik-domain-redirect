Chapter 10 · The Prologue

4. The Domains of Purpose

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) sets out four domains a scene may serve — plot, character, theme and texture — and proposes a fifth, the relational domain, which Chapter 3 adopts. Later chapters adapt the list to their unit. The prologue’s version keeps plot, character and theme, puts world (as Chapter 4 uses it) in place of texture, audits relations under character, since a prologue usually precedes the relations the book follows, and adds a fifth of its own: the reader contract. This domain is unique to the prologue’s position: it concerns the deal struck with the reader before the story proper has begun, which no other unit is in a position to strike.

The model’s value is diagnostic. It converts the unanswerable instruction make your prologue better into a set of separable questions with separable remedies, and — more importantly for this chapter than for any other — it converts the higher bar from an intuition into an audit. A writer who can name the domains their prologue serves can usually also name what the separation buys, because the domains are stated in terms of what distance makes possible. Each entry below is not a job a prologue may do but a job that being outside the main sequence makes available.

The model’s danger is mechanical application, in two specific forms:

  • The checklist prologue, which visibly discharges obligations rather than telling anything, and which is the prologue-specific form of Chapter 9’s overstuffed first page.
  • The retrospective justification, in which a writer who wants to keep a prologue for reasons of affection constructs a domain analysis after the fact. The audit only works if it is run honestly, which in practice means running it on someone else’s draft first.

Five domains are set out below, followed at §4.6 by a proposed sixth. For each: what it is, why the separation makes it possible, its textual carriers, discussion, under- and over-delivery, and illustrations from published fiction.

4.1 Plot — What Only Distance Permits

What the separation makes possible: the delivery of events the main narrative’s own sequence cannot reach — because they happened too early, too far away, or to someone the protagonist will never meet.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
The frame of an ending Where is all this going? A flash-forward; a retrospective narrator’s present tense; an outcome stated as known
The unwitnessable incident How did this start, if nobody saw it? A viewpoint nobody in the main narrative holds; an event with no surviving witness
The running clock What is already in motion? A threat, a decision, a journey begun before Chapter One’s first page
Compressed antecedent How far back does this go? Summary at a scale no scene can occupy — generations, centuries, geological time

Discussion

The four carriers are not variations on one another; they solve different structural problems, and confusing them is the source of a good deal of muddled drafting.

The frame of an ending trades suspense for curiosity, in Sternberg’s exact sense (§3.7). It is the most powerful and the most dangerous of the four, because it changes the reader’s relation to the entire book, and it cannot be undone.

The unwitnessable incident is the plot domain’s least contestable justification, and the one that most cleanly clears the higher bar. If an event is causally necessary to the book and no character in the main narrative could have witnessed it, the writer’s options are: report it secondhand later (which distances it and delays it), invent a witness (which distorts the cast), reveal it in a document (which requires the document to exist), or give it its own compartment before the story starts. The fourth option is what a prologue is for. This is the household example’s justification (§9.9): the father’s last ordinary day is an event none of his children could have seen, and the book is about their reconstruction of it.

The running clock is the off-page threat: something dangerous already moving, of which the protagonist is unaware. Its purchase is dramatic irony as a continuous background condition rather than as a local effect.

Compressed antecedent is the carrier most likely to fail, because it is the one nearest to pure exposition (§8.1). The test is whether the compression requires the exterior position. Generational history that a grandmother could plausibly tell in the novel’s twelfth chapter does not require a prologue; it requires that chapter.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the prologue that gestures at plot without committing — a vague portent, an unnamed figure watching, “something was coming”. Nothing has been delivered that the main narrative could not deliver better, so the higher bar is failed without the material even clearing the first test.
  • Over: the prologue that delivers so much plot that Chapter One begins in an anticlimax. If the prologue contains the book’s most dramatic event, everything after it is descent. This is the structural cause of failure pattern §8.2, and it is why “start with your most exciting scene” is bad advice for a prologue specifically.

Illustrations

1. Romeo and Juliet (c. 1595) — the ending framed at maximum volume. The sonnet-prologue supplies the setting, the feud, the lovers’ deaths and the reconciliation, in fourteen lines, before a word of the action. The plot domain is served not by withholding but by disclosure, and the whole play’s irony is bought with it. §8.6 argues that this single instance is sufficient to demolish “the prologue that spoils” as a general prohibition.

2. Frankenstein (1818) — the unwitnessable, twice. Walton’s letters deliver an event no participant in Victor’s story could report: a stranger’s account of finding Victor, near death, on the ice, pursuing something across a frozen sea. The letters also, in the ordinary sense, frame the ending, since the reader meets Victor at the terminus of his life before hearing how he arrived there. Full analysis at §9.1.

3. Rebecca (1938) — the running clock as a completed catastrophe. The novel’s opening chapters, functionally a prologue though not labelled one, place the narrator in exile with her husband after unnamed events, dreaming of a house that no longer stands. What is established is not a threat but its aftermath, which converts the whole main narrative into a countdown to a disaster the reader already knows occurred. It is the plot domain and the reader-contract domain served by the same page.

4.2 Character — The Absent Vantage

What the separation makes possible: occupancy of a consciousness the main narrative cannot enter — because that character is dead, absent, unknown to the protagonist, or structurally barred from holding viewpoint.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
Early access Whose head is this, and why won’t I be back? A viewpoint that appears once, distinguished by diction and by what it notices
Dramatic irony installed What do I know that they don’t? Knowledge given to the reader and withheld from the main cast
The seeded origin Why is this person like this? A formative scene the protagonist will never learn firsthand
The retrospective self Who is telling me this, and from where? A narrator situated after the events, whose distance is itself characterising

Discussion

Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) establishes that moving a reader between consciousnesses costs something — an empathy budget that is finite and that resists reallocation. A prologue spends that budget in advance, before the reader has attached to anyone, and often on a person they will not occupy again. This is a real expenditure and it produces the character domain’s characteristic risk: a reader who forms an attachment in the prologue and then finds themselves ejected into Chapter One with a different protagonist may experience Chapter One as a demotion.

The remedies are two, and they are not equivalent.

  • Make the prologue viewpoint plainly not the protagonist. A voice sufficiently unlike the main narrative’s — older, remoter, more formal, a chorus, a document — signals its own provisionality, and the reader does not attach.
  • Make the ejection the point. The father in the household example is a person the reader is supposed to lose, because the book is about a family losing him. Here the attachment and the ejection are the material, and the structure enacts the subject.

The second is much the stronger move, and it generalises: when the prologue’s viewpoint is one the book is about not having, the separation justifies itself without further argument.

Dramatic irony installed deserves a caution. Giving the reader knowledge the protagonist lacks is a powerful instrument, but it has a specific cost: it converts the protagonist’s discovery scenes from revelations into confirmations. A book whose pleasures depend on the reader learning with the protagonist should not have a prologue in this mode. A book whose pleasures depend on watching a person walk towards something the reader can see should.

The retrospective self is the modernist frame’s characteristic move, and it is the character carrier that most reliably clears the higher bar, because the vantage is by definition unavailable to the main narrative: the narrator of the prologue knows how it ends and the narrator of Chapter One does not, even when they are the same person.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the prologue viewpoint who is a function rather than a person — a nameless watcher, a soldier who exists to see a battle. The reader is given a camera position and told it is a character. Failure pattern §8.7.
  • Over: the prologue viewpoint who is so vivid that Chapter One’s protagonist cannot compete. This is a real and underdiagnosed problem, and its remedy is usually not to weaken the prologue but to ask whether the book has chosen the wrong protagonist.

Illustrations

1. Invisible Man (1952) — the retrospective self, declared. Ellison’s labelled Prologue places the narrator underground, in a cellar lit by more than a thousand filament bulbs stolen from the power company, addressing the reader from a position of total retrospection; Chapter One then goes back to his grandfather’s deathbed and the humiliations of his youth. The Prologue’s function is to install the voice that knows, so that the naïveté of the young man in the main narrative is legible as naïveté from the first page. The novel closes with a matching Epilogue: distance opened and totally closed (§6.5).

2. Wuthering Heights (1847) — the vantage that is wrong on purpose. Lockwood’s opening chapters give the reader a consciousness so comprehensively unequal to what it is observing that the reader’s dawning sense of his inadequacy becomes the novel’s first structural event. Full analysis at §9.2.

3. The household worked example (§9.9) — the vantage that is lost. The father’s own interior on his last ordinary day is a place no later chapter can revisit, because the book’s subject is that his children cannot revisit it. The prologue gives the reader something the characters spend the novel failing to reconstruct, and the resulting asymmetry recolours every subsequent domestic scene.

4.3 World — The Register Shift

What the separation makes possible: a mode of address the main narrative cannot sustain — mythic, historical, choric, documentary, panoramic — used to establish scale, rules or texture before the book narrows to one person’s view.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
Register declaration What kind of world is this, and at what pitch? Diction, syntax, sentence length; the presence or absence of a personal viewpoint
Lore at a distance Where did all this come from? Historical or legendary summary framed as inherited telling
Scale How big is this? The size of the entities the prologue treats as ordinary: nations, centuries, gods
The social panorama Who lives here? A cross-section rather than an individual

Discussion

The world domain is the prologue’s oldest and most abused justification. Abused, because “the main narrative can’t carry this exposition” is the excuse that licenses the info-dump (§8.1); oldest, because the prologos and the epic proem exist for this.

The discrimination that matters is between register and information. A register shift is a change in how the book speaks. An info-dump is a quantity of what it says. A prologue that is actually doing world-domain work will read differently from Chapter One at sentence level — its rhythms, its pronouns, its relation to time will differ — and will not merely contain more facts.

The test is a substitution: take the prologue’s content and rewrite it in Chapter One’s voice. If it survives with nothing lost but length, the material was information and belongs inside. If it becomes flat, wrong, or impossible — because Chapter One’s close third person cannot say “for three hundred years”, or because a novel narrated by a fourteen-year-old cannot adopt the tone of a chronicle — the register was doing the work, and the separation is earned.

Lore at a distance works by a mechanism that needs naming: historical or legendary framing converts exposition from the author telling the reader into the culture telling itself. The information is identical; the speaker is not. The reader’s tolerance for the second is far higher, because it is characterising rather than instructional.

Scale is the world domain’s most efficient carrier and the least often used deliberately. A prologue that treats a century as a unit teaches the reader, before any person appears, that this book’s frame of reference is larger than a life — which then makes the narrowing to one person in Chapter One feel like a choice rather than a limitation.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the decorative register shift — heightened language with nothing behind it, portentous syntax used to make ordinary material feel important. The reader detects it as tone without content.
  • Over: the register the book never returns to and cannot support. A prologue in the high mythic mode attached to a novel of contemporary realism does not establish the world; it establishes a different world, and the primacy effect (§3.9) then makes the actual novel read as a falling-off. This is the world domain’s version of false advertising.

Illustrations

1. The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue (c. 1387) — the social panorama. The pilgrims are introduced in sequence, by estate, before any tale begins. The prologue is not about the tales; it is about the world that produces them, and it establishes a scale (a whole society), a method (portraiture by significant detail), and a contract (these people will be judged by how they tell). It remains the most complete demonstration in English of world-domain prologue work.

2. The Scarlet Letter, “The Custom-House” (1850) — the register licence. Hawthorne’s introductory essay contains the celebrated passage theorising a neutral territory somewhere between the actual world and fairy-land, where the real and the imaginary may meet. The essay is thereby doing something no chapter of the romance itself could do: it establishes the mode in which the following narrative is to be taken, and it does so in a register — familiar, essayistic, satirical — that the romance categorically excludes. Full analysis at §9.3.

3. Tolkien, “Concerning Hobbits” (1954) — lore at a distance, at its most exposed. The prologue to The Lord of the Rings is unashamed exposition: hobbit ethnography, pipe-weed, the ordering of the Shire, a note on the sources. It is the pattern-case for everything §8.1 objects to, and it succeeds for its readers because the exposition is characterised — it has a voice, an antiquarian’s affection, a pretence of scholarship — and because the book that follows sustains that voice’s premise. It is also, unmistakably, a prologue many readers skip, which is the honest cost.

4.4 Theme — The Promise

What the separation makes possible: a statement of the book’s central image, question or proposition, in distilled form, before the narrative begins complicating it.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
The distilled proposition What is this book about, at the level of idea? Aphorism; a general statement in the present tense; a figure offered as a type
The governing image What will I be seeing again? A concrete object or scene given disproportionate attention
The frame to be completed What shape will this take? A statement or scene the ending will answer, invert or repeat
The question posed What am I being asked to decide? An explicit interrogative, or a case presented without verdict

Discussion

The theme domain is where the prologue is least like an ordinary scene and most like an epigraph — and the boundary between them is the subject of failure pattern §8.11. The discriminating question is whether the material is narrative (something happens, or someone is described in a way that constitutes an event) or assertion (a proposition offered for assent). A prologue may make assertions; a prologue that is only assertion is an epigraph with a longer word count.

The theme domain’s characteristic strength is that it is the cheapest domain to serve well and the most expensive to serve badly. A single well-chosen figure costs two hundred words and pays out across four hundred pages. A poorly chosen one announces the book’s meaning in advance and thereby forecloses it — the reader spends the novel checking the thesis rather than discovering it.

Two disciplines follow.

  1. State the question, not the answer. A thematic prologue that says this is a book about how families keep secrets has told the reader what to conclude. One that shows a man deciding not to say something has posed a question. Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) makes the same distinction in terms of denotation and connotation: the object is denoted, the meaning is left to accrue.
  2. Choose an image you are willing to repeat. Per Rabinowitz (§3.9), the thematic prologue’s image is the most heavily marked object in the book. If it recurs three times it becomes a motif; if it recurs once it becomes a decoration; if it never recurs the reader concludes the book forgot it.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the thematic prologue whose figure is too general to be recognised again. Water, the road, time: these cannot be redeemed because they cannot be identified when they return.
  • Over: the thematic prologue that argues. When the prologue explains what its image means, the novel that follows has nothing left to do but illustrate.

Illustrations

1. Middlemarch, the “Prelude” (1871–72) — the type and the question. Eliot opens with Saint Theresa of Ávila, a child setting out to seek martyrdom, and then asks what becomes of later-born Theresas who find no coherent social faith to give their ardour shape. No character from the novel appears. The Prelude states the book’s question in the interrogative, supplies a figure the “Finale” will return to, and establishes the narrator’s habit of moving between the individual case and the general law. It is a thematic prologue of unusual economy, three paragraphs long.

2. Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937) — the image and its gendered turn. Hurston’s opening — ships at a distance carrying every man’s wish, some coming in with the tide and some sailing forever on the horizon — is a proposition about desire and time, immediately followed by its counter-proposition for women, whose relation to the dream is stated as different in kind. The overture is not labelled a prologue, which is what makes it a test case (§9.5), but it performs every function in this table.

3. Anna Karenina (1878) — the limit case. The novel’s epigraph, “Vengeance is mine; I will repay”, is a thematic threshold consisting of six words of quoted scripture in English (five in Tolstoy’s Russian). It frames the whole book, has been argued over for a century and a half, and is not a prologue. It is the clearest available demonstration of where the theme domain stops being a prologue’s business and becomes an epigraph’s (§8.11).

4.5 The Reader Contract — Hook, Patience and Protocol

What the separation makes possible: an explicit negotiation with the reader about what kind of book this is and how it should be read, conducted before the main narrative can complicate the message.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
Tone declaration What kind of book am I in? Register, subject, the narrator’s stance towards suffering
Patience purchased Why should I stay through a quiet opening? Tension, wonder, dread or violence held in reserve behind Chapter One
Felt stakes What is underneath this? A danger the main narrative will not name for some time
Reading protocol How am I supposed to take this? Instructions, explicit or implied, about the status of what follows

Discussion

The reader contract is the domain unique to the prologue’s position, and it is the one most often served alone — which is the shape readers, agents and editors have learned to distrust on sight. A prologue that only hooks is a prologue that could as easily be an unconnected short story stapled to the front of the book. §5 makes the argument in full.

But the domain is not disreputable; it is only over-used. Three of its four carriers do work nothing else can do.

Patience purchased is the most defensible and the least discussed. A novel with a deliberately quiet opening — a domestic novel, a slow literary novel, a book whose engine takes sixty pages to start — has a real structural problem, and one legitimate solution is to establish, before Chapter One, that something is coming. The prologue is not there to be exciting. It is there so that Chapter One can afford not to be. The instrument is Kermode’s (§3.8): a promise of shape licenses a reader to wait.

Felt stakes is the same purchase in the emotional rather than the structural register: the reader carries a dread the characters do not have, and every ordinary scene is coloured by it. The household example (§9.9) works almost entirely in this mode.

Reading protocol is the carrier the craft literature almost never names, and it is the bridge to §4.6. When Dracula’s unsigned note explains that the papers have been placed in sequence and that all needless matters have been eliminated, it is not establishing tone or stakes. It is telling the reader what kind of object they are holding and how to process it. That is a distinct job, and the fact that the five-domain model can only file it under “tone” is the strongest argument for a sixth domain.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the prologue that hooks and does nothing else — the isolated exciting scene. §8.10.
  • Over: the prologue that over-promises. Every increment of tension in a prologue is a debt against the main narrative’s actual pitch, and the interest rate is set by the primacy effect (§3.9). This is failure pattern §8.2, the chapter’s most expensive error.

Illustrations

1. Henry V, the Chorus (1599) — protocol, declared. The opening Chorus does not narrate. It instructs: imagine what the stage cannot show, divide one man into a thousand, let the actors work upon your imaginary forces. The audience is told, before anything happens, what kind of participation is required of them. Nothing in a novel’s Chapter One can do this without breaking its own fiction; a prologue can.

2. Don Quixote, the Prologue to Part I (1605) — tone by performance. Cervantes opens with an account of himself unable to write a prologue, and a friend’s advice to fabricate the learned apparatus that convention demands. The reader learns, before meeting the knight, that this book’s relation to its own conventions will be sceptical and comic — a contract the novel keeps for nine hundred pages. Full analysis at §9.4.

3. The Master and Margarita (written 1928–40; published 1966–67) — the epigraph as protocol. Bulgakov’s epigraph from Faust, in which a spirit identifies itself as part of the power that eternally wills evil and eternally works good, tells the reader how to take the devil who arrives at Patriarch’s Ponds three pages later. It is a threshold doing reader-contract work with a borrowed sentence. See §9.6.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Authority Domain

This section proposes an addition to the model. It is named the authority domain to keep it apart from Chapter 13’s epistemological domain, which concerns who inside a book knows what. Chapter 13 (its section 4.6, “The authority domain: a ruling”) rules that it is not a domain of the viewpoint grant and keeps it here, as a domain of the separated unit.

The gap in the five-domain model

Return to the illustrations that keep resisting classification.

  • Frankenstein opens with letters from a man who did not witness most of what the book contains, addressed to a sister who never replies, framing a story told to him by a dying stranger who in turn reports a story told to him by a creature.
  • Dracula opens with an unsigned note explaining how a set of documents was assembled and asserting that they are contemporaneous records.
  • The Scarlet Letter opens with fifty pages establishing that the narrator found a scrap of embroidered cloth and a packet of papers in a custom-house attic.
  • Lolita opens with a foreword by a psychologist explaining the provenance of the manuscript and the fates of its subjects.
  • Robinson Crusoe opens with an editor asserting that the narrative is a history of fact.

Try to file these under the five domains and each attempt goes slightly wrong. It is not the world domain: the register shift in Dracula’s note is a symptom, not the point, and the note establishes nothing about Transylvania. It is not the reader contract in the ordinary sense: tone is not being set, patience is not being purchased, and the material is frequently rather dull. It is not plot, since nothing happens; not character, since the addresser is often barely a person; not theme, since no proposition about life is being advanced.

What these prologues actually do is make a claim about the text’s evidentiary status.

The authority domain. The prologue establishes how this book knows what it tells, and on whose authority — by supplying a provenance for the narrative, a transmitting intermediary, a documentary basis, or an explicit account of the limits of what can be known.

Carriers

Carrier The reader’s implicit question Where it lives in the prose
Provenance Where did this account come from? The found document, the inherited papers, the discovered tapes
The transmitting intermediary Who is passing this on, and what did they change? An editor, a translator, a listener, a compiler
The authority claim Why should I believe it? Assertions of fidelity, completeness, contemporaneity, or their pointed absence
The limits of knowledge What can’t be known here? Gaps acknowledged; sources described as partial; a narrator’s admission of reconstruction

Why it is a distinct domain

Three arguments.

1. It has its own failure mode. A prologue can serve plot, character, world, theme and the reader contract impeccably and still fail in the authority domain — by installing an authority claim the book then ignores. A common instance: a novel opens with a frame narrator receiving a story and then narrates hundreds of pages of scenes that narrator could not possibly know, in a voice that is not theirs. Readers feel this as a slippage they cannot name, and no other domain names it either.

2. It has its own instruments. Provenance, intermediary, authority claim and acknowledged limit are technical devices with a two-and-a-half-century history (§3.4). They are not register choices and not tonal choices. They are structural commitments about the ontology of the text, and they constrain everything that follows.

3. It is the domain that most obviously requires exteriority. This is decisive for a chapter organised around the higher bar. A claim about the text’s evidentiary status cannot be made from inside the text’s ordinary narration without breaking it. A close third-person chapter cannot pause to explain where its own account came from; the moment it tries, it has become a different kind of book. If a novel wants to say these are documents, assembled thus, by this person, for this reason, it must step outside — and a prologue is the step. Of the six domains, this is the one whose separation is not merely useful but necessary, which makes it, on this chapter’s own criteria, the strongest of the six.

The argument against

The case is not one-sided, and a student should be able to make the opposing argument well.

  • It is a special case of two existing domains. The register shift into documentary prose is world-domain work; the instruction about how to take the following pages is reader-contract work. Splitting the difference into a sixth category multiplies entities without explaining anything the original five could not, and the five-domain model’s virtue is compactness.
  • It is historically local. The epistemological prologue belongs overwhelmingly to a band running from Defoe to the Gothic revival, with a modernist afterlife and a postmodern revival. The great majority of contemporary novels make no claim about their own authority at all, and a domain that is irrelevant to most of the form is a poor candidate for elevation to a general category.
  • Genette has already handled it. The fictive and apocryphal prefaces of Paratexts (§3.2) describe this apparatus, with a finer typology than the domain model offers. Adding it to a craft taxonomy duplicates existing theory less finely.

The chapter adopts the sixth domain for diagnostic purposes, on the grounds that its failure mode is real, common, and unnameable in the original five — but it registers the objection that the domain is genre-bound in a way the other five are not.

Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class, with Dracula, The Handmaid’s Tale and The Name of the Rose as evidence.

Illustrations

1. Dracula (1897). The unsigned prefatory note serves almost nothing but the authority domain: it establishes provenance, an unnamed compiler, a claim to contemporaneity, and an editorial principle. It sets no tone, advances no plot, and describes no world. §9.7.

2. The Handmaid’s Tale (1985). The “Historical Notes” perform every function of the authority domain — provenance of the tapes, a transmitting intermediary, an authority claim, and an extensive, uncomfortable account of the limits of what can be known — from the other end of the book. §9.8 argues that this positional inversion is the best available evidence that the domain is distinct, since the material does not change when it moves.

3. The Name of the Rose (1980). Eco’s “Naturally, a Manuscript” describes a chain of transmission — a nineteenth-century French edition of a seventeenth-century Latin version of a fourteenth-century monk’s memoir — with such elaborate and self-undermining care that the provenance becomes a joke about provenance, while still functioning as one. It is the authority-domain prologue at the point where the tradition becomes self-aware.

4.7 Summary: the six domains

Domain Carriers Under-delivery Over-delivery
Plot Framed ending; unwitnessable incident; running clock; compressed antecedent Vague portent; nothing the main narrative couldn’t do The book’s best scene spent before Chapter One
Character Early access; installed irony; seeded origin; retrospective self A camera position mistaken for a person A prologue protagonist stronger than the actual one
World Register declaration; lore at distance; scale; social panorama Decorative heightening; tone without content A register the book cannot sustain
Theme Distilled proposition; governing image; frame to complete; question posed A figure too general to be recognised again The prologue explains the book’s meaning
Reader contract Tone declaration; patience purchased; felt stakes; reading protocol Hook alone; nothing riding on it A promise the book’s real pitch cannot meet
Authority (proposed) Provenance; intermediary; authority claim; acknowledged limits A frame that opens and is then ignored Apparatus so elaborate the story cannot start

4.8 Must all six be served at once?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

  1. Two or three is the working target, not six. §5 argues that a prologue serving one domain is suspect and a prologue serving two or three is usually safe. Six would be the checklist prologue, and it would read as an exercise.
  2. Some domains are mutually exclusive in practice. A prologue that installs dramatic irony (character) forecloses a book whose pleasure is shared discovery. A prologue that frames the ending (plot) is very hard to combine with a reader contract built on suspense. These are not defects in the model; they are the actual structure of the choices.
  3. The domains have unequal weight for the higher bar. Plot’s unwitnessable incident and the authority domain clear the bar almost automatically, because their work is impossible from inside. The reader contract clears it least easily, because tone can nearly always be set inside Chapter One. A prologue serving three weak domains is worse placed than one serving a single strong one.
  4. The audit’s output is a decision, not a score. The domains exist to answer one question: keep separate, absorb, or relocate? A prologue serving two domains that both require exteriority is kept. One serving four domains none of which requires exteriority is absorbed, and the material is not lost — it moves.
