Chapter 10 · The Prologue

The Prologue

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 10. Part III: The Architecture of the Whole.

The second chapter of Part III, a companion to the chapters on scene, sequel, dialogue, detail, transition, chapter break, backstory, viewpoint, subplot, opening pages and ending — and, in particular, the immediate neighbour of Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), against which this chapter defines its subject.

Chapter Abstract

A prologue is the only unit of a novel that must argue for its existence twice. Like every other unit, it must earn its keep: it must do work the reader would miss. Unlike every other unit, it must also earn its position — it must justify not merely existing but existing outside the main narrative, before the book has properly begun, in a compartment the book has explicitly labelled as apart. This chapter formalises that double obligation into a teachable model.

It proceeds in four movements:

  1. Theory — why a separated preliminary unit is held to a stricter standard than an ordinary opening chapter, drawing on Genette’s account of the paratext (and on the precise ways in which a novelistic prologue fails to fit that account), on the long history of the prologos, the chorus, the induction, the accessus and the editorial fiction, on Sternberg’s expositional modes, on Kermode and Said, and on the cognitive literature on privileged position, primacy, and the maintenance of dormant contextual frames.
  2. Taxonomy — the five domains of purpose (plot, character, world, theme, and the reader contract), each anatomised in terms of its textual carriers and characteristic failure modes, and expanded here with a proposed sixth: the authority domain, the prologue that establishes on whose authority the book knows what it tells.
  3. Method — the principle of multiplicity applied to the separated unit; the mechanism of distance and closing the distance, developed into four axes of distance, a taxonomy of fourteen prologue types, and a taxonomy of the ways a distance can be closed; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol for revision.
  4. Application — twelve failure patterns, each with a published counterexample; eight close readings; the prologue across forms; and a full apparatus of exercises, glossary, sources and further reading.

The chapter’s governing claim is that a prologue is not a strong opening that happens to sit before Chapter One. It is a positional claim — an assertion that this material could not have done its work from inside the main narrative — and the whole of the craft discussion follows from taking that assertion seriously enough to test it.

The chapter’s governing caution is the mirror of that claim. The contemporary hostility to prologues is real, widespread, and largely local: a product of Anglo-American acquisition practice since roughly 1980, not a discovery about narrative. A student should finish this chapter able to apply the higher bar rigorously and able to say which parts of the prejudice against prologues describe the form and which describe the market.

A note on the examples. The illustrative prologues are chosen for what they demonstrate. A concordance mapping each concept to the work that best displays it appears at §10.7. The Core chapter adds post-2000 fiction and screen cases, listed in §14.5.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Define the prologue by its two constitutive features — separation along at least one of four axes, and explicit labelling as separate — and distinguish it from the cold open, the frame narrative, the preface, the epigraph and the first chapter.
  • State and apply the higher bar: the requirement that a prologue justify not its existence but its exteriority, and the consequence that a prologue failing only the second test is absorbed rather than cut.
  • Explain, with reference to Genette, why a novelistic prologue occupies a boundary the standard theory of the paratext does not accommodate — typographically paratextual, ontologically diegetic — and why that anomaly is the source of most confusion in craft discussion of the form.
  • Historicise the modern prejudice against prologues, naming its institutional causes and identifying which of the chapter’s own prohibitions describe narrative and which describe a market.
  • Analyse any prologue in terms of the six domains of purpose, and audit which domains a draft actually serves.
  • Diagnose the axis of distance a prologue opens and the mode of closing by which the book closes it, and argue for or against fragmentary closure in a given case.
  • Apply the ten-test protocol to a draft prologue and produce a prioritised revision plan whose first question is always absorb, relocate, or keep separate?
  • Evaluate the limits of the model, including its assumption of a sceptical reader, and defend a prologue that fails several of its own tests.

How to Use This Chapter

Reader Route Purpose
Student in a seminar §§1–6 in order, then §9, then §12 Conceptual grounding, worked models, guided practice
Writer in revision §7 (diagnostics) → §8 (failures) → §6.5 (closing) → §11 (checklist) Fast triage of a live prologue, ending in a keep/absorb/relocate decision
Teacher or workshop leader §3.4, 6.3, 8, 9 for material; §12.1 for assignments; §12.3 rubric A teaching sequence that includes the historical corrective, not only the craft rule

A caution before beginning, in the same terms as Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages). This chapter contains taxonomies, tests and checklists. None of them is a formula. They are instruments for noticing, and their proper output is a question. A protocol that returns the answer “this prologue fails test two” has done nothing until the writer asks whether the book wants it to — and, in this chapter more than any other, whether the test is measuring the book or the marketplace.
