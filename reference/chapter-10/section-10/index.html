Chapter 10 · The Prologue

10. The Prologue Across Forms

Continue in Core

The model developed above was built for the novel. It transfers to adjacent forms with adjustments that need stating, because students frequently import novel-prologue habits into forms that punish them — and, in one case, into a form that rewards them far more generously.

10.1 The short story

The short story has, in the main, no room for a prologue, and the reason is arithmetical rather than aesthetic. A prologue’s costs (§1.1) are fixed: a doubled start-up, an orientation decision, a suspended frame. Its benefits scale with the length of the book that follows. In four thousand words the costs are unchanged and the benefits have almost nowhere to accrue.

Two consequences follow.

  • Where a story does use a separated preliminary unit, it nearly always serves the authority domain (§4.6) — a found letter, a frame narrator, an editorial note — because that is the one domain whose payoff is instantaneous rather than cumulative. The frame does its work in the first paragraph.
  • The story’s title carries a disproportionate share of the threshold’s work. As Chapter 9 (§10.1) observes, a story’s title is often a working part of the opening rather than paratext. It is also, frequently, the story’s entire prologue: a claim about how to read what follows, made in six words.

10.2 The novella

The novella is where prologues most often go wrong, and for the reason Chapter 9 gives about openings generally: novellas are written by novelists. A prologue of a thousand words is two per cent of a novel and five per cent of a novella, and the closing schedule that a four-hundred-page book can afford — a fragment at page 90, another at 240, a landing at 380 — has no room to operate.

The working adjustment is compression of the schedule, not of the prologue. Where a novel’s first fragment should arrive within the first quarter (§6.6), a novella’s should arrive within the first tenth, and the total number of fragments should drop from four or five to two.

James’s The Turn of the Screw (§8.9) is the form’s demonstration of the alternative: open the frame, never return, and let the non-closure be the effect. That is a solution available to the novella because the interval is short enough for the reader to hold an unclosed frame without strain.

10.3 Series and multi-volume works

The series inverts several of this chapter’s economics, and students working in genre should know how.

Variable In a single novel In a series
Tolerable interval before closing Rarely more than one book Can extend across volumes and years
Reader’s frame maintenance Unaided Aided by rereading, wikis, discussion, and the interval itself
The prologue’s authority Provisional until redeemed Becomes a convention if repeated, and conventions are self-justifying
Cost of a viewpoint that never returns High (§8.9) Low, where the series has declared that vantages are disposable

The decisive difference is repetition. A device used once is a choice the reader assesses; a device used at the head of every volume is a formal signature, and readers stop assessing signatures and start expecting them. This is the mechanism by which Martin’s disposable-viewpoint prologues (§8.7) convert a failure pattern into a structural feature, and it is available to any series willing to commit.

Two cautions. First, the same repetition that legitimises a device makes it a promise: a series that opens five volumes with a prologue and the sixth without has broken a formal contract in Chapter 9’s sense (§4.6 there). Second, the extended interval is a licence and not an exemption; a distance opened in volume one and never closed by volume seven is still §8.4, merely at a scale where the reader has had longer to notice.

10.4 The screen comparison

The nearest screen analogue is the teaser or cold open: the pre-title sequence, the body discovered before the credits, the Bond stunt that has nothing to do with the film’s plot.

Three things transfer, and three do not.

Transfers: - The distance-and-closing logic is identical. A teaser that opens a gap the film never closes produces the same retrospective dissatisfaction. - The false-advertising failure is identical, and screen has its own name for it: the trailer moment that is not in the film. - Fragmentary closure works in the same way, and screen exploits it more systematically than prose does, through recurring visual motifs.

Does not transfer: - The cost of the doubled start-up is far lower on screen. A viewer re-orients in a second and a half; a reader takes a page. This is the single largest structural difference and it explains why teasers are near-universal in television and prologues are contested in fiction. - Skipping is not available. A viewer cannot bypass a teaser as a reader bypasses a prologue, which removes the whole of §7.1’s test 8 and with it the design constraint it imposes. - The label does no work. Screen has no equivalent of the word Prologue printed at the head of a page; the boundary is marked by the title sequence, which is an ending marker rather than a beginning one. The consequence is that screen has the prologue-function without the paratextual costume, which is to say without the higher bar.

The inference for writers who have absorbed screen conventions: the teaser is cheap and the prologue is not. Advice imported from television about opening with a hook before the titles is advice calibrated to a form where the reader cannot leave, cannot skip, and re-orients almost instantly. None of those conditions holds in prose.

10.5 Drama

Drama is where the form began, and it retains two capacities the novel has lost.

  • The prologue can be spoken by a body. A chorus, a presenter, an actor stepping out of the fiction: the threshold is occupied by a person the audience can see, which converts an abstract framing device into an encounter. Henry V’s Chorus is not a voice but a man asking a favour of the house.
  • The prologue can recur without becoming a system. Because a theatrical chorus is a role rather than a compartment, it can return between acts without the structural implications a novel’s repeated prologue would carry. §9.6 argues that Bulgakov achieves the novelistic equivalent only by giving up the label and using chapter breaks instead.

The novel’s compensating advantage is the one Genette names: a printed threshold can be skipped, and a device that can be skipped can afford to be long. No theatre audience would sit through “The Custom-House”.

10.6 Translated fiction

The series’ standing distinction applies with unusual force here: structural claims survive translation; texture claims do not.

Three specific observations for students working with translated prologues.

  1. The label may not survive. Prologue, Prólogo, Vorwort, Предисловие and Introduzione do not map cleanly, and translators and publishers routinely renumber, retitle, relocate or silently absorb front matter. A student analysing whether a passage “is labelled as apart” must check the original’s apparatus, not the paperback’s.
  2. Prefatory apparatus accretes across editions. Frankenstein (§9.1) is the standard warning: the volume a student buys may contain the 1818 Preface, the 1831 Introduction, a modern scholarly introduction and a chronology. Of these, Mary Shelley wrote only the 1831 Introduction. Genette’s time variable (§3.2) is the instrument for sorting them.
  3. Register distance is the axis most damaged in translation. Where a prologue’s separation rests on register (§6.1) — an archaic diction, a scriptural cadence, a bureaucratic flatness — the axis may simply vanish, because the receiving language’s registers do not sit in the same relation. The Master and Margarita’s alternation between Soviet-satirical Moscow and the grave Yershalaim chapters is legible in every English version, but the degree of the drop varies substantially between them, and a claim about how far the register falls is a claim about the translator.
