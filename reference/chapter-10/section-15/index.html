Chapter 10 · The Prologue

15. Further Reading

Continue in Core

Annotated and grouped by what each source is good for. Starred items are the recommended first three.

15.1 On thresholds and the apparatus of a book

  • ★ Genette, Gérard. Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation (1987; Eng. 1997). The indispensable book for this chapter and the one every serious student of prologues should own. Read the chapters on the original preface, the later preface and the fictional preface first. Read them against the novelistic prologue, noting where the categories strain: the strain is the subject of §3.1.
  • Genette, Gérard. Narrative Discourse (1972; Eng. 1980). For anachrony, reach and extent, and the crucial distinction between advance notice and advance mention (§6.2) — the technical vocabulary for the two modes of closing.
  • Dames, Nicholas. The Chapter: A Segmented History from Antiquity to the Twenty-First Century (2023). The definitive modern study of the unit the prologue label belongs to. Read alongside Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break): the prologue is one member of a segmentation system with a long history of its own.

15.2 On beginnings as a problem

  • ★ Said, Edward W. Beginnings: Intention and Method (1975). Difficult and worth the difficulty. The origin/beginning distinction (§3.8) is the most useful single idea available to a writer deciding whether to have two beginnings, and Said is far more precise about what is at stake than any craft manual.
  • Sternberg, Meir. Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978). Demanding, formalist, and still without a rival. The suspense/curiosity distinction alone reorganises how a writer thinks about disclosure, and it is the instrument §8.6 uses to dismantle the prohibition on spoiling.
  • Nuttall, A. D. Openings: Narrative Beginnings from the Epic to the Novel (1992). A historical and philosophical survey attentive to the metaphysical oddity of beginning at all. The best complement to Sternberg’s formalism, and unusually good on the classical material behind §3.3.
  • Kermode, Frank. The Sense of an Ending (1967). For fictions of concord, and for understanding why a prologue that frames an ending buys patience. Read with Chapter 16 (The Ending).

15.3 On the reader

  • ★ Rabinowitz, Peter J. Before Reading (1987). Underread and immediately practical. The rules of notice and privileged position explain more about why prologues behave as they do than any craft manual, and they supply the argument for §7.1’s plant-audit.
  • Emmott, Catherine. Narrative Comprehension: A Discourse Perspective (1997). Contextual frames, priming, and frame recall. The empirical foundation of §6.6’s argument for fragmentary closure, and the best available account of what a reader is doing while a prologue lies dormant.
  • Zwaan, Rolf, and Gabriel Radvansky (1998). A review article; the clearest statement of the dimensions readers track. Directly relevant to §8.8 and to why a mythic overture can be unstorable.
  • Asch (1946); Perry, “Literary Dynamics” (1979). For the primacy effect, and for why a prologue’s frame governs the reading of everything after it.

15.4 On the history of the form

  • Watt, Ian. The Rise of the Novel (1957). For the eighteenth-century context of §3.4 — why the early novel needed to manufacture evidentiary status at its threshold, and what formal realism was doing instead.
  • Scholarship on the accessus ad auctores. Any standard history of medieval literary theory or of the commentary tradition will supply the headings and their rationale. Worth an afternoon for the shock of encountering a culture that thought a reader should be told the author’s intention before reading.
  • Editions with full prefatory apparatus. The most useful reading here is primary: a scholarly Frankenstein containing both the 1818 Preface and the 1831 Introduction; a Don Quixote with both prologues; a Scarlet Letter with Hawthorne’s preface to the second edition. The apparatus is the argument.

15.5 Craft literature, used critically

  • Lukeman, Noah. The First Five Pages (2000). Valuable as ethnography of the acquisition process rather than as aesthetics. It is the clearest single document of the institution described in §3.6, and should be read as evidence about a market rather than as instruction about narrative.
  • Gardner, John. The Art of Fiction (1983). For the “vivid and continuous dream”, and for the observation that the dream is easiest to break before it has begun — which is the prologue’s doubled start-up cost.
  • Swain, Dwight V. Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965). For the underlying scene mechanics that a prologue must satisfy in addition to its positional obligations.

15.6 Within this textbook

  • Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) — the immediate neighbour and the necessary companion. Its zero-context problem, its five contracts, and its §8.4 on the borrowed hook, which is Chapter One’s version of this chapter’s false advertising.
  • Chapter 16 (The Ending) — the other boundary. Its mismatched final note is the same genre-contract violation as §8.2, committed at the exit; and its treatment of the “Historical Notes” and the Appendix to Nineteen Eighty-Four completes §9.8.
  • Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) — inherits this chapter’s distance-and-closing vocabulary and applies it to the cutaway inside the running narrative.
  • Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) — the segmentation system the prologue label belongs to, and the origin of proposition and payoff.
  • Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) — the empathy budget, which a prologue spends in advance on a consciousness the reader has not met.
  • Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) — the discipline of the recoverable object, without which fragmentary closure is impossible.
  • Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) — the four domains this chapter extends, and the underlying question every unit must answer.
