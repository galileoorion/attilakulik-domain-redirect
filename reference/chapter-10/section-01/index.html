Chapter 10 · The Prologue

1. Introduction: The Problem of the Second Beginning

Continue in Core

1.1 A book that begins twice

Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) described the threshold problem: page 3 has no account, and must generate on the spot every unit of trust that page 203 spends freely. That asymmetry is a fact about position. A book’s first page has nothing behind it, and everything it claims must be secured out of its own resources.

A prologue inherits that problem and adds a second one on top of it.

Consider what a reader encounters when they turn past the half-title, the epigraph and the dedication and find, at the head of the first page of narrative, the word PROLOGUE. Three things have been communicated before a sentence has been read:

  1. Something is about to happen that is not the story. Or, strictly, not the story in the form the book will mostly take. The label is a boundary marker.
  2. The book has more than one beginning. There will be a Chapter One after this, and Chapter One will also be a beginning — of the main sequence, in the main voice, at the main pace.
  3. The reader is being asked to hold something in reserve. Whatever is established here will not be immediately continued. It will be set down and picked up later, or not.

Each of those is a cost. The first costs orientation: the reader must decide how to file this material. The second costs momentum: the reader who invests in a prologue’s situation must disinvest at its end and invest again eight pages later, and Chapter 9’s account of transportation as a threshold phenomenon with a start-up cost (Gerrig; Green and Brock; Kuijpers et al.) implies that the start-up is paid twice. The third costs memory: a frame opened and then suspended must be maintained, unrewarded, across an interval the reader cannot estimate.

None of these costs is fatal. Every one of them is routinely paid, in books nobody would improve by removing the prologue. But they are real, they are borne by the reader rather than the writer, and they are the reason a prologue is judged more harshly than an equally good scene placed inside Chapter One. The reader is not being difficult. The reader is being asked for something extra and is, quite reasonably, checking what they get for it.

This is the problem of the second beginning, and it produces the chapter’s first principle.

1.2 The claim in its strong form

A prologue is not asking to be kept. It is asking to be kept separate. Those are different arguments, and the second one is harder to win.

Every unit of narrative in this textbook answers the same underlying question, first posed in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene): does it do a job the reader would miss if it were cut? A prologue answers that question twice over. It must earn its own existence like any scene — a character wanting something, meeting resistance, ending in a changed condition, or whatever the equivalent is for the register it adopts. And it must, additionally, justify existing outside the main narrative rather than inside it.

The consequence is procedural, and the chapter’s tests depend on it:

A scene that fails the first test is cut. A prologue that fails only the second test is not cut — it is absorbed.

Absorption is the operation of folding a prologue’s material into Chapter One, or relocating it to the point in the main timeline where it actually belongs, and deleting the label. It is not a lesser form of deletion. It is very frequently an improvement, because the material was good and only its position was wrong; and it is the reason the common workshop instruction “cut your prologue” is both widespread and imprecise. The objection is rarely to the pages. It is to pages that could have been integrated and were not.

What the claim does not assert needs stating with equal care, because the strong form invites three misreadings.

  • It does not claim that prologues are suspect as a class. The suspicion is a contemporary and largely commercial attitude with a traceable history (§3.4), and the form itself is older than the novel by two thousand years.
  • It does not claim that a prologue must be dramatic, short, or exciting. The Scarlet Letter’s prologue runs to some fifty pages of autobiographical essay; Don Quixote’s consists of a man failing to write a prologue. Both are canonical.
  • It does not claim that the separation must be defended to a hostile party. It claims that the writer should be able to name what the separation buys. Naming is a revision instrument. The market’s scepticism is a fact about the market, and §3.8 and §16 both insist on the distinction.

1.3 Where this chapter sits in the book

Chapters 9, 10 and 16 form the textbook’s treatment of the book’s boundaries, and they are deliberately asymmetrical.

Chapter Unit Its condition
9 (The Opening Pages) Chapter One Zero context. No accumulated trust; nothing to borrow from
10 (The Prologue) The separated preliminary unit Zero context, plus a positional debt. It must be interesting and explain why it is over here
16 (The Ending) The close Nothing but context. Everything is accumulated; nothing may be introduced

Chapter 9 defines the opening pages partly against the prologue, and the definition is worth repeating here because this chapter depends on it: the opening pages “cannot borrow authority from separateness. A prologue can be strange because it announces itself as strange. Chapter One must be interesting while also being normal for this book.” The present chapter takes the other side of that sentence. A prologue can borrow authority from separateness — and the loan must be repaid.

Three further dependencies run through the chapter and are named where they bear:

  • The contracts (Chapter 9, §4). A prologue opens the same voice, genre, character, world, stakes and formal contracts as any opening — but it opens them provisionally, on behalf of a passage the reader has been told is not typical. §4.5 and §8.2 address what happens when the provisional contract and the real one disagree.
  • Proposition and payoff (Chapter 6, The Chapter Break). A prologue is the extreme case of a proposition: the largest structural promise a book can make with the fewest resources, and the one with the longest delay before payoff.
  • Distance and closing. This chapter is the origin of the concept; Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) reuses the idea of distance for the cutaway, where the point at which a cutaway ends is called its return, and the same gap-and-closure logic operates on a smaller scale and inside the main narrative rather than before it.

Two symmetries the book deliberately maintains should be stated at the outset.

First, the prologue’s characteristic failure — false advertising, a prologue promising a more violent, more mysterious or more exciting book than the one that follows (§8.2) — is the same violation as Chapter 16’s mismatched final note. A genre contract can be broken at the entrance or at the exit; the mechanism is identical, and only the direction of the reader’s disappointment differs. Chapter 9’s borrowed hook (§8.4 there) is Chapter One’s version of the same error, and the three sections should be read together.

Second, a prologue is a viewpoint shift performed before the reader has a viewpoint to shift from. Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) sets out the empathy budget and the cost of moving a reader between consciousnesses; the prologue’s peculiarity is that it spends that budget in advance, on a mind the reader has not yet met, and often on one they will never occupy again. That is a real expenditure, and §4.2 treats what it buys.
