Chapter 10 · The Prologue

3. Why the Separation Must Be Argued

Continue in Core

The higher bar is usually asserted in craft literature and rarely argued. It can be argued, and from five independent directions: the theory of the paratext, the history of the form, the theory of exposition, the philosophy of beginnings, and the cognitive psychology of reading. This section assembles the case. A writer who understands why a separated preliminary unit is expensive will revise one more intelligently than a writer who has merely been told that agents dislike them.

3.1 Genette, and the boundary his system does not quite accommodate

The indispensable frame for any discussion of prologues is Gérard Genette’s Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation (1987; English 1997), the systematic study of everything that surrounds a text and mediates its reception — titles, covers, dedications, epigraphs, notes, interviews, and above all prefaces. Genette’s governing image is spatial: the paratext is a threshold, a vestibule, a zone of transaction between the inside and the outside of a book, which offers the reader the possibility of stepping in or of turning back. That image is right for the prologue, and this chapter uses it throughout.

But the fit is imperfect, and the imperfection is the most instructive thing in the theory.

Genette’s préface is defined as a liminal text consisting of discourse about the text that follows. The prefacer — whoever they are, and whatever their ontological status — speaks of the book: what it is, how it came to be, how it should be read, why it deserves attention. The preface’s canonical functions, in Genette’s account, are to secure the reading and to secure the right reading. It is commentary occupying a doorway.

A novelistic prologue is usually not that at all. It is fictional narrative inside the text. Nobody comments on anything. A man stands at a register and counts a till twice; a ship is trapped in Arctic ice; a chorus in fourteen lines tells us two households will bury their children. These are not statements about the book. They are the book, beginning earlier and elsewhere than it will mostly take place.

The prologue therefore sits on a boundary Genette’s system does not quite accommodate. It is:

  • typographically paratextual — labelled, set apart, positioned before the numbered chapters, printed in a way that marks it as a different kind of unit, and skippable in the way Genette describes prefaces as being skippable; and
  • ontologically diegetic — part of the storyworld, populated by the fiction’s own persons and events, subject to the same rules of narrative construction as any chapter.

The chapter’s central theoretical observation. A prologue is a diegetic threshold: a piece of the story wearing the costume of the apparatus. Almost every practical difficulty the form presents follows from that hybridity. The reader treats it with a paratext’s licence to skip and a narrative’s demand for meaning; the writer builds it with a narrative’s tools and is judged by an apparatus’s standard of necessity.

Three consequences follow, and they organise the rest of the chapter.

First, the label is a genuine paratextual act with genuine consequences. The word Prologue is not a title in the ordinary sense. It is an instruction about reading procedure. It tells the reader that what follows is bounded, that it will end, and that something else will start afterwards. Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) treats intertitles as a segmentation system; the prologue label is the most consequential single member of that system, because it is the only one that changes the reader’s relation to the ontology of what follows rather than merely to its extent.

Second, skippability is a real property and must be designed for. Genette repeatedly notes the preface’s precariousness: it is the most easily bypassed zone of a book, and prefacers know it, which is why so many prefaces begin by arguing that they should be read. Prologues inherit this precariousness. Some readers skip them on principle (how many is not known: no survey this chapter knows of has measured it), and — this is the point students find hardest — the writer cannot know which readers those are. §7.1, test 8 turns this into a diagnostic: what does a reader who skips this lose, and is the book still legible to them?

Third, the hybridity explains why craft advice about prologues is so contradictory. Advice derived from the paratextual half (“keep it short; it is not the story; readers skip it”) collides with advice derived from the diegetic half (“it must be a real scene with a real want; it must be as strong as anything in the book”). Both are correct about their half. Neither is correct about the whole. A model that names the hybridity can hold both.

3.2 Genette’s three variables, transferred

Whatever its ontological awkwardness, Genette’s analytical apparatus transfers to the prologue with almost no loss, and it supplies vocabulary the craft literature lacks. He classifies prefaces along three dimensions.

Genette’s variable His categories Transferred to the prologue
Place Preliminary (before the text), postface (after) Prologue vs epilogue — and the hybrid case of the retroactive frame (§9.8)
Time Original, later (ultérieure), delayed or late (tardive) The prologue added in a second edition or a later volume; the prologue written last, which most are
Addresser Authorial, allographic, actorial; and authentic, fictive, apocryphal Who is speaking in the prologue, and what claim to authority they make (§4.6)

The time variable is more useful to a working writer than it first appears. Genette’s préface ultérieure — the preface added to a later edition, commenting on the reception of the first — has an exact novelistic analogue: the prologue written after the novel is finished, to fix something. That is a legitimate compositional order and a suspicious one at the same time, and §8.10 anatomises the specific failure it tends to produce.

The addresser variable is where the transfer pays most. Genette distinguishes an authentic preface (signed by the real author, meaning what it says), a fictive one (attributed to an invented person, and understood as fiction), and an apocryphal one (falsely attributed, and meant to be believed). Novelistic prologues use all three, and the choice is not decorative:

  • Frankenstein’s 1818 Preface is authorial in appearance and was in fact written by Percy Shelley in Mary Shelley’s voice — an authenticity claim that is not quite true (§9.1).
  • The Castle of Otranto’s first preface (1764) is apocryphal in Genette’s strict sense: Walpole presents the novel as a translation of a black-letter manuscript printed at Naples in 1529, and expects the claim to be taken seriously. The second edition’s preface withdraws the claim and admits invention. The two prefaces together are the clearest available demonstration that a threshold can make a claim about the text’s authority, and that the claim can be revoked.
  • Lolita’s foreword by “John Ray, Jr., Ph.D.” is fictive and understood as such by every reader, which is why it can carry information Humbert could not (§8.6).

This is the material from which §4.6 builds its proposed sixth domain. Hold it in reserve.

3.3 The prologue before the novel: prologos, chorus, induction

The history of the form is unusually rich, and it matters here for a reason beyond antiquarian interest: nearly every practice the contemporary workshop distrusts was once the norm, and knowing that is the difference between a student who can break a rule and one who can only obey it.

The Greek prologos

Aristotle’s definition in the Poetics is structural and blunt: the prologue is the whole part of a tragedy preceding the entrance-song of the chorus. It is a position, not a function — which is the definitional move this chapter makes, and Aristotle makes it two and a half thousand years earlier.

What filled that position varied. In Euripides it hardened into a convention: a speaker, often a god, sometimes a minor character, addresses the audience directly and supplies the antecedents — who these people are, what has already happened, what the situation is at curtain-up. The Euripidean prologue is pure preliminary exposition, delivered outside the action, by a voice that will not be the play’s principal. It is, in other words, everything the modern workshop objects to, performed by the most self-consciously modern of the three Athenian tragedians.

It was already controversial. Aristophanes’ Frogs satirises the Euripidean prologue’s metrical monotony in the celebrated joke in which Aeschylus interrupts one Euripidean opening after another with the same phrase about a little flask of oil, demonstrating that the prologues are so mechanically constructed that a single formula can be inserted into any of them. That is an early recorded complaint about prologues, and it is a complaint about formula, not about the device. The distinction survives intact: what is being mocked is exposition arriving in a predictable slot in a predictable rhythm, and the modern complaint about the genre prologue is the same complaint with different examples.

The chorus and the induction

English drama inherited the position and put it to different work.

Romeo and Juliet’s prologue is a sonnet. In fourteen lines it names the setting, the feud, the lovers, their deaths, the reconciliation their deaths produce, and the running time of the performance. It gives away the entire plot, including the ending, before a character has spoken.

This deserves more than a passing mention, because it inverts the whole modern anxiety about spoilers, and §8.6 builds a failure pattern’s exception on it. Note what the sonnet buys:

  • Dramatic irony as the default condition. Every line the lovers speak is heard by an audience who knows how it ends. The play’s pathos is a function of the prologue.
  • A guarantee of shape. The audience is told the story has a design and what it is. Kermode’s account of fictions of concord (§3.7) is the theoretical statement of what that guarantee does for an audience’s tolerance of a slow middle.
  • A shift of the operative question. The question is no longer what happens but how, and whether it could have gone otherwise. Sternberg’s distinction between suspense and curiosity (§3.6) is the exact instrument for describing that trade.

Henry V uses the position for a different job again. Its Chorus opens by wishing for “a Muse of fire” and then apologises, at length, for the inadequacy of the theatre — a wooden O that cannot hold the fields of France — and asks the audience to supply with their imaginations what the stage cannot. This is a contract-setting prologue of the purest kind: it does not deliver exposition so much as instruct the audience in how to watch. It is also, incidentally, a prologue that recurs — Shakespeare brings the Chorus back before each act and again at the end, which makes Henry V one of the earliest and clearest demonstrations of the principle this chapter calls fragmentary closure (§6.6).

The induction — Christopher Sly in The Taming of the Shrew, the Scrivener’s mock-contract with the audience in Jonson’s Bartholomew Fair, the ghost of Andrea and the figure of Revenge who sit down to watch The Spanish Tragedy — is a prologue with a fiction of its own: characters who are not in the play, watching or framing the play. It is the direct ancestor of the frame narrative, and the reason §6.3 lists the induction as a distinct type rather than folding it into the choric prologue.

3.4 Two prose traditions: the accessus and the editorial fiction

The accessus ad auctores

The medieval schoolroom developed a standard prefatory apparatus for reading an auctor. The accessus ad auctores supplied, before the text proper, a fixed set of headings: the title of the work, the name of the author, the author’s intention (intentio), the subject matter (materia), the manner of treatment, the order of the book, its usefulness (utilitas), and the branch of philosophy to which it belonged.

Two features of this apparatus matter to a novelist.

First, it is an orientation technology. Its premise is that a reader who knows the author’s intention and the work’s utility before beginning will read better than one who does not. That premise is the opposite of the contemporary aesthetic, which prizes discovery and treats front-loaded framing as a failure of nerve. Neither premise is a fact about narrative; both are period conventions with a rationale.

Second, it is where the “how should I read this?” question was historically answered. When the accessus disappeared, the question did not. It migrated: into the novelistic preface, into the prologue, into the jacket copy, into the genre shelf. Chapter 9 (§3.7) notes that readers never in fact arrive at page one with zero context; the accessus is the ancestor of the apparatus that supplies that context, and the prologue is one of its heirs.

The eighteenth century’s editorial fictions

The English novel’s first century was, to a remarkable degree, a century of prologues doing work in what this chapter calls the authority domain.

Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe (1719) opens with a preface in which an unnamed “editor” states his belief that the narrative is a true history of fact with no appearance of fiction in it. Richardson’s Pamela (1740) is presented by an editor who has merely arranged the letters; his second edition adds commendatory correspondence attesting to the work’s moral utility. Clarissa (1747–48) surrounds its correspondence with editorial apparatus of increasing elaboration, including, in later editions, footnotes and a table of contents that argue with the reader about how the heroine and Lovelace should be judged.

The common purpose is not decoration. The eighteenth-century novel had no settled licence to invent, and its prefaces are engaged in manufacturing evidentiary status: this is a document, not a fable; here is how it came into my hands; here is why you may believe it. Ian Watt’s account of formal realism in The Rise of the Novel (1957) treats circumstantial particularity as the novel’s authenticating technique; the editorial preface is the same authentication performed at the threshold rather than in the sentences.

Walpole’s Castle of Otranto (1764) makes the manoeuvre visible by breaking it: an apocryphal first preface asserting a found manuscript, then a second preface confessing the fiction. Manzoni’s I promessi sposi (1827) makes it comic: the novel opens with a pastiche of an ornate seventeenth-century manuscript, which the narrator then abandons in exasperation, announcing that he will retell the story in his own prose. The apparatus is being displayed as apparatus.

The line runs directly to Frankenstein’s letters (§9.1), to Hawthorne’s Custom-House and its packet of papers (§9.3), to Dracula’s unsigned editorial note (§9.7), to Nabokov’s John Ray, and to Eco’s “Naturally, a Manuscript” at the head of The Name of the Rose (1980). This is a continuous, two-hundred-and-fifty-year tradition of prologues whose subject is not the story but the story’s authority, and §4.6 argues that the five-domain model cannot name what it does.

3.5 Integration, and the return of the genre prologue

The nineteenth century moved decisively towards the integrated opening. As the novel’s licence to invent became secure, the authenticating preface lost its job; and as narrators grew more confident, the work formerly done by an editorial frame was absorbed into the narrative voice itself. A novel that could open with the fog of Chancery did not need an editor to explain what it was.

Prologue-like material did not disappear so much as change register. Scott retained elaborate prefatory fictions — dedicatory epistles, invented antiquarian editors — but they became increasingly playful. George Eliot prefixed Middlemarch with a short “Prelude” on Saint Theresa, which is a thematic prologue of the purest kind: no scene, no character from the novel, an image and a question, and a matching “Finale” at the far end. Hawthorne wrote fifty pages of custom-house essay. But the dominant nineteenth-century practice was to begin the book with the book.

The twentieth century restored the prologue on different terms. Two developments matter:

  • The modernist frame. Heart of Darkness, The Turn of the Screw, Brideshead Revisited, The Go-Between and Invisible Man use a separated preliminary unit to establish a retrospective vantage — a narrator situated after the events, whose distance from them is the book’s subject. Here the prologue is not exposition but positioning.
  • The genre prologue. From roughly the middle of the century, and overwhelmingly from the 1970s onward, commercial fantasy, science fiction, horror and the thriller adopted a standardised preliminary unit: the mythic overture, the ancient prophecy, the anonymous victim, the distant catastrophe. Tolkien’s “Concerning Hobbits” is an early and unusually distinguished instance; the convention it helped found is now so uniform that readers can predict its contents from the label.
Period Dominant practice at the threshold
Classical drama The prologos: antecedents supplied by a speaker outside the action
Elizabethan / Jacobean Chorus and induction; the audience instructed how to watch; plots given away
Medieval scholarly The accessus: author, intention, subject, utility, declared before reading
18th century The editorial fiction; found manuscripts; authenticity manufactured at the threshold
19th century Integration; the narrator absorbs the frame’s work; prologue survives as thematic prelude
Modernist The retrospective frame; the prologue as vantage rather than exposition
Mid-century to present, genre The standardised preliminary unit: overture, prophecy, victim, catastrophe
Contemporary literary and commercial Institutional suspicion; the prologue as a submission liability (§3.6)

3.6 The modern prejudice, and its actual causes

This section is a corrective, and it is offered without either endorsing or dismissing the attitude it describes.

Anyone who has attended a creative-writing workshop, read an agent’s submission guidelines, or spent an hour among writers online in the last three decades knows that the prologue has a reputation. The advice is remarkably uniform: don’t. Cut it. Nobody reads them. Make it Chapter One.

This attitude is recent, local and largely commercial. It is not a discovery about narrative. It is roughly forty years old, concentrated in Anglo-American trade publishing and the workshop culture that feeds it, and it has three identifiable causes, none of them aesthetic.

1. Submission-reading practice. Agents and commissioning editors read manuscripts in volume, under time pressure, and — as Chapter 9 (§3.6) sets out, following Lukeman — frequently decide within the first page or two. A prologue means that the first page or two an agent reads is, by the author’s own admission, not typical of the book. The reader who must judge four hundred pages from two is being handed the two that the author has labelled unrepresentative. Whatever the prologue’s quality, it degrades the sample. The rational response of a reader in that position is irritation, and forty years of irritation hardens into doctrine.

2. The sample-chapter convention. The standard submission package is a query letter plus the first three chapters or the first fifty pages. A prologue consumes part of that allowance without advancing the main narrative. A writer who submits a prologue plus two chapters has shown less of their book than a writer who submits three. The convention is arbitrary — there is no reason the sample must begin at the physical beginning — but it is entrenched, and it makes the prologue structurally costly at the exact point where cost is least affordable.

3. Genre overuse. The commercial genre prologue became, over about three decades, so uniform that it stopped carrying information. When fantasy novels commonly open with a mythic overture and thrillers with an unnamed woman being murdered (an impression from the shelves, not a count), the label begins to predict the contents, and a device that predicts its own contents has ceased to be a device. Readers learned to skip; then learned to say they skip; then learned to say prologues are bad.

Two further contributors are secondary.

  • Screen convention leaking into prose advice. The cold open is free on television and expensive in prose, for the reasons Chapter 9 (§10.5) gives. Advice to “start in the action” is often screen advice, and the prologue is the form it most obviously collides with.
  • The self-reinforcing folklore. “Readers skip prologues” is repeated far more often than it is measured. It is probably true of some readers and certainly false of others, but its circulation as a fact makes writers defensive and makes editors confident.

How to hold this properly

The correct position is double, and it is the same double position Chapter 9 adopts about opening conventions generally.

  • The pressures are real and worth knowing. A writer submitting to trade publishers in the present decade who attaches a prologue is paying a tax, and should know they are paying it, and should be able to say what they are buying.
  • The pressures are not a theory of narrative. Romeo and Juliet, Frankenstein, Wuthering Heights, The Scarlet Letter, Don Quixote, Dracula, Invisible Man: an aesthetic that rules these out has not identified a fault in them. It has identified its own boundary.

The formulation this chapter recommends to students. The higher bar is a craft principle. The hostility to prologues is a market condition. They point in the same direction often enough to be confused, and they are not the same thing. §16 returns to the distinction as the chapter’s closing argument.

3.7 Sternberg: the prologue as the limit case of preliminary exposition

Meir Sternberg’s Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978) supplies the most rigorous available framework, and Chapter 9 (§6.2) has already introduced it. Sternberg’s insight is that exposition is not a place in a novel but a distribution problem: every narrative must convey its antecedents, and the only variables are when and in what order.

Mode Description The prologue’s relation to it
Preliminary (concentrated) Antecedents delivered up front, before the action The prologue is this mode’s limit case — preliminary exposition so concentrated that it has been given its own compartment
Delayed Action first, antecedents later The prologue’s rival: the same material, distributed through the main narrative
Distributed Antecedents dispersed and never gathered The prologue’s opposite, and often the correct revision when a prologue fails (§7.2)

Two consequences follow, and both are practical.

First, the prologue’s characteristic vice is inherent in its mode, not incidental to it. Preliminary exposition spends an asset: information delivered early cannot generate curiosity later. This is the precise mechanism of the info-dump prologue (§8.1), and it explains why the failure is so persistent. A writer who chooses the most concentrated form of the most front-loaded expositional mode has chosen the structure in which that spending is easiest.

Second, Sternberg’s distinction between suspense and curiosity explains what a proleptic prologue trades away. He separates suspense — uncertainty about the future — from curiosity — uncertainty about the past. A prologue that flashes forward to a later crisis (the body in the river, the burning house, the trial) does something very specific to that pair:

  • It destroys or reduces suspense about whether. The reader knows the house burns.
  • It manufactures curiosity about how and why. The reader now has a question about the past of a future event — a peculiar and powerful hybrid.
  • It converts the whole main narrative into an approach. Every scene is now read as a step towards a known destination, which sharpens causal attention and flattens surprise.

That is a real trade, with real gains, and it is the trade Romeo and Juliet makes at maximum volume. Whether it is a good trade depends entirely on whether the book’s interest is in outcomes or in causes. A book whose pleasures are causal gains from a proleptic prologue. A book whose pleasures are outcome-based is destroyed by one. §7.1, test 10 turns this into a question a writer can ask.

3.8 Kermode and Said: shape promised, and beginning chosen

Two further theoretical strands bear directly, and they answer different questions: what a prologue does to a reader’s sense of the whole, and what it claims about the book that has one.

Kermode: the prologue as a promise of shape

Frank Kermode’s The Sense of an Ending (1967) argues that human beings make sense of duration by imposing fictions of concord — structures in which a middle is intelligible because it lies between a beginning and an end that are felt to belong together. His example is the clock: we hear tick-tock, a little plot with an ending, rather than an undifferentiated series.

A prologue that frames an ending — a flash-forward, a retrospective narrator, a chorus announcing the outcome — is a promise of concord issued in advance. It tells the reader: this has a shape; there is a tock coming; the middle you are about to read is a middle of something.

Two implications for the writer:

  • It is why a proleptic prologue buys patience. The reader who has been shown the destination will tolerate a slower approach than one who has not, because the approach has been converted from wandering into travel. This is the mechanism underneath the reader-contract domain’s “earn patience” (§4.5).
  • It is why the promise must be kept in the form in which it was made. A prologue that promises concord and delivers dispersal has committed a specifically Kermodean failure: not that the ending is unsatisfying, but that the frame the prologue installed is never completed. §8.4 is that failure in its practical form.

Said: origin and beginning

Edward Said’s Beginnings: Intention and Method (1975) supplies the sharper conceptual instrument, and it is the one this chapter most wants students to acquire.

Said distinguishes an origin from a beginning. An origin is passive, given, divine, mythical — the point from which things come, not chosen by anyone. A beginning is active, secular, made: an intentional act by which a writer designates a point as the first, and thereby accepts responsibility for the designation. Every beginning, in Said’s account, is a claim, and it must be continually re-earned by what follows.

Apply that to a book with a prologue, and something clarifies immediately:

A prologue is a book choosing to have two beginnings. It is not a longer run-up. It is a second designation, a second intentional act, and therefore a second claim requiring a second justification.

This is the philosophical form of the higher bar, and it is more precise than the craft formulation. The craft formulation says: prove the separation was necessary. Said’s formulation says: you have performed the constitutive act of authorship twice, and each performance must answer for itself. It also explains why the prologue’s failures feel like failures of nerve rather than failures of skill — an unjustified second beginning reads as a writer who could not decide where the book starts, which is to say, could not commit to the act Said describes.

The corollary is more generous than it sounds. If a beginning is made rather than given, then there is nothing intrinsically improper about making two. The impropriety is in making the second one accidentally.

3.9 The cognitive strand: notice doubled, primacy, and the dormant frame

Three findings from the empirical literature on reading bear on the prologue with unusual directness. None of them is a law of literary value; all describe tendencies in comprehension, and the chapter says so.

Privileged position, doubled

Peter Rabinowitz’s Before Reading (1987) argues that readers operate with tacit rules of notice — conventions determining which parts of a text are treated as significant — of which the strongest are the rules of privileged position: titles, epigraphs, first and last sentences, and the openings and closes of chapters carry automatic interpretive weight.

A prologue occupies a privileged position twice over. It is the beginning of the book, and it is also a bounded unit with its own beginning and its own end, both of which are themselves privileged. In practical terms, every detail in a prologue is read as significant, and the last sentence of a prologue is one of the most heavily weighted positions in a novel.

The operational consequence is severe and is the reason §7.1 includes a plant-audit:

An unredeemed detail in a prologue is far more damaging than an unredeemed detail on page 100. On page 100 a stray specific is texture. In a prologue it is a promise, made in a position the reader has been trained to treat as load-bearing, in a unit the book has explicitly marked as exceptional. The reader will look for it. When it never returns, the whole unit’s necessity comes into question retrospectively.

The inverse is the resource. A detail placed in a prologue and redeemed three hundred pages later produces a recognition disproportionate to its size — which is the mechanism the household worked example in §9.9 relies on, and which Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) treats as the payoff side of denotation and connotation.

Primacy: the prologue sets the interpretive frame

Solomon Asch’s 1946 experiments on impression formation established that identical information presented in different orders produces different judgements, because earlier items govern the interpretation of later ones. Menakhem Perry’s “Literary Dynamics” (1979) transposed the finding into reading theory as the primacy effect: readers build a hypothesis from initial material and assimilate subsequent material to it, resisting revision.

A prologue is the first material a reader meets. Whatever frame it installs — this is a thriller; this is a book about a family; this is a world of gods and prophecy; this is a story someone is telling us — becomes the frame through which Chapter One is read.

This is what makes false advertising so costly. The failure is not merely that the reader was promised excitement and given quietness. It is that the promised book becomes the interpretive frame for the actual book, so that the actual book is read for three hundred pages as a deviation rather than as itself. Chapter 9 makes the same point about the borrowed hook; the prologue’s version is worse, because the separation makes the promise more emphatic and the label makes it deliberate.

Emmott’s frames: the empirical form of distance and closing

Catherine Emmott’s Narrative Comprehension: A Discourse Perspective (1997) describes readers as building contextual frames — running mental representations binding characters to locations and times — and as switching between them, keeping some primed (currently active) and others dormant while the narrative is elsewhere. Returning to a dormant frame requires recall: the reader must reinstate the context, and the reinstatement has a cost that rises with the interval and with how thinly the frame was built.

This is the empirical form of the mechanism this chapter calls distance and closing, and it yields three practical propositions.

  1. A prologue builds a frame the reader must immediately suspend. Chapter One begins somewhere else; the prologue’s frame goes dormant at once, having been primed for only a few pages. It is therefore an unusually fragile frame — built quickly, held long.
  2. The reinstatement cost rises with the interval. A prologue recalled at page 40 costs the reader little. A prologue recalled only at page 380 may cost more than the recognition is worth, and some readers report having to turn back and reread a prologue at the moment it finally becomes relevant. That turning-back is the cost made visible.
  3. Frames that are periodically touched stay warm. A dormant frame refreshed at intervals — by an object, a phrase, a name — is cheaper to reinstate than one left untouched. This is the cognitive argument for fragmentary closure over the single late reveal, developed fully at §6.6, and it is the chapter’s main practical argument. Applying Emmott’s model to prologues is this chapter’s inference, not her finding.

3.10 The higher bar, restated — and qualified

The chapter’s first principle can now be stated in full.

The higher bar. A prologue must satisfy two independent tests. (1) It must do work the reader would miss if it were cut — the test every unit of narrative faces. (2) It must do work that specifically requires being outside the main narrative: a vantage the protagonist cannot supply, a register the main voice cannot sustain, a span of time no single scene can cross, a claim about the text’s authority that the text cannot make from inside itself. A unit failing (1) is cut. A unit failing only (2) is absorbed.

And now the qualification, which is where the more interesting teaching lies.

The bar as stated assumes a sceptical reader. It assumes someone who must be persuaded that the detour was necessary, who will hold the separation against the book until it is justified, and who is entitled to an accounting. That assumption is not a description of the form. It is a description of a particular market condition — the condition described in §3.6, in which prologues are read by professionals under time pressure who have been trained by three decades of interchangeable genre openings to expect the worst.

Change the reader and the bar moves.

  • A seventeenth-century playgoer did not require a prologue to justify itself; the prologue was the expected furniture of the occasion, and its absence would have been the marked case.
  • A medieval reader trained on the accessus expected to be told the author’s intention and the work’s utility before beginning, and would have found an unheralded plunge into narrative disorienting rather than bracing.
  • A reader of an eighteenth-century novel expected the editorial frame, because the alternative — a book asserting invented events with no account of how they were known — was not yet a settled convention.
  • A reader who has chosen a nine-hundred-page epic fantasy has, by the act of choosing it, indicated a tolerance for mythic overtures that the general trade reader does not have.

The bar, in other words, is real but not natural. It is a good instrument because it makes a writer articulate a choice that would otherwise be made by default. It is a poor instrument if it is mistaken for a fact about narrative, because then it licenses the removal of the best prologues in the language.

The chapter’s practical recommendation is therefore not “clear the bar” but:

Know which of the two tests your prologue passes, and know whether the party demanding the second test is your reader or your market. Both are legitimate considerations. They are not the same consideration, and a writer who cannot tell them apart is not making a decision — they are obeying one.
