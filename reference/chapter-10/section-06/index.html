Chapter 10 · The Prologue

6. Distance and Closing

Continue in Core

6.1 The mechanism, and the four axes

Every prologue opens a gap between itself and the story that follows. What separates a working prologue from an orphaned one is whether the main narrative eventually does something with that gap.

Working mechanism. Distance — how far the prologue sits from Chapter One, measured along four axes: an earlier or later time, a different place, a viewpoint the reader will not hold again soon, a register the main narrative does not share. Closing the distance — the point, or points, at which the main narrative catches up to, recontextualises, completes, or deliberately refuses to close what the prologue opened.

Distance never closed is, in this edition’s judgement, the commonest structural complaint made against prologues, and the complaint is rarely about the pages. It is that the gap was never closed, so the separation reads, in hindsight, as unearned. Distance never closed is a promise the book forgot it made.

The four axes

The axes are not interchangeable. Each opens a different kind of gap, each buys different things, and each is closed by different means. A writer who can name which axis is doing the work has already answered most of §7’s questions.

Axis The gap What it buys How it is normally closed Characteristic failure
Time The prologue is set earlier or later than Chapter One Compressed antecedent; dramatic irony; a framed ending The narrative arrives at the prologue’s moment, or explains it The interval is never crossed; the reader waits for a rendezvous that does not occur
Place The prologue happens somewhere the main narrative is not Scale; an off-page threat; a second front A character reaches the place, or its consequences arrive The location is decorative and never becomes relevant
Viewpoint The prologue is focalised through someone the main narrative does not follow The absent vantage; installed irony; access to an antagonist or a dead person The viewpoint returns, or the reader recognises its traces in what others report An orphaned consciousness; the reader attaches and is abandoned (§8.9)
Register The prologue speaks in a mode the book otherwise does not use Protocol; lore; mythic or documentary authority The register recurs, or its claims are cashed in the ordinary voice The book reads as a falling-off from its own opening (§8.2)

Two working principles follow.

First, most prologues use more than one axis, and one of them is doing most of the work. The household prologue (§9.9) is distant in time (three days before), in viewpoint (the father), and marginally in register (a slightly more formal retrospection). Of the three, viewpoint is load-bearing: the time gap could be closed by any flashback, but the vantage cannot be recovered at all. Identifying the load-bearing axis tells the writer what the return must actually deliver.

Second, the axes differ in how expensive they are to leave open. A register gap left open is often tolerable — the reader accepts that the book had an overture. A viewpoint gap left open is the most expensive of the four, because the reader has bound a consciousness into a contextual frame (§3.9) and then had it withdrawn. This asymmetry is the reason §8.9 treats the orphaned voice as a separate failure pattern rather than as an instance of unresolved distance.

6.2 The scholarly treatment

Two bodies of work describe the mechanism, and their vocabulary is useful.

Genette on prolepsis, reach and extent

Genette’s Narrative Discourse (1972; English 1980) analyses anachrony — every discordance between the order of events and the order of their telling — using two measurements that transfer directly to the prologue:

  • Reach: how far from the present moment of the narrative the anachrony goes.
  • Extent: how much narrative time the anachrony itself covers.

A prologue is, in these terms, an anachrony of maximal reach and (usually) minimal extent, placed before the narrative has a present moment to be measured from — which is why it feels categorically different from an ordinary flashback and why Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback), which inherits this section’s vocabulary, treats the cutaway as the same mechanism operating under gentler conditions.

Genette’s more useful distinction for this chapter is between two kinds of forward reference:

Genette’s term What it is Prologue application
Advance notice (annonce) An explicit announcement of something to come The choric prologue; Romeo and Juliet; a narrator saying what will happen
Advance mention / seed (amorce) An apparently insignificant detail whose significance appears only in retrospect The prologue’s planted objects; the pen, the drawer, the sign

This pair is the technical vocabulary for the two modes of closing. A prologue built of annonces is closed by arrival: the announced event occurs. A prologue built of amorces is closed by recognition: the reader meets the object again and understands it. The second mode is the one §6.6 argues for, and Genette’s term names what it plants.

Emmott on frames, priming and recall

Catherine Emmott’s account of contextual frames (§3.9) supplies the reader-side mechanism. A prologue builds a frame — persons bound to a place and time — which goes dormant the instant Chapter One begins somewhere else. Returning to it requires frame recall: the reader must reinstate a context that has not been active, and the reinstatement costs attention proportional to the interval and inversely proportional to how well the frame was built.

Three consequences that the craft literature does not usually state:

  1. A prologue’s frame is unusually fragile. It is primed for a few pages only, on a first reading, before any of the reader’s ordinary consolidation has occurred. It is therefore both the earliest and the thinnest frame in the book.
  2. A cold reinstatement after four hundred pages may fail outright. Some readers report turning back to reread prologues at the moment of the closing (a report, not a measured finding). That turning-back is the reinstatement cost made visible, and it is a defect: the effect the writer designed depended on a recognition the reader could not perform unaided.
  3. Frames that are touched stay warm. Any element of the dormant frame — an object, a name, a phrase, a place — reactivates part of it at very low cost. This is the empirical basis for fragmentary closure.

6.3 A taxonomy of prologue types

The four axes describe the dimension of a prologue’s distance; they do not describe its shape. What follows is a working repertoire of fourteen recognisable types, each with its characteristic purchase and cost. They are not mutually exclusive — a found document is very often also a frame narrator’s reception, and a mythic overture is often also choric — and strong prologues frequently braid two.

# Type Description Exemplar Buys Costs
1 Proleptic flash-forward A later moment shown first The Go-Between; Invisible Man Enormous pull-forward; converts the book into an approach Spends suspense; risks false advertising if the flash outclasses the book
2 Deep-historical antecedent Events centuries or generations before Tolkien, “Concerning Hobbits” Scale; lore delivered as inheritance The nearest type to pure exposition; highest skip rate
3 The antagonist’s vantage Focalised through the opposing force Fleming, From Russia, with Love (its first ten chapters, before Bond appears) Off-page threat; sustained dramatic irony Forecloses shared discovery; can make the protagonist look slow
4 The victim’s last hour A character we will not follow, shortly before their death A Game of Thrones (1996); The Da Vinci Code (2003) Immediate stakes; a crime with a face The genre’s most exhausted convention; §8.7
5 The frame narrator’s reception Someone receives the story we are about to read Frankenstein; The Turn of the Screw Reliability questions; a listener; distance as an interpretive instrument Two openings to earn instead of one
6 The found document A text discovered, described, and reproduced The Scarlet Letter; The Name of the Rose Provenance; the authority domain at full strength Apparatus can delay the story past the reader’s patience
7 The editorial note An unnamed or named compiler explains the assembly Dracula; Lolita Reading protocol; authenticity; compression Almost pure information; no scene, no want
8 The mythic or cosmogonic overture The world’s origins, in a high or scriptural register Beowulf’s exordium; the epic proem Scale; register licence; permission for the marvellous Names and cosmologies the reader cannot yet hold (§8.8)
9 The aphoristic or choric statement A general proposition or an outside voice framing the whole Middlemarch’s Prelude; Romeo and Juliet Theme stated once, cleanly; concord promised Defers everything; fails if the voice is not distinguished
10 The false prologue A scene the reader assigns to the wrong point in the timeline Wuthering Heights; Rebecca A structural surprise; a second reading that differs from the first Depends on a misreading the writer cannot guarantee
11 The epigraph-prologue A quotation or fragment standing at the threshold Anna Karenina; The Master and Margarita Maximum compression; an outside authority summoned Not narrative; may be the wrong instrument (§8.11)
12 The induction A framing scene with its own characters, watching or presenting The Taming of the Shrew; Bartholomew Fair Metafictional licence; an audience made visible Requires a second fiction to be built and paid for
13 The dramatis-personae prologue The cast introduced before the action The Canterbury Tales; Vanity Fair’s “Before the Curtain” Orientation for a large cast; a social panorama Six promises at once (Chapter 9, §8.9); no momentum
14 The last ordinary day The quotidian immediately preceding a rupture the main narrative will investigate Agee, “Knoxville: Summer, 1915”, placed by the editors before A Death in the Family Dread without event; an unwitnessable vantage; objects for fragmentary closure Reads as slight to a reader who does not yet know what is coming

Notes on six of the types

Type 1, the proleptic flash-forward, is the type most often reached for and least often examined. The critical question is not whether to reveal the outcome but which outcome. A flash-forward to the book’s climax destroys the book. A flash-forward to a position after the climax — a narrator in exile, in a cellar, in a foreign hotel, remembering — reveals the existence of an aftermath without revealing what happened, and buys concord (§3.8) at almost no cost in suspense. Hartley’s The Go-Between and Ellison’s Invisible Man both take the second option; so, functionally, does Rebecca.

Type 4, the victim’s last hour, is included not to be dismissed but because its exhaustion is instructive. The type asks a reader to invest in a consciousness for two thousand words and then destroys it, on the understanding that the investment purchases stakes for a book about the investigation. It works when the victim is a person; it fails, reliably, when the victim is a function. §8.7 sets out the discrimination.

Type 7, the editorial note, is the shortest and least fashionable type and it is disproportionately effective. It is close to free: Dracula’s runs to under a hundred words, no scene required, and it discharges the entire authority domain. Its cost is that it violates every contemporary rule about beginning with action, which is a good demonstration of how narrow those rules are.

Type 10, the false prologue, is the only type in the table that depends on an error the reader makes rather than on information the text supplies, which makes it the riskiest and, when it works, the most durable. Lockwood’s arrival at Wuthering Heights reads on first encounter as the beginning of a story; it is in fact the beginning of that story’s final movement, and the whole retrospective apparatus of Nelly’s narration exists to bring the reader back to where they started. A reader who does not make the initial error loses nothing important, which is what makes the type viable: it must reward the misreading without depending on it.

Type 12, the induction, is nearly extinct in the novel and unusually alive in adjacent forms — the framing device of a film, the narrator-host of a television anthology, the wraparound of a graphic novel. It is included because its logic explains a family of contemporary experiments, and because Chapter 9 (§6.5) lists the Frame as an opening gambit without distinguishing the induction’s peculiarity: an induction has characters of its own who are not the story’s characters, which is a heavier commitment than a frame narrator who simply listens.

Type 14, the last ordinary day, is proposed here as a distinct type rather than a soft version of type 4. Its logic is the opposite of the victim’s last hour: nothing violent occurs, no threat is visible, and the entire effect is produced by the reader’s foreknowledge that this ordinariness is terminal. It is the type the household worked example uses, and it is the type in which fragmentary closure is not merely preferable but structurally required, because the prologue contains no event to be arrived at — only objects to be recognised.

6.4 How to use the taxonomy: substitution testing

Its purpose is not classification but substitution. A prologue that is not working is very often the right material in the wrong shape, and the productive exercise is to ask what this prologue would be as a different type.

The procedure:

  1. Identify the type the draft currently is.
  2. Identify the load-bearing axis (§6.1) and the domains served (§5.5).
  3. Rewrite the same material as two other types that preserve that axis and those domains.
  4. Compare what each version buys and costs before deciding which is best.

Some substitutions recur often enough to name:

From To When the substitution helps
Deep-historical antecedent (2) Editorial note (7) When the material is information, not experience — the note delivers it in a fifth of the space
Victim’s last hour (4) Last ordinary day (14) When the book is about aftermath rather than pursuit
Mythic overture (8) Epigraph-prologue (11) When the cosmology is atmosphere rather than mechanism
Proleptic flash (1) Frame narrator (5) When the future moment matters less than who is telling from it
Found document (6) Nothing — absorbed When the provenance is a habit rather than a claim the book cashes

The last row is the important one. Substitution testing frequently ends in absorption, and that is a success of the method rather than a failure of the draft.

6.5 Closing the distance: the taxonomy of closure

A distance can be closed in four ways, and they are not ranked by quality — each suits a different book. What is not acceptable is closing it by accident, or not knowing which one the book is attempting.

Mode Mechanism Exemplar Buys Costs
Total closure The narrative reaches the prologue’s moment; the frame is re-entered and completed Frankenstein (Walton resumes); Invisible Man (the Epilogue); The Go-Between (the Epilogue) Formal satisfaction; the shape Kermode describes as concord Can feel schematic; the reader may see the rendezvous coming from far off
Partial or fragmentary closure Elements of the prologue recur singly across the book and are recognised Henry V (the recurring Chorus); the household example (§9.9) Keeps the frame warm; produces repeated small recognitions rather than one large one Requires discipline; easy to leave a fragment unredeemed
Recontextualisation The narrative never reaches the prologue, but changes what it meant Lolita (John Ray’s foreword read again after the novel); Wuthering Heights (Lockwood’s fatuity, re-encountered) A second reading that differs from the first; retrospective irony Invisible to a reader who does not return; the effect may be entirely lost
Deliberate non-closure The gap is held open as a permanent condition The Turn of the Screw (the frame never resumes); The Scarlet Letter (the Custom-House narrator never reappears) An unresolved note carried through the whole book; an unsettling formal asymmetry Indistinguishable, from outside, from the failure at §8.4

The fourth row is where students most need instruction, because deliberate non-closure and negligent non-closure look identical on the page. The discrimination is not in the prologue but in the book’s overall behaviour towards its own frames. Three signals distinguish design from oversight:

  1. The book withholds other closures too. A novel that leaves several things open has a policy; one that closes everything except its prologue has an oversight.
  2. The non-closure is thematically motivated. The Turn of the Screw refuses to resume its frame because the story it contains cannot be adjudicated, and returning to the fireside would supply an authority the tale spends its length denying.
  3. The prologue is about irrecoverability. If what the prologue establishes is that something cannot be got back, then getting it back would contradict the material.

Where none of the three applies, the honest diagnosis is §8.4.

6.6 The argument for fragmentary closure

This subsection is the chapter’s main practical argument.

Craft advice on prologues, where it addresses the closing at all, tends to assume the single late reveal: the prologue’s meaning is withheld and then delivered, in one place, usually near the end. So that’s what the man on the ice was carrying. The structure is satisfying to design and it is what most drafts attempt.

It is usually the weaker of the two available structures, and Emmott’s frame model explains why.

Recall the three propositions from §6.2. A prologue’s contextual frame is thin (built in a few pages) and dormant (suspended immediately). A single late reveal requires the reader to perform a cold reinstatement of that frame after the longest possible interval — the moment in the book when the frame is at its weakest and the reinstatement cost at its highest. The writer has designed a recognition and scheduled it at the point where the reader is least equipped to have it.

Fragmentary closure inverts the schedule. Instead of one large reveal at the end, the book offers a series of smaller recognitions throughout, each of which:

  • re-primes the dormant frame at negligible cost — a named object, a repeated phrase, a place;
  • confirms to the reader that the prologue is being used, which is itself reassuring and forestalls the suspicion of §8.4;
  • compounds, because each recognition makes the next one cheaper and more resonant; and
  • distributes risk, since a reader who misses one fragment can still catch the next.

The technical name for the planted element is Genette’s amorce, the seed (§6.2): a detail that passes as ordinary on first encounter and acquires significance retrospectively. A prologue built of seeds rather than announcements is a prologue designed for fragmentary closure.

Three practical rules follow.

  1. Plant three to five recoverable specifics, not one. Objects and phrases are recoverable; atmospheres and feelings are not. A reader can recognise a fountain pen; they cannot recognise a mood.
  2. Schedule the first fragment early. Within the first quarter, and preferably within the first fifty pages. The purpose of the first fragment is not payoff but reassurance: it tells the reader the prologue is load-bearing, and it converts them from someone tolerating a frame into someone tracking one.
  3. Vary the fragment’s weight. A return that is always the same size becomes a tic. The strongest sequences move from unremarked mention, to noticed mention, to a scene that turns on the object.

The counter-argument, stated fairly

Fragmentary closure is not universally superior, and two situations favour the single reveal.

  • When the prologue’s content is a genuine structural secret. If the prologue’s meaning is the book’s central concealment — the identity of a killer, the true relation between two characters — then fragments risk giving it away early. Here the cold reinstatement is a price worth paying, and the remedy is to reduce the interval rather than to fragment.
  • When the effect depends on the reader having forgotten. Some books want the closing to arrive as a shock, and a warm frame cannot be shocked. This is a legitimate design, and it is why the taxonomy above is not a ranking.

The generalisable statement is therefore conditional rather than absolute:

Where a prologue’s purpose is to be carried — held as a dread, an irony, or a knowledge the characters lack — fragmentary closure is stronger, because the effect is continuous and the frame must stay warm. Where a prologue’s purpose is to be sprung, the single reveal is correct, and the writer should shorten the interval instead of lengthening the suspense.

Most prologues, on inspection, turn out to be of the first kind while having been built as though they were of the second.

6.7 Historical drift in the axes, and a caution

The four axes have not been used in equal proportions across the history of the form, and the drift, tabulated below as an impression rather than a count, matters because a student who knows it will not mistake the current distribution for a natural one.

Period Dominant axis Typical type Typical return
Classical drama Register (a speaker outside the action) Choric statement (9) Total: the announced events occur
Elizabethan / Jacobean Register and viewpoint Chorus, induction (9, 12) Total or recurring; the Chorus often returns
18th-century novel Register and authority Editorial note, found document (6, 7) Frequently none — the frame is a licence, not a promise
19th century Time and theme Aphoristic prelude, dramatis personae (9, 13) Recontextualisation; the “Finale” answering the “Prelude”
Modernist Viewpoint Frame narrator, proleptic flash (1, 5) Total closure or deliberate non-closure, often ambiguously
Mid-century genre Time and place Overture, victim, antagonist (2, 3, 4, 8) Total: the narrative arrives at the threat
Contemporary literary Time and viewpoint Last ordinary day, proleptic flash (1, 14) Fragmentary; objects carried through

Two inferences pull in different directions, which is the point.

  • The contemporary preference for time-and-viewpoint distance with fragmentary closure is a convention, and a good one — it suits realist fiction, it survives the sceptical reader of §3.6, and it is what this chapter’s own worked example demonstrates. It is not, however, a discovery about how prologues must work.
  • The abandoned axes have not been refuted. The eighteenth century’s register-and-authority prologues, which frequently open a distance and never close it at all, produced Robinson Crusoe, Clarissa and the whole apparatus of the Gothic. A model that reads them as failures of closing has mistaken its period for its subject.

The properly critical position is the one Chapter 9 (§6.7) recommends for openings generally: know the current convention well enough to depart from it deliberately, and read historically well enough to know that it is a convention.
