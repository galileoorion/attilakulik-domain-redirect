Chapter 10 · The Prologue

16. Coda: Against Mechanical Application

Continue in Core

Everything in this chapter is an instrument for noticing. None of it is a rule, and the prologue is the unit where that caveat matters most, because the prologue is the unit about which the surrounding culture holds the strongest and least examined opinions.

The evidence is in the chapter itself. Romeo and Juliet gives away its entire plot in fourteen lines and has held the stage for four centuries. “The Custom-House” runs to fifty pages, is largely about a customs officer’s colleagues, opens a distance on all four axes and closes none of them, is routinely skipped, and Hawthorne knew all of this and republished it without altering a word. Dracula opens with under a hundred words of pure information delivery, no scene and no person. Wuthering Heights advertises a comedy and delivers a metaphysical tragedy, and performs every prologue function while refusing the label. Frankenstein buys patience for a slow opening — the manoeuvre §8.10 diagnoses as a bandage — and is the better for it. A model that rules these out has not found a defect in them. It has found its own edge.

What the model does reliably is convert a vague dissatisfaction into a specific question. This prologue isn’t working becomes: the distance is on the viewpoint axis and the closing is on the time axis, or there are eleven specifics here and the book uses two, or this is a victim’s last hour and the book is about aftermath. Specific questions can be answered. Vague ones can only be worried at.

But the chapter has a second obligation, and it is the one the subject makes unavoidable.

The contemporary hostility to prologues is a market artefact wearing the costume of a craft principle. It arose from three institutional facts — how submissions are read, how samples are conventionally sized, and how thoroughly one genre convention was exhausted — and none of those facts is a discovery about narrative. The hostility is nonetheless expressed in the vocabulary of craft, delivered with the confidence of craft, and taught in workshops as though it were craft. That is what makes it hard to see.

The higher bar is real. It is a good instrument, and this chapter has spent thirty pages building it out, because a writer who can say what the separation buys will make a better prologue than one who cannot. But the bar assumes a sceptical reader who must be persuaded that the detour was necessary, and that reader is a historical figure, not a natural one. A seventeenth-century playgoer expected a chorus. An eighteenth-century reader expected an editor. A medieval reader expected to be told the author’s intention before beginning. Only the reader of the last forty years, in one publishing culture, expects a book to apologise for having a threshold.

A student should be able to tell the two apart, and the test is simple enough to state. When you are told to cut your prologue, ask which of these the objection is:

Is the problem that the separation does no work — or that the separation is expensive to sell?

Both are worth knowing. They call for different responses. The first is a structural defect, and §7 will locate it. The second is a cost, and a writer may reasonably decide to pay it, or reasonably decide not to — but the decision belongs to the writer, and it cannot be made by someone who has confused the two.

There is a last, least mechanical test, to be applied after the other ten. It is the prologue’s version of the question Chapter 9 ends on, and it is asked not of the page but of the writer:

Do you know what this page is doing over here — and could you say it out loud to someone who had not read the book?

If you could, the separation is a choice. If you could not, it is a habit, and a habit is the one thing a threshold cannot afford to be, because a threshold is where a book declares what kind of thing it intends to be. Said’s point, finally, is not that beginning is difficult. It is that beginning is an act, and a book that performs it twice has claimed something twice — and ought, both times, to mean it.
