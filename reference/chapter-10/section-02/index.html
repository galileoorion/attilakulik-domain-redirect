Chapter 10 · The Prologue

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion of prologues is unusually prone to terminological collapse. “Prologue”, “preface”, “foreword”, “prelude”, “frame”, “cold open”, “teaser” and “epigraph” are used interchangeably in workshops, and since they have different functions, different lengths, different addressers and different failure modes, the chapter begins by separating them.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A prologue is a unit of narrative set apart from the main story’s time, place, viewpoint or register, positioned before it begins, and labelled as apart — whose separation from Chapter One is itself doing work the main narrative’s own opening pages could not do as well.

Four elements of that definition are load-bearing.

  • “Set apart … by time, place, viewpoint or register.” These are the four axes of distance, developed in §6.1. A passage that shares the protagonist’s time, place and vantage, and speaks in the book’s ordinary voice, has not opened a distance, whatever it is called.
  • “Labelled as apart.” This is the criterion most often omitted and it is the one that does the definitional work. A stylised, urgent cold open is not automatically a prologue. What makes a passage a prologue is that the book has told the reader it sits outside the main sequence. §2.3 and §7.1 (test 3, the label test) pursue the consequences, and §9.2 tests the criterion against the hardest available case.
  • “Before it begins.” Position is constitutive. A separated unit after the main narrative is an epilogue or an afterword, and §9.8 argues that the epilogue-frame is not merely a mirror image but a different instrument.
  • “Whose separation is itself doing work.” This is the higher bar built into the definition. A passage that could be trimmed and folded seamlessly into Chapter One with nothing lost was never a prologue. It was an opening scene wearing a label its content did not need.

Note that the definition is functional and typographic at once, and that this is deliberate rather than sloppy. A prologue is constituted by what it does and by how the book presents it. §9.2 (Wuthering Heights) and §9.5 (Their Eyes Were Watching God) are chosen because they satisfy the functional half and not the typographic half, and force the definition to declare itself.

2.2 Scale and extent

There is no principled length, and any number offered is a convention. Four bands are in circulation and they behave differently:

Band Extent Typical instances Characteristic risk
The epigraphic A few hundred words or fewer Middlemarch’s Prelude; an aphoristic overture May be doing an epigraph’s work under a prologue’s label (§8.11)
The scenic 500–3,000 words The commercial genre prologue; the proleptic flash False advertising (§8.2); the anonymous-victim cold open (§8.7)
The chaptered 3,000–10,000 words Frankenstein’s four letters; Invisible Man’s Prologue Reads as a first act rather than a threshold; the reader may never accept Chapter One
The sectional 10,000 words and up “The Custom-House”; Tolkien’s “Concerning Hobbits” The skip problem at maximum (§7.1, test 8)

The band matters because it changes which failure is likely and which test is diagnostic. A prologue of a few hundred words almost never fails the redundancy test and frequently fails the epigraph test; a 12,000-word prologue almost never fails the epigraph test and frequently fails every other one.

A working generalisation, offered as a convention and not a discovery: the longer the prologue, the more of the closing the book must schedule early. A short prologue can afford to be paid off at the end. A long one has borrowed too much to leave the reader waiting; the first instalment should arrive within the first quarter.

2.3 The term most often confused: the cold open

A common misidentification in draft manuscripts, in this edition’s judgement the commonest, is the cold open labelled as a prologue.

A cold open begins inside a scene already running, with framing deferred. It is a gambit — one of the twelve tabulated in Chapter 9 (§6.5) — and it is available to Chapter One as freely as to any separated unit. Nothing about beginning in motion requires separation. When a writer opens with a tense, in-progress scene featuring the protagonist, in the protagonist’s own time and place and vantage, and heads it Prologue, they have not made a prologue. They have made a Chapter One and mislabelled it, and the label creates an expectation of distance that the material then fails to honour. This is failure pattern §8.5, Chapter One in Disguise, and, by the same judgement, the one met most often of the twelve.

The diagnostic is mechanical. Name the axis. If the passage does not differ from Chapter One in time, place, viewpoint or register, no distance exists, and there is nothing for the label to mark.

There is a second, subtler confusion, and it is the one Genette’s theory illuminates: the prologue confused with the preface. A preface is discourse about the text — the author, or an editor, or a fictive third party, addressing the reader concerning the book they are holding. A prologue is normally part of the text: fictional narrative, inside the storyworld, populated by the book’s own characters or by a narrator who belongs to its fiction. The two can be hard to tell apart in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century books, which frequently blend them, and §3.1 argues that the blend is not a historical accident but a permanent feature of the form.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the prologue
Preface / préface Discourse about the text, addressed to the reader by an author, editor or fictive intermediary Genette’s category; ontologically outside the fiction. The prologue’s nearest theoretical neighbour and the source of most confusion (§3.1)
Foreword A preface written by someone other than the author Allographic, in Genette’s terms; almost never fictional
Introduction Scholarly or authorial apparatus, usually critical rather than narrative Paratext proper; may be added by later editions, as in Frankenstein 1831 (§9.1)
Epigraph A quotation placed at a threshold Not narrative; makes a claim by juxtaposition rather than by telling. See §8.11
Argument A prose summary of the action prefixed to a book or canto An older and now rare device; the ancestor of the spoiler prologue (§8.6)
Proem / exordium The formal opening invocation of an epic The mythic overture’s ancestor (§6.3, type 8)
Induction Elizabethan and Jacobean framing scene, often with its own characters A dramatic prologue with a fiction of its own; §6.3, type 12
Chorus A speaker outside the action who comments on and frames it Romeo and Juliet, Henry V; the choric prologue, §6.3, type 9
Frame narrative An outer story containing the main one Overlaps heavily; a frame’s opening panel is very often a prologue, but a frame returns, and a prologue need not
Cold open A scene begun in motion with framing deferred A gambit, not a position. §2.3
Teaser Screen term for a pre-title sequence The nearest screen analogue; §10.4
Dramatis personae A list of characters before the text Paratext; sometimes performs a prologue’s orientation function (§6.3, type 13)
Epilogue A separated unit after the main narrative The positional inverse; §9.8
Coda A closing movement continuous with the main narrative but formally distinct Chapter 16’s territory

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • Chapter One itself, and everything about the first pages of the main narrative — Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages).
  • The single late disclosure considered for its own sake — Chapter 15 (The Reveal); and the prologue or recap at the head of a series instalment — Chapter 17 (Series and Genre), with §10.3 here as a first account.
  • Analepsis and prolepsis inside the running narrative — Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback), which inherits this chapter’s distance-and-closing vocabulary and applies it to the cutaway.
  • Chapter titles, part divisions, epigraph systems and the architecture of segmentation — Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break), which treats the prologue label as one member of a larger family of segmentation devices.
  • Endings, epilogues and final notes — Chapter 16 (The Ending), except at §9.8, where The Handmaid’s Tale’s “Historical Notes” is read as the prologue’s structural inverse.
  • Non-narrative front matter — dedications, acknowledgements, maps, family trees, author’s notes, jacket copy. These are paratext in Genette’s strict sense and are discussed only where they compete with a prologue for the same threshold (§8.12).
  • Multi-viewpoint architecture as such — Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift), though §4.2 borrows its account of the cost of a change of consciousness.
