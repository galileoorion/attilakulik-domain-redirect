Chapter 5 · The Transition

12. Studio Work

Continue in Core

12.1 Practical exercises

Exercise 1 — The transition inventory. Run the full procedure at §5.5 on a complete draft, or on fifty consecutive pages. Produce the table. Write 300 words on what the distribution reveals that reading could not.

Exercise 2 — Anchor substitution. Take one join that is not working. Identify its anchor type (§6.3). Rewrite the first two sentences three times, using three anchor types at maximum distance from each other and from the original. Do not judge until all three exist. Write 200 words comparing what each buys.

Exercise 3 — The cold read. Give five joins from your draft to a reader, each with the preceding unit removed. Ask them to mark the exact word at which they knew when, where and whose vantage the passage occupied. Record their marks without correcting them, and compare with your own estimate.

Exercise 4 — The deletion ladder at the join. Take a transition you believe is necessary. Delete its first sentence; read. Delete two; read. Continue until deletion causes loss. Record where the loss occurs and what exactly was lost.

Exercise 5 — Six crossings. Take one gap in your draft and cross it six times, once with each technique in §4: hard cut, time-stamp, match-cut, summary bridge, handoff, bridging object. Some will be absurd; write them anyway. Then write 400 words on why the gap admits some techniques and refuses others.

Exercise 6 — The launching line. Take ten joins. Rewrite only the last sentence before each gap, so that it names a destination, an intention or an object. Then see how much of the material after each gap can be deleted. This exercise reliably removes more words than any other in the chapter.

Exercise 7 — The bracket. Following Woolf (§9.1), write a bridging passage of 500 words that covers an interval of at least five years, in which the human events of the interval appear only in square brackets and the running narration attends to something non-human: a building, a landscape, a machine, a garden. Then write 200 words on what the bracketing did that a scene could not.

Exercise 8 — The iterative bridge. Write a transition of 150 words that crosses an indefinite stretch of time entirely in the iterative — every Thursday, she had taken to, the shop opened later now — without a single specific occasion. Then write the same crossing as a singulative summary and as a time-stamp, and compare what each conveys about the character.

Exercise 9 — Simultaneity. Write two scenes occurring at the same hour in different places, and join them three times: with an explicit marker; with a shared object or event; and with an interpolation in the manner of “Wandering Rocks” (§9.3). Give all three to a reader and ask which chronology they built.

12.2 Seminar questions

  1. Is the orientation budget an aesthetic or an instrument? The chapter concedes at §3.7 that the metaphor encodes a preference for invisible transitions, and that the preference is culturally local. Test the model against Tom Jones and against Pedro Páramo. Does it describe narrative, or a period’s taste?

  2. Is the bridging object a technique or an anchor type? §4.6 proposes it as a sixth technique and gives three arguments against, the second of which is not answered. Argue for or against admitting it, using Mrs Dalloway and To the Lighthouse as evidence.

  3. Does the film analogy help or harm? §3.5 argues that “cut to” thinking systematically under-budgets the arrival. Take a contemporary novel written in a visibly cinematic idiom and assess whether its transitions are impoverished by the borrowing or enriched by it.

  4. Is “Time Passes” integral or detachable? §9.1 stages the disagreement: is the section the novel’s structural centre, or a prose poem inserted between two social novels whose finest effects are purchased by refusing to write the scenes it summarises? Both positions have distinguished advocates.

  5. Do the Levin and Anna plots converge? Critics have disagreed since 1878 about whether Anna Karenina’s two threads constitute a single structure or an alternation. What would count as evidence? Is the question about the transitions, about the plots, or about what “structure” means?

  6. Whose conventions? The failure patterns at §8 are drawn from Anglo-American practice since about 1960. Examine the transitions of a novel from outside that tradition — The Tale of Genji, Dream of the Red Chamber, Season of Migration to the North, or a nineteenth-century Russian or French novel — and identify which of §8’s prohibitions describe narrative comprehension and which describe a local literary culture.

12.3 Assessment rubric

For a submitted portfolio: three transitions from a single project (2,000–2,500 words of narrative, including the units on either side of each join), with a 750-word critical commentary.

Band Craft (60%) Commentary (40%)
Distinction Each join’s technique is chosen and defensible; anchors resolve the dimensions the gap changes, at appropriate speed; at least one join does substantive second work; departures are as considered as arrivals; the three joins are audibly different from one another Names the technique and anchor type of each; costs the gap in dimensions; identifies its own risks and the alternatives rejected; engages at least two theoretical sources critically rather than decoratively
Merit Techniques appropriate; anchors present and reasonably timed; some variation across the three; a second job attempted Accurate self-analysis; some engagement with the model; risks acknowledged if not resolved
Pass Joins are comprehensible; a technique is discernible; anchoring functional if slow or uniform Describes the transitions accurately; theory referenced but not applied
Fail One or more joins orphaned or over-bridged; no discernible choice of technique; failure patterns present and unrecognised Description without analysis; no engagement with the model
