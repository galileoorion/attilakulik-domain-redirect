Chapter 5 · The Transition

1. Introduction: The Join Problem

Continue in Core

1.1 An asymmetry between the writer’s page and the reader’s

Consider what happens in a writer’s mind at a scene break, and what happens in a reader’s.

For the writer, the gap is not a gap. It is the interval between two things they already know: the argument in the doorway, and the drawer in the shop. They have imagined the drive across town, the parking, the key in the lock. They know it is a Monday, that it is late afternoon, that Elena is still angry. None of this has been written down, and none of it needs to be, because for the writer it is all present at once, as a single continuous imagined world. The break on the page is a piece of punctuation between two known quantities.

For the reader, the gap is a genuine discontinuity in the only world they have access to, which is the text. At the moment their eye crosses the white space, everything they were holding — a doorway, two people, a withheld reason, a key changing hands — becomes unreliable. They do not know whether five minutes have passed or five months, whether they are in the same town, whether they are behind the same eyes. They are, briefly, holding nothing.

This asymmetry is the join problem, and in its uncomfortable form it runs: the writer cannot perceive the gap they have created, because they are the one person in the world who is never on the far side of it. Everything the reader needs at a join is, by definition, information the writer already possesses and therefore does not miss.

The consequence is that transitions are almost never fixed during drafting. They are found in revision, and they are found by a specific procedure: reading the join from a cold start, as though the preceding scene did not exist. §7 formalises that procedure. The rest of this section explains why the crossing is expensive enough to require one.

1.2 The claim in its strong form

A transition is not the white space. It manages whatever changes across the gap and re-anchors the reader. When story time is omitted, it also manages an ellipsis.

The two tasks can fail differently and be repaired differently. A simultaneous change of viewpoint or place has no temporal ellipsis, but still has an arrival to manage.

  • The managed ellipsis is a question of duration: how much story time is being skipped, whether the skip is declared, whether its size is legible, and whether anything happened inside it that the reader needs. A transition that mishandles this half produces confusion about how much has changed.
  • The re-anchoring is a question of comprehension: how fast, and by what cue, the reader is reinstalled in a stable frame. A transition that mishandles this half produces confusion about where the reader now is.

A draft can fail either task independently. When time is skipped, a hard cut into a fully anchored new scene can make that ellipsis legible. A hard cut into an unanchored one may leave the elapsed time and the new frame unclear. A three-paragraph bridge that summarises a fortnight and then deposits the reader in an unnamed room has spent heavily on the first half and defaulted on the second — a combination that is commoner in manuscripts than either failure alone, because the writer feels they have done the transition work.

What the claim does assert:

  • That a gap can impose a processing cost on the reader (§3.3), and that the cost varies with the number of dimensions that shift at once.
  • That the cost is paid in words, and that words spent on orientation are words not spent on the new unit’s own business.
  • That the correct expenditure is therefore a judgement about a particular gap, not a house style.

What the claim does not assert:

  • That shorter transitions are better. The hard cut is one technique among six, and a manuscript that uses it everywhere has the same disease as one that time-stamps everywhere (§8.3).
  • That disorientation is always a defect. Disorientation deliberately produced, sustained and eventually resolved is one of the major resources of modernist fiction, and §9 spends most of its length on novels that use it.
  • That transitions are merely functional. §5 argues the opposite: a transition that only orients has wasted an opportunity, and the best transitions in fiction do two or three jobs in the same handful of words.

1.3 Where this chapter sits in the series

Part II of this textbook treats the joins, and its three central chapters divide a single problem three ways. The division is set out here because a transition problem is easily brought to the wrong chapter.

Question Chapter What it owns
How is the gap crossed? Chapter 5 (The Transition) The orientation budget; the anchor; the six techniques; the typography of the gap
Where is the gap placed? Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) Segmentation; proposition and payoff; where a unit is cut off and why
Who is on the other side? Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) Focalisation; the empathy budget; the rules governing a change of consciousness

The three interact. A viewpoint change is also a gap, and is therefore also a transition; the placement of a chapter break determines what the transition on its far side must accomplish. But the questions are separable in revision, and separating them is usually the first useful move: this join is not working resolves into the gap is in the wrong place, or the crossing is badly paid for, or the reader was not told whose head this is.

Four further dependencies run into this chapter from elsewhere in the series.

  • From Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) — the categories of duration. Chapter 1 §6 sets out Genette’s four movements and a twelve-mode continuum from full scene to ellipsis, treating them as a decision about the unit: at what speed should this material be narrated? This chapter approaches the identical scholarly ground from the join: not how fast a unit runs, but what happens at the seam where one speed gives way to another. The two treatments are complementary and deliberately non-overlapping; where Chapter 1 asks should this be summarised?, this chapter asks what does the summary have to do at its beginning and its end? Readers should have Chapter 1 §6 in view, and this chapter does not restate it.
  • From Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) — the causal chain. A transition can be technically faultless and still read as arbitrary if the units on either side of it do not follow from one another. Chapter 2’s Decision beat, in particular, is a powerful transition-preparation device, because a decision that names a place, an object or a person pre-pays most of the next unit’s orientation costs. §4.1 and §9.9 both depend on this.
  • From Chapter 1 again — the principle of multiplicity, applied here to the least glamorous material in the novel (§5).
  • Forward, to Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) — the interest deficit, Chapter 7’s name for the reader’s reluctance to invest in material whose relevance has not yet been established. A transition backwards in time incurs that deficit on top of the ordinary shift costs, which is why the cutaway is treated as a separate chapter rather than as a seventh technique here.
