Chapter 5 · The Transition

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and its exception — a published work that commits the fault and succeeds.

The exceptions are not decorative. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults and not writers who can make choices. In every case the pattern fails because of a mechanism, not because of a surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless.

Note also a structural fact about this section peculiar to its subject. Transitions are the one craft object whose most celebrated instances are almost all violations. The great transitions in English fiction are orphaned, overlong, unmarked or simultaneous; the well-made ones are, by design, unmemorable. The exceptions below are therefore not marginal cases scraped together to satisfy a methodological requirement. They are the canon.

8.1 The Orphaned Cut

Symptom. A hard cut into a new unit that supplies no anchor for a paragraph or more. The new material opens on an unnamed pronoun, in an unspecified place, at an unstated time, doing something whose relation to the previous scene is unclear.

Mechanism. A frame switch is required and not signalled (§3.2). The reader’s previous contextual frame remains primed, so they assimilate the opening sentences to the wrong context and must then perform frame repair — dismantling a partial, confidently built and false representation. The cost is not the confusion; it is the backtrack.

The mechanism explains a detail that surprises writers: the orphaned cut is worse when the prose is fluent. Vivid, confident sentences are assimilated to the wrong frame more thoroughly, so there is more to undo.

Persistence. This is the failure the writer cannot see, for the reason set out at §1.1: they are the only reader who arrives at the far side already oriented. It is also encouraged by good advice badly generalised — trust the reader, don’t over-explain, cut late — each of which is sound and none of which is a licence to withhold the anchor.

Remedy. Test 1, run cold. Then §6.3: put a resolving cue in the first clause. Usually one word will do it, and the word is usually a proper noun.

The exception: Faulkner, The Sound and the Fury (1929). The Benjy section cuts between 1898 and 1928 without warning, without reliable typographic signal, and often without any cue the reader can identify until several sentences later. The italics that are supposed to mark the shifts are applied inconsistently, a fact Faulkner acknowledged and at one point proposed to solve with coloured inks. This is the orphaned cut as a total system, and it succeeds for a reason that generalises: the disorientation is the represented consciousness. Benjy has no sense of temporal sequence, and a reader who is not disoriented has not been given his mind. The anchors, when they come, are not names or dates but sensory: honeysuckle, the smell of trees, a fence, water. §9.5 treats the section in full.

The discrimination to teach: Faulkner’s cuts are unanchored by any conventional cue and are densely anchored by unconventional ones, and the novel trains the reader in the unconventional cues within twenty pages. An orphaned cut in a draft is unanchored by any cue at all.

8.2 The Throat-Clearing Bridge

Symptom. A paragraph of bridging narration before the new unit begins. The drive across town, the parking, the walk to the door, the reflection on the weather, the recollection of what was said earlier — and then, at last, the scene.

Mechanism. Over-payment of the orientation budget. The reader has been given material they would have inferred, at the position of maximum impatience, and the new unit’s engine is delayed while they receive it. The material is not wrong; it is simply unnecessary, which is why it survives revision after revision.

There is a second and less obvious cost. Per Rabinowitz’s rules of notice, discussed in Chapter 9 §3.2, material placed at the head of a unit is read as significant. A bridging paragraph therefore does not merely delay the scene; it promises that the drive matters, and the promise is not kept.

Persistence. The writer needs the run-up. Transitional prose is where a writer re-enters the world of the book, and the paragraph that got them in is functional — for them. It is drafting apparatus retained in the finished text, exactly as Chapter 9 §8.7 describes for openings.

Remedy. A useful revision test: delete downward from the gap, one sentence at a time, until deletion causes loss. Where several opening sentences can go without loss, they may have been drafting apparatus rather than material the reader needs.

The exception: Fielding, Tom Jones (1749). Each of the eighteen Books opens with an introductory chapter that has nothing to do with the story — on critics, on love, on the qualities required in a historian of this kind — before the narrative resumes. By any modern measure these are throat-clearing at industrial scale: they delay the engine by whole chapters and are, for many readers, the best writing in the novel. They succeed because the narrator’s company is one of the things the book is offering, and because Fielding’s joins are not concealed seams but occasions. The generalisable point: a bridge is throat-clearing only when the reader would rather be somewhere else. A bridge written by a narrator the reader wants to spend time with is not a delay; it is a course.

Proust supplies a second and quite different exception, where the approach to a scene is the book’s method rather than its preamble — treated as the model case in Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback).

8.3 The Metronome Transition

Symptom. Every gap crossed with the same instrument, almost always the numerical time-stamp. Three days later… A week after that… The following Tuesday… The manuscript acquires a single rhythmic register at its joins.

Mechanism. Two failures, one local and one cumulative. Locally, a defaulted technique will be wrong for some proportion of gaps — over-informative at some, under-informative at others. Cumulatively, and more seriously, the joins stop carrying information about relative importance. In a book where every seam looks the same, the reader loses the ability to feel that one gap is bigger, stranger or more consequential than another, and the novel’s architecture flattens into a sequence.

Persistence. A time-stamp often prevents simple chronological confusion, so it may not generate the feedback that would prompt a change even when it dulls the book’s rhythm. Defaults can be hard to notice from inside a draft.

Remedy. The transition inventory (§5.5) and test 10. Then a targeted exercise: take the six joins that matter most in the book and re-cut each with a technique it does not currently use.

The exception: Stoker, Dracula (1897). Most major document transitions in the novel are marked by headings and dates. The repeated dating is central to the novel’s form: a story assembled from journals, letters, telegrams and phonograph cylinders, in which the date-stamp is not connective tissue but the evidentiary apparatus that makes the assembled testimony credible. The monotony of the device is what produces the book’s central effect — a sense of documentation closing in on something that cannot be documented.

Two conditions make it work, and both are demanding. The sources and some of the stamps vary, so the reader’s orientation task at each join is not temporal but epistemic: whose account is this, and how much do they know? And the mechanical regularity of the dating becomes an instrument of suspense, since the reader can compare what different documents know on the same day. A metronome succeeds when the beat is doing something other than marking time.

8.4 The Precious Match-Cut

Symptom. An echo transition whose connection most readers will not consciously register: a repeated ordinary word, a visual rhyme separated by intervening material, a parallel too faint to survive reading speed.

Mechanism. The transition’s whole value is in the reader’s noticing, and the noticing does not occur. The writer has therefore spent effort and received nothing, and — this is the part that makes the pattern durable — has no way of detecting the loss, since they cannot unsee the connection they made. The failure is invisible to precisely the person who could fix it.

There is a compounding cost when the echo is faint but the placement is emphatic. A reader who senses that a join is meant to be significant, and cannot find the significance, does not conclude that they have missed something; they conclude that the writing is mannered.

Persistence. Match-cuts are the most pleasurable transitions to write. They feel like craft in a way that a time-stamp does not, and the pleasure of making one is not correlated with its detectability.

Remedy. Test 9, in its corrective half: give the two units to a reader and ask what connects them. Then apply the three detectability conditions at §4.3 — proximity, distinctiveness, difference.

The exception: Joyce, Ulysses (1922). A great many of Joyce’s cross-episode echoes are undetectable on a first reading and some are undetectable on a fifth. The throwaway handbill, the potato in Bloom’s pocket, the recurrence of a phrase heard in the morning at four in the afternoon, the “M’Intosh” figure — these are a system of connections whose density exceeds what any reading in real time can recover. The novel is written for rereading and says so in every other feature of its design, and its echoes are therefore not failed transitions but a deferred reward.

The discrimination: an echo may be undetectable if the book has a second reading in view and does not depend on the echo for orientation. Joyce’s joins are anchored conventionally — names, places, times — and the echoes ride on top. A draft that relies on an undetectable echo to carry the crossing has no transition at all.

8.5 The Whiplash Handoff

Symptom. Viewpoint or location cuts so frequent that no thread accumulates continuous time. The reader is handed between three or four storylines every few pages, each interrupted before its tension can build.

Mechanism. Two mechanisms, and they compound. The first is cost: per §3.3, each handoff shifts three or four dimensions, and a book that performs one every eight pages is asking the reader to pay that bill forty times. The second is structural, and belongs to Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel): tension is a function of continuous, uninterrupted duration inside a want. A thread that is cut away from every four pages never establishes a want strong enough for the deferral to be felt as deferral. The technique intended to create suspense destroys the conditions for it.

The pattern is often defended as “cutting on the cliffhanger”. The defence is sound in principle and fails on frequency: a cliffhanger works because the reader has been allowed to invest, and investment takes pages.

Persistence. Alternation feels like structure. It also solves a real problem — the writer’s uncertainty about which thread is most interesting — by refusing to choose. And it is reinforced by contemporary screen practice, where the cost of a cut is much lower (§3.5).

Remedy. Count pages per thread-visit across the manuscript. Where the median is under about eight pages in a full-length novel, consolidate: merge two visits into one, or delay a thread’s introduction until it can be given room.

The exception: Joyce, “Wandering Rocks” (Ulysses, 1922). Nineteen sections in one episode, each a handoff, with interpolated fragments from other sections cutting across the one in progress. It is whiplash raised to a principle, and it succeeds because the episode’s subject is the simultaneity of a city — the impossibility of any consciousness containing Dublin, and the mechanical indifference of the vice-regal cavalcade and the Jesuit’s walk to one another. No thread builds tension because no thread is meant to. §9.3.

John Dos Passos’s U.S.A. trilogy (1930–36) does something comparable at novel scale with its Newsreel and Camera Eye interpolations. The condition in both cases: the interruption must be the subject, not the pacing strategy.

8.6 The Recap Bridge

Symptom. A transition that re-establishes what the reader already knows: a paragraph reminding them who this character is, what they decided last time, why they are angry with their brother.

Mechanism. The writer has confused frame recall with frame construction (§3.2). A reader returning to a thread does not rebuild it; they retrieve it, and the retrieval is triggered by one cue. Supplying twenty is not redundancy in the harmless sense — it is an assertion that the reader has forgotten, and readers experience such assertions as condescension.

There is also a plot cost. Recap distributes the story’s information twice, which halves the value of each occurrence, and it teaches the reader that they may skim the openings of sections without loss. A reader trained to skim section openings will skim the one that matters.

Persistence. Anxiety about a long manuscript, usually justified in the writer’s own experience of reading their draft in pieces over months. The writer’s memory of their book is fragmentary in a way no reader’s is.

Remedy. Delete the recap and substitute a single cue: a proper name, a resumed object, a phrase from the previous scene. Then check whether anything is actually lost. Where something is, supply it as present consequence — a character behaving in a way the previous scene explains — rather than as summary.

The exception: serial publication. Dumas’s The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46) and Dickens’s part-issues were written for readers who could be separated from the previous instalment by a week or a month, and recaps can serve those readers — sometimes identifying a character by function as well as name. Under those material conditions recap can be service rather than condescension. The pattern can also matter in a series volume (§10.3), a long-running serial, or a novel published in parts. Whether a recap helps depends on the publishing format, the interval between readings and what the prose has made memorable.

8.7 The Double Anchor

Symptom. The reader is oriented twice. A dated heading followed by a first sentence stating the date; a section titled with a character’s name followed by a paragraph establishing whose viewpoint this is; a time-stamp followed by a summary of the interval it stamped.

Mechanism. Redundancy at the exact point where the reader is most attentive. Because the second orientation carries no new information, it reads as a stall, and — worse — it invites the reader to suspect that the two orientations disagree, so they check. A double anchor thus costs more than the sum of its parts, because it generates a comparison operation as well.

The pattern is also a symptom of a process problem rather than a craft one: it typically arises when a writer adds headings late in revision without deleting the prose the headings replace.

Persistence. Each element seemed necessary when written, and neither is wrong. Nothing in a read-through flags redundancy of this kind, because redundant prose is never confusing.

Remedy. Test 7. For every join, ask what dimension each orientation element resolves. Delete any element that resolves nothing the previous one left open.

The exception: Faulkner, Absalom, Absalom! (1936). The novel returns to the same handful of events — Sutpen’s arrival, the design, the murder at the gate — repeatedly, re-anchoring each time, in the mouths of different tellers. This is double, triple and quadruple anchoring as a method, and it works because each re-anchoring is not identical: the facts are re-established with different emphases, by narrators with different investments, and the reader’s job is to notice the discrepancies. Genette would call this the repeating frequency (§3.1); Chapter 7 treats it as reconstruction.

The older exception is oral: Homeric epic re-identifies its characters by formulaic epithet at every reappearance, because a listening audience joining mid-performance cannot flip back. Double anchoring is a defect only where a single anchor would suffice, and the conditions under which one suffices are historically and technologically specific.

8.8 The False Ellipsis

Symptom. A gap that turns out to have contained the important event. The narrative skips an interval, presenting it as an ordinary lapse, and the reader later discovers that the thing the book is about happened inside it.

Mechanism. A false ellipsis can involve Genette’s paralipsis (§3.1) when the established focalisation would have disclosed the event but the narration withholds it. Other omissions may simply be undisclosed gaps. The reader’s model of the story is therefore not merely incomplete but wrong, and they have no way of knowing it is wrong.

The failure has two quite different forms, and separating them is the whole of the teaching.

  • The unintentional false ellipsis is a drafting accident: the writer skipped the confrontation because it was hard to write, or because they had not yet decided what happened in it. The symptom appears far from the cause — a consequence arrives sixty pages later without its antecedent, and the reader experiences the consequence as unmotivated.
  • The deliberate false ellipsis is one of the most powerful devices in fiction, and everything depends on whether the reader is eventually made aware that the gap was loaded.

Persistence. Gaps are cheap and scenes are expensive. A writer under pressure will skip what they have not solved, and a skipped scene leaves no hole on the page.

Remedy. Test 6. Write out what happens in every significant gap. Where a gap contains something the reader will later need, choose deliberately between dramatising it, summarising it, and marking the omission as an omission.

The exception: Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927). “Time Passes” withholds dramatized scenes but discloses the decisive events, so it is not a straightforward case of paralipsis. It is also one of the great formal achievements in English fiction. The novel’s central events — three deaths, including that of its central consciousness — occur inside a bridging section, reported in square brackets, in subordinate clauses, as interruptions to a narrative about weather and dust. Mrs Ramsay’s death is delivered inside a sentence about her husband stumbling along a passage with his arms out. The gap contains everything, and the reader is told so in a form that makes the telling worse rather than better. §9.1.

A second exception: Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847). Heathcliff leaves; three years pass; he returns transformed — educated, moneyed, dangerous — and the novel never explains where he went or how. The gap contains the transformation on which the entire second half depends, and its permanent unavailability is the point: Heathcliff’s power in the second half derives partly from the fact that no one, including the reader, knows what he became or how.

The discrimination: a deliberate false ellipsis makes the reader aware, at some point, that the gap was loaded. Woolf offers a related but different device: she tells us what was in the interval and refuses to dramatise it. Brontë tells us the gap exists and refuses to fill it. An accidental false ellipsis leaves the reader unaware there was anything to know.

8.9 The Travel Montage

Symptom. A journey narrated as a summary of stages. They drove north; the country flattened; on the second day it rained; by Thursday they had reached the coast. Nothing happens; the passage exists to move people.

Mechanism. The travel bridge is the summary bridge applied to an interval whose contents are, by hypothesis, uneventful — so the writer must fill it, and fills it with landscape. The result serves neither purpose well: it is too long to be a transition and too empty to be a scene. It is also the passage in which drafts most reliably lapse into the weather report described in Chapter 9 §8.2, because a moving vehicle supplies an endless stream of describable things and no reason to prefer any of them.

Underneath is a structural error: the journey has been treated as story time to be crossed rather than as a decision about where the gap goes. In most cases the correct move is not to write a better travel montage but to put the gap at the departure and the anchor at the arrival, and to skip the road entirely.

Persistence. Chronological completeness feels like responsibility. Writers also find travel easy to write, since it requires no dialogue and no conflict, which is precisely the diagnosis.

Remedy. Delete the journey; cut from the decision to leave to the arrival. If something in the journey matters, it is a scene and should be written as one. If nothing does, the road is a hard cut waiting to be recognised.

The exception: Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939), chapter 12. The Route 66 chapter is a travel montage of enormous power, and the reasons are precise. It is collective rather than individual: the road is described as the path of a people in flight, not as a family’s itinerary, so the summary form matches the subject, which is aggregate rather than particular. It is argumentative: the geography is doing thematic work about dispossession and scale. And it is formally marked as an intercalary chapter, so the reader knows they are in a different mode rather than in a slow scene.

The transferable rule: a travel montage works when the journey is the subject and fails when it is the interval. Bakhtin’s chronotope of the road — where the road is a space of encounter rather than a distance to be covered — names the same distinction from the other side.

8.10 The Unmarked Simultaneity

Symptom. Two threads occupy the same story time and the text does not say so, so the reader reads them as consecutive. The chronology in the reader’s model is wrong, sometimes for many pages, and is corrected — if at all — by a collision later.

Mechanism. Prose can mark simultaneity with words such as while and meanwhile, or with matching time cues. An unmarked handoff can invite sequential reading because readers often apply this default: narrated order is story order unless told otherwise. The default is not a weakness in readers; it is what makes ordinary narrative processable. A text that violates it silently has exploited a comprehension mechanism rather than used one.

The cost is peculiar in that it is delayed and diffuse. The reader does not stumble at the join; they build a slightly wrong chronology and then find, twenty pages later, that a character knows something they should not, or arrives somewhere too soon. The felt experience is that the book is careless.

Persistence. The writer knows the two threads are simultaneous and has no sense of having withheld anything. Simultaneity is also awkward to signal without clumsiness — meanwhile is a word many writers will not use — so the signal is dropped.

Remedy. Test 8. The repair is usually one phrase: that same afternoon; while Elena was still in the doorway; at the hour the shop should have opened. Where the simultaneity is structural rather than local, a paratextual device — a repeated date, a repeated clock time at the head of alternating sections — resolves it permanently.

The exception: Rulfo, Pedro Páramo (1955; trans. Margaret Sayers Peden). The novel refuses to mark not only simultaneity but the boundary between the living and the dead, between memory and event, and between one speaker and another. Voices arrive without attribution; a scene that appeared to be present turns out to be decades old and narrated by someone in a grave. The unmarked shift is the novel’s central proposition: in Comala, time does not run, and the categories the reader’s defaults depend on — before, after, alive, remembered — are not available. §9.6.

Faulkner’s Benjy section (§8.1) and One Hundred Years of Solitude’s temporal glide (§9.7) are milder members of the same family. In every case the condition is the same: the reader must eventually understand that the chronology is not recoverable, rather than believing they have merely failed to follow it.

8.11 The Undeclared Backward Cut

Symptom. A transition that moves backwards in time without saying so. The reader assumes forward motion, reads several paragraphs as consequence, and then discovers they were reading antecedent.

Mechanism. The same default as §8.10 — narrated order is story order — with the additional cost that the reader has been assigning causal significance to material that cannot have any, since it precedes rather than follows. The frame repair is therefore double: temporal and causal.

Persistence. Writers who use flashback frequently develop a habit of dropping in and out of the past within scenes, where the pluperfect and the surrounding context carry the shift. Across a white-space break the same move loses both supports, and the habit does not notice.

Remedy. Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) treats the cutaway in full; the transition-side repair is a tense signal within the first clause — a single pluperfect will usually do it — or an explicit marker. The rule is that a backward cut must be declared earlier than a forward one, because the reader’s default is working against it.

The exception: Ford, The Good Soldier (1915). The novel is constructed almost entirely from undeclared movements in time. Dowell circles the same few years, arriving at events out of order, retracting, restating, giving away endings and then returning to the middle. What makes it succeed is that the disordering is the narrator’s, not the author’s: a man who cannot bear to approach his subject directly tells it in loops, and the reader is meant to feel the evasion. The novel also declares its method in its opening pages — the famous claim that this is the saddest story the narrator has ever heard, discussed in Chapter 9 §4.1 — so the reader is warned that the telling will be unreliable about sequence.

The discrimination: an undeclared backward cut works when the disordering is characterised. Where it is merely the author’s convenience, the reader has no one to attribute it to and attributes it to carelessness.

8.12 The Frictionless Manuscript

Added, and the inverse of most of the above.

Symptom. Every transition is smooth, every join is bridged, every gap is explained. Nothing is ever confusing. The manuscript nevertheless reads as undifferentiated, and readers report — in exactly these words — that “it all runs together”.

Mechanism. Two, and they are both consequences of over-application of the advice in this chapter. First, articulation requires discontinuity: per §3.3, readers segment narrative at event boundaries; a text that smooths every join may make its formal divisions less noticeable. Second, emphasis is relational: a book in which no join is abrupt has no way of making one abrupt when it matters.

The pattern is disproportionately common in heavily revised manuscripts, and it is the transition-side equivalent of the overstuffed opening described in Chapter 9 §8.5 — the failure mode of good advice.

Persistence. Every individual smoothing was an improvement. The damage is only visible in aggregate, and aggregate effects are invisible during line revision.

Remedy. The transition inventory (§5.5). Then deliberately harden three or four joins: delete the bridge, delete the stamp, let the far side anchor itself. A book needs some joins the reader feels.

The exception: Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925). The novel has no chapters at all and comparatively few white-space breaks; the narration moves between minds, streets and decades in continuous prose, pivoting on shared perceptual events. It is the most frictionless novel in the modern canon and is anything but undifferentiated — because its articulation is supplied elsewhere, by the striking of Big Ben, by the movement of a single day, and by the periodic returns to Clarissa. A book may dispense with joins entirely if it has another system of articulation. What it may not do is dispense with articulation.
