Chapter 5 · The Transition

16. Coda: Against Mechanical Application

Continue in Core

Everything in this chapter is an instrument for noticing. None of it is a rule, and the chapter’s central metaphor is more provisional than most.

The orientation budget is a metaphor, and it is worth being explicit about what it smuggles in. A budget implies scarcity, a currency, an accounting, and the virtue of thrift. Applied to reading, it implies that the reader’s attention is a limited fund, that orientation is an overhead, and that the well-made transition is the one that costs least — which is to say, the invisible one. Every part of that is a description of a particular literary economy: the Anglo-American realist and commercial traditions of the last sixty years, reading conditions shaped by acquisition practice and by a market that rewards frictionlessness, and a critical vocabulary that treats smooth as a compliment.

The evidence against it is in the chapter itself, and it is not marginal.

  • “Time Passes” spends unusual space on the crossing. A transition that occupies about a tenth of its novel in the 1927 American edition, that narrates a decade mostly through the house and its surroundings while briefly entering Mrs McNab’s and Mrs Bast’s perspectives, and that puts the book’s three deaths in square brackets, is not an efficient crossing. It is a refusal of the whole idea that a crossing should be efficient, and it is the finest thing in the novel.
  • Pedro Páramo declines to pay at all. Rulfo does not delay his anchors; he withholds them as a matter of principle, because the categories they would resolve are the ones his novel denies. A model that says he has under-oriented his reader has said something true and useless.
  • The Sound and the Fury pays in a currency the budget cannot count. Faulkner offers, in place of orientation, a texture so specific that the reader stays without knowing where they are. That bargain is not in the ledger.
  • Fielding and Dickens overspend deliberately and buy a relationship. The Victorian narratorial bridge is not an inefficiency to be edited out; it is the place where a certain kind of novel keeps its narrator, and a literature that has abolished it has lost something it did not intend to trade.

So the honest statement of what the chapter is for is narrower than its apparatus suggests. The budget does not describe good transitions. It describes what a transition costs, so that a writer can tell whether they are paying deliberately.

That is a real service, and it is worth defending against the charge of mechanism. Nearly every transition failure in an unrevised manuscript is not a considered violation. It is a default: the time-stamp reached for out of habit, the bridging paragraph retained because it was where the writer got into the scene, the anchor omitted because the writer could not see the gap they had made. The model’s job is to convert this join isn’t working into something answerable — the anchor arrives at word forty in a four-dimension shift, or the launching line aimed and the arrival re-established everything anyway, or this gap contains the scene I have been avoiding. Specific questions can be answered. Vague ones can only be worried at.

But the model must not be allowed to become the standard of judgement, and there is a simple reason. A transition is the one place in a novel where the writer’s relationship with the reader is at its most naked. Everything else in a book can be attributed to the characters; at a join, only the writer is present. The decision to cut hard, to bridge, to speak to the reader directly, to leave them briefly in the dark, to spend twenty pages on ten years of weather in an empty house — these are decisions about how much the writer trusts the reader and how much they wish to be seen managing them. Those are not budget decisions. They are decisions about what kind of book this is going to be, and about what kind of company its author intends to keep.

Which suggests the last and least mechanical test, to be applied after the other ten:

On the far side of this gap, has the reader been left alone, taken by the hand, or thrown? And is that what this book does?

A writer who can answer that has understood the subject. A writer who cannot has an accounting instrument and no idea what they are buying.
