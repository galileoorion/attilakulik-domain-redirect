Chapter 5 · The Transition

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion of joins is more prone to terminological confusion than any other area this textbook covers, and for an identifiable reason: the field’s central term names a mark on the page while the craft object is a stretch of prose. This section separates them.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A transition is the narrative material, however brief, that carries a reader across a gap in time, place, viewpoint or scale — answering, in as few or as many words as the gap actually requires, the reader’s implicit questions of when, where and whose vantage. It comprises the last words before the gap and the first words after it, and it may be a paragraph long, a clause long, or entirely absent.

Four features of that definition carry weight.

  • “However brief.” A transition of zero bridging words is still a transition. The hard cut — white space followed immediately by new material — does not decline to transition; it delegates the whole orientation task to the new unit’s opening sentence. This is a choice with costs, not an absence of choice.
  • “The last words before the gap.” Transitions are habitually taught as an arrival problem and are at least as often a departure problem. The final sentence of the outgoing unit — this chapter calls it the launching line (§5.3, §7.1 test 3) — determines how much orientation the incoming unit has to supply. A departure that names the destination pre-pays the arrival.
  • “When, where and whose vantage.” These three are not an arbitrary list. They correspond, with one addition, to the dimensions that discourse psychology finds readers actively tracking (§3.3).
  • “As the gap actually requires.” The requirement is a property of the gap, not of the writer’s habits. This is the orientation budget, and it is the chapter’s first principle (§3.7).

2.2 Scale and extent

Transitions occur at every level of segmentation, and the same principles govern all of them, at different magnitudes.

Scale Typical signal Typical gap Orientation usually required
Within a paragraph A conjunction; a sentence-initial adverbial Minutes A clause, often none
Between paragraphs Paragraph break alone Minutes to hours A phrase
Between scenes White space, dinkus, ornament Hours to weeks One sentence to a short paragraph
Between chapters Chapter number or title Any Variable; the number itself does work
Between parts or books Part title, sometimes a new date Months to decades Often a full bridging passage
Between narrations A named section, a changed voice Any, including simultaneity The most expensive case (§4.5)

This chapter treats the scene-to-scene and chapter-to-chapter scales as primary, because that is where the craft decisions carry most weight and where drafts most often go wrong. The paragraph-level case is governed by the same budget logic but rarely repays deliberate attention; the part-level case is comparatively rare and is discussed at §9.1 and §10.3.

A note on measurement. It is useful, in revision, to be able to say how large a transition is, and the serviceable unit is words spent before the new unit’s own business begins. A transition of nought words is a hard cut; of six to twenty words, a light bridge; of a paragraph, a summary bridge; of more than about three hundred words, a bridging passage, which has become a small unit in its own right and should be judged as one. “Time Passes” (§9.1), at about twenty-eight pages in the 1927 American edition, is a transition that has become a substantial middle part — one reason the chapter does not offer an upper bound.

2.3 The term most often confused: the scene break

A scene break is a typographic device. A transition is a piece of writing. The two are confused constantly, and the confusion produces two specific and opposite errors in drafts.

The scene break is the signal that a gap exists. The transition is what pays for crossing it. Inserting the signal does not discharge the obligation.

Error one: the break treated as a transition. The writer inserts white space and believes the work is done. Nothing bridges; the new scene opens on an unnamed pronoun in an unnamed place; the reader backtracks. This is the orphaned cut (§8.1), and by this chapter’s estimate (from reading, not a count) it is the commonest transition failure in unrevised manuscripts.

Error two: the transition treated as requiring a break. The writer inserts white space wherever anything changes, including at gaps of ninety seconds, producing a manuscript pockmarked with breaks that signal a discontinuity the story does not contain. Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) treats the placement question in full; the point here is that the mark is a claim about size, and a mark that overstates the gap is misinformation, not neutral punctuation (§6.6).

There is a third relation, which matters because it is the one competent writers use and rarely articulate: a transition can cross a gap without any typographic signal at all. A paragraph that moves from a Tuesday to the following spring inside a single sentence of iterative summary has crossed a very large gap with no mark on the page. The break and the transition are independent variables, and treating them as a single object is what makes the subject hard to teach.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the transition
Ellipsis Genette’s category: story time elapses while discourse time is nil The gap itself, considered as a duration problem; §3.1
Scene break White space, ornament or rule marking a discontinuity The signal; not the transition
Dinkus A small typographic ornament — commonly three spaced asterisks — used as a section divider One of several signals; §6.6
Asterism The three-asterisk mark ⁂, historically a section divider A signal; now rare in trade setting
Bridge Informal craft term for explicit connective prose One technique among six (§4.4), not a synonym for the whole
Segue Borrowed from music; connective material that conceals the join Describes a style of transition, not a category
Cut Borrowed from film; an instantaneous change of vantage Useful shorthand; misleading if taken literally (§3.5)
Match cut Film term: a cut joining two shots by visual or aural rhyme Adapted at §4.3 as the echo transition
Establishing shot Film term: a wide frame locating the action A metaphor with limits; prose has no free wide frame
Frame switch Emmott’s term: the reader’s move to a new contextual frame The cognitive event a transition manages; §3.2
Anachrony Genette’s term for discordance between story order and discourse order Analepsis and prolepsis; owned by Chapter 7
Simultaneity Two threads occupying the same story time The hardest gap to signal in prose; §8.10, §9.3
Interpolation Insertion of material from elsewhere into a running unit Joyce’s device in “Wandering Rocks”; §9.3
Gap / blank Chatman’s gap; Iser’s Leerstelle The reader-side account of what an ellipsis creates
Paralipsis Genette’s term for withholding information required by the established focalisation One possible mechanism of a false ellipsis (§8.8)

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • Where to place a break, and how to end a unit so that the reader turns the page — Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break). This chapter assumes the gap is where it should be and asks how to cross it.
  • The rules governing a change of focalisation — Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift). §4.5 treats the handoff as a transition technique with a particular cost profile; the questions of which consciousnesses a novel may enter, and on what terms, belong to Chapter 13.
  • Transitions backwards in time — Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback). The cutaway is a transition, and everything in §6 applies to it, but it incurs an additional and quite different cost (the interest deficit), and its scholarly frame is Genette on anachrony rather than on duration.
  • The book’s first transition, from the world into the text — Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), and, where a prologue intervenes, Chapter 10 (The Prologue). An opening has no near side; it is a re-anchoring with no ellipsis to manage.
  • The final transition, out of the text — Chapter 16 (The Ending).
  • Typesetting and design practice as such. §6.6 treats typographic signals as narrative signals; the production questions — which ornament a house uses, how breaks are handled in ebook reflow — are noted where they bear on meaning and otherwise left aside.
