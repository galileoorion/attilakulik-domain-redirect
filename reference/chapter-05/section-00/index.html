Chapter 5 · The Transition

The Transition

Continue in Core

Crossing the Gap: The Managed Ellipsis, the Anchor, and the Orientation Budget

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 5. Part II: The Joins.

The first chapter of Part II treats Genette’s ellipsis and measures of duration at the seam between units (Chapter 1 sets out his categories as a decision about the speed of a unit). Chapter 5 handles how a gap is crossed; Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) handles where the gap is placed; Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) handles who is on the other side. The orientation budget and the anchor–drift distinction set out here are used by all three, and again by Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback).

All illustrative passages are quoted briefly for the purposes of criticism and study; full citations appear in §14. Where a work was not written in English, the translator is named, and the chapter observes the distinction the series maintains throughout: structural claims often survive translation more readily than texture claims.

Chapter Abstract

A transition is the narrative material that carries a reader across a gap in time, place or vantage. It is not the white space; the white space is only a signal that a gap exists. The transition is what the prose does on either side of that signal to get a reader from one condition of orientation to another — and it may be a paragraph long, a clause long, or nothing at all.

The chapter’s working model has two questions: is there elapsed story time to manage, and how is the reader re-anchored? When story time is skipped, the transition manages an ellipsis and an arrival. A simultaneous shift of place or viewpoint still needs anchoring, but it is not an ellipsis in Genette’s sense.

The chapter proceeds in four movements:

  1. Theory — five strands. Genette’s account of narrative duration supplies the frame, with his distinctions between explicit, implicit and hypothetical ellipsis doing real analytical work; Catherine Emmott’s contextual frames supply an account of what an anchor does; the event-indexing model of Zwaan, Langston and Graesser, and Zwaan and Radvansky’s synthesis, show that readers track several dimensions, while processing effects vary by dimension and study; Sternberg supplies the theory of exposition distributed across gaps; and a film strand — Bordwell and Thompson on continuity editing, Eisenstein on montage — is admitted and then handled critically, because the uncritical import of “cut to” thinking can cause errors in prose. A historical strand compares the unmarked hard cut with the explicit narratorial handovers found in many eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novels.
  2. Taxonomy — the five techniques (hard cut, time-stamp, match-cut, summary bridge, POV or location handoff), each anatomised by its textual carriers, its under- and over-delivery and its published illustrations; then a proposed sixth, the Bridging Object, argued for and argued against.
  3. Method — the principle of multiplicity applied to connective tissue; a full account of the anchor, including a taxonomy of twelve anchor types and an account of drift as a controlled effect; a treatment of how gaps are signalled typographically and what each signal implies about size; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol.
  4. Application — twelve failure patterns, each with a published counterexample; eight close readings, with “Time Passes” from To the Lighthouse as the centrepiece; a revision case worked through the series’ household canon; and a full apparatus of exercises, glossary, sources and further reading.

The chapter’s governing claim is that a transition can be examined as a budget problem as well as a style problem: every gap requires some quantity of orientation, that quantity varies enormously from gap to gap, and nearly all transition failure is a mis-estimate of the requirement in one direction or the other. Its governing qualification, developed at §3.7 and again in the Coda, is that the budget is a metaphor — a serviceable one for revision, and one that several of the finest transitions in fiction deliberately overspend, or decline to pay at all.

A note on the examples. The close readings are drawn chiefly from the nineteenth- and twentieth-century novel; the Core chapter adds post-2000 fiction and screen cases, listed in §14.6. A concordance mapping each concept to the novel that best displays it appears at §10.7.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Distinguish the transition from the scene break, and explain why conflating the narrative content with the typographic signal is, in this chapter’s experience, a common conceptual error in workshop discussion of joins.
  • Distinguish a transition with an ellipsis from a simultaneous shift, and classify an actual ellipsis as explicit, implicit or hypothetical, definite or indefinite.
  • Explain, with reference to Emmott’s contextual frames and to the event-indexing literature, what an anchor does cognitively and why the cost of a compound shift exceeds the cost of a simple one.
  • Select deliberately among six transition techniques rather than defaulting to the time-stamp, and justify the selection against what the specific gap requires.
  • Identify the anchor in any published transition, name its type, and measure the delay before it arrives.
  • Distinguish drift — a delayed anchor used as a controlled effect — from the orphaned cut, using the criterion that the anchor still arrives, late and on purpose.
  • Apply the ten-test diagnostic protocol to a draft transition, and the transition inventory to a whole manuscript, producing a prioritised revision plan.
  • Evaluate the limits of the orientation-budget model, including its dependence on twentieth-century typographic convention and on an economy of reader attention that several major novels refuse.

How to Use This Chapter

Reader Route Purpose
Student in a seminar §§1–6 in order, then §9 and the exercises at §12.1 Conceptual grounding, close reading, then guided practice
Writer in revision §7 (diagnostics) → §8 (failures) → §6.3 (anchor types) → §11 (checklist) Fast triage of a live draft, then a repertoire to substitute from
Teacher or workshop leader §4, 6.3, 8 for material; §9.9 for a worked revision; §12.1 for assignments; §12.3 rubric A ready-made teaching sequence with a model revision case

A caution before beginning. This chapter contains taxonomies, tests and a checklist. None of them is a formula. They are instruments for noticing, and their proper output is a question. A protocol that returns the answer “this transition fails test one” has done nothing until the writer asks whether the book wants it to.

One further caution, specific to this subject. Transitions are the least visible craft object in the novel. A reader who notices a transition has usually noticed a fault; a reader who notices nothing has been carried across a gap by writing that worked. This makes the material hard to teach from positive examples, because the successful instances are the ones that leave no impression. The chapter compensates by spending unusual time on failure (§8) and on transitions that are deliberately visible (§9), and the student should be warned that both are unrepresentative of ordinary practice. Most good transitions in most good novels are boring, brief and correct.
