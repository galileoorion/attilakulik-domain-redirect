Chapter 5 · The Transition

4. The Techniques

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The taxonomy below names six recognisable ways of spending the orientation budget. Its value is selective: it converts the undifferentiated instruction write a transition into a choice among options with known costs, and it makes the commonest transition fault — reaching for the same instrument at every gap — visible as a choice rather than invisible as a habit.

Three cautions about its status.

  • The categories describe strategies, not sentences. A single join often braids two: a time-stamp that is also a match-cut, a summary bridge that ends in a handoff. §4.8 treats compounding.
  • They are not ranked. The hard cut is not more sophisticated than the time-stamp. It is cheaper at the join and more expensive at the arrival, and there are gaps for which it is the wrong answer.
  • They are misused as a checklist of things to include. The taxonomy answers which one, never how many.

Each technique is set out with: what it is; why it matters; its textual carriers — the specific places in the prose where it lives; discussion; its characteristic under-delivery and over-delivery; and illustrations from published fiction.

4.1 The Hard Cut

What it is: a gap crossed with no bridging language whatever. The unit ends; white space or a chapter number intervenes; the next unit begins inside its own material, and does the whole orientation job itself, in its own first sentences, while also getting on with its own business.

Why it matters: the hard cut is the only technique that spends nothing at the join. Everything it costs is paid on the far side, folded into sentences that are doing other work. Where it succeeds, the reader crosses a gap without registering that they have crossed anything — which is why it is now the default in contemporary fiction, and why its failures are so common: a technique that costs nothing at the point of use invites use at gaps that cannot afford it.

Textual carriers

Carrier What it does Example of the form
The launching line The last sentence before the gap, which names the destination or the action about to be undertaken She drove to the shop instead, and went straight for the register drawer.
The first noun after the gap Supplies place or object, and is usually the anchor The drawer came free on the third try.
The first proper name Supplies protagonist and, by implication, vantage Any name in the first clause
Verb tense and aspect Signals whether time has passed and how much Pluperfect for a filled interval; simple past for continuity
A resumed referent An object or action already in play, requiring no reintroduction The drawer; the coat; the key
Physical evidence of elapsed time Light, weather, healing, dust, season The light had gone round to the other side of the room.

Discussion

The hard cut is misdescribed in most craft advice as an absence of transition. It is better understood as a transfer of the orientation cost from the join to the opening sentence of the new unit, and it succeeds or fails on whether that sentence can bear the additional load without buckling.

Two conditions predict success, and both are checkable.

Condition one: the launching line has done preparatory work. A hard cut into a scene the reader has been told to expect costs almost nothing, because the frame has been pre-built. Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) establishes the mechanism: a Decision beat that names a place, an object or a person is a transition instrument as much as a character instrument. She would open the register drawer installs a destination; the cut then delivers what the reader is already reaching for. A hard cut into an unannounced destination is a different and much more expensive operation, and the two should not be given the same name in revision notes.

Condition two: the dimension count is low. Per §3.3, a hard cut that changes one or two dimensions is comfortable. A hard cut that changes four is the whiplash handoff (§8.5) in embryo. This yields a simple heuristic that survives most cases:

Hard-cut across one dimension freely; across two with a clear anchor; across three only when the new frame is one the reader already holds; across four almost never, unless disorientation is the effect you want.

The technique has a second, less obvious use: the jolt as content. A cut can enact discontinuity rather than merely execute it. A book about a fractured life, an interrupted history, an unassimilable event may cut hard precisely so that the reader experiences the break as a break. When this is what is happening, the disorientation is not a cost to be minimised; it is the thing purchased. The diagnostic question is always the one from §6.5: does the anchor arrive late and on purpose, or not at all?

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the orphaned cut (§8.1). No anchor in the first several lines; the reader continues to read inside the abandoned frame, and the resulting misreading has to be repaired at cost.
  • Over: hard cuts used at every gap regardless of dimension count, producing a manuscript that reads as a series of fragments with no articulation between them. This is over-delivery of the technique, not of words; the symptom is a reader who cannot say how much time the book covers.

Illustrations

Faulkner, The Sound and the Fury (1929). The novel’s four sections are hard cuts of the most extreme kind — a new consciousness, a new date, a new prose system, with no bridging sentence and no explanation of the relation between them. Within Benjy’s section the cuts are harder still: the narration moves between 1898 and 1928 without warning, keyed by a smell, a word, a fence. Faulkner’s italics are an inconsistent and famously unreliable guide. The novel is the chapter’s standing counterexample (§8.1) and is analysed at §9.5.

Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925). The novel has no chapters. Its only segmentation is white space, and its cuts between consciousnesses are frequently made without any break at all — the narration crosses from one mind to another inside a continuous paragraph, using a shared object as the pivot (§4.6, §9.2). Where Woolf does use a break, the new section is anchored within a clause, usually by a proper name.

Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878). The chapter-level hard cut between the Anna and Levin threads is executed without a syllable of bridging: a chapter ends in Petersburg and the next begins on Levin’s estate. Tolstoy’s anchoring is invariably immediate — a name in the first sentence, a place in the second — and the technique is so unobtrusive that most readers never notice that the novel’s largest structural device is being operated in plain sight. §9.4 treats it.

4.2 The Time-Stamp

What it is: an explicit statement of elapsed time or of temporal position, placed at or near the head of the new unit. Three days later. In April. By the time the shop reopened. The following Tuesday, at nine.

Why it matters: the time-stamp is the cheapest reliable orientation in the language. In Genette’s terms it converts an implicit ellipsis into an explicit and usually a definite one (§3.1), which removes at a stroke the reader’s largest single source of uncertainty at a gap. It is also, for that reason, the technique most reached for by default, and the one whose overuse produces the most recognisable manuscript-level symptom (§8.3).

Textual carriers

Carrier Form Note
Relative interval Three days later; a fortnight after that The default; measures from the last thing narrated
Absolute date 14 March; the following spring; 1931 Common in historical and multi-thread fiction; often set as a heading
Event-relative After the funeral; the week the shop reopened; once the rain stopped Anchors to a known event rather than a number: cheaper, and characterising
Clock time At ten past four Implies a world in which the hour matters — procedural, military, medical
Seasonal or liturgical By Michaelmas; that Advent; before the first frost Carries a culture as well as a date
Physiological When the bruise had gone yellow; by her seventh month Time measured on a body; almost always superior to a number
Iterative All that autumn she went in on Thursdays Crosses a stretch of time while establishing habit (§3.1)
Typographic heading London, March 1943 Paratextual; carries a genre claim (§6.6, §10.6)

Discussion

The time-stamp’s reputation among writing teachers is worse than it deserves, and the reason is a confusion between the instrument and its abuse. The instrument is excellent. Three days later answers, in three words, the question that generates most reader backtracking, and answers it before the reader has had time to form it.

What earns the technique its bad name is defaulting: reaching for the numerical relative interval at every gap, including those where the interval is meaningless or where the far side would have anchored itself. The symptom is a manuscript in which every section begins with a temporal adverbial and no other kind of orientation is ever attempted.

The remedy is not to ban the technique but to exploit its varieties, which the table above sets out and which drafts almost never use. Note in particular the difference between a numerical and an event-relative stamp:

Version Words What it delivers
Three days later, she went back to the house. 9 Time. Nothing else.
The day after the funeral, she went back to the house. 11 Time; a death; a reason to go; the weight of the house

The second is longer and cheaper, because the two extra words are not spent on orientation at all: they are spent on the plot, and the orientation comes free. This is multiplicity (§5) applied to the least promising material in the book.

Two further notes. First, the definiteness question is a real decision, not a default. Some weeks later is not a weaker version of three weeks later; it is a claim that the exact interval does not matter, which is sometimes true and, when true, saves the reader a piece of bookkeeping they would otherwise have to maintain. Second, the time-stamp is under-used at large gaps and over-used at small ones. Drafts routinely stamp a cut of two hours and leave a cut of two years to be inferred, which is backwards: small intervals are inferable from context, large ones are not.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: an indefinite stamp where the reader needed a definite one — later, some time after — leaving them to maintain an open variable through the following scene.
  • Over: the metronome transition (§8.3): the same idiom at every gap, flattening the book’s rhythm into a single register. Also the over-qualified stamp, which uses the time-stamp position to deliver a summary the gap did not require (§8.2).

Illustrations

Stoker, Dracula (1897). Every transition in the novel is a dated heading — journal entries, letters, telegrams, ship’s logs, each with a date and often a time. The metronome is the form. What saves it from monotony is that the sources vary while the stamp does not: the reader’s orientation problem at each join is not when but whose document is this, and how much does this witness know. §8.3 uses the novel as its counterexample.

García Márquez, One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967; trans. Gregory Rabassa). The novel’s time-stamps are exact to the point of parody and are used to make duration unbelievable rather than clear: it rains, in the celebrated formulation, for four years, eleven months and two days. The precision is the joke and also the method — a definite interval, stated with the authority of a chronicle, whose content is impossible. §9.7 treats the technique as a system.

Defoe and the journal tradition. The eighteenth-century fiction of the found document — Robinson Crusoe’s journal, Pamela’s letters — makes the dated stamp constitutive rather than connective: the date is not a transition device inside a narrative but the organising principle of the artefact the narrative pretends to be. The distinction is worth teaching, because it explains why dated headings in contemporary fiction carry a faint documentary charge whether or not the writer intended one (§6.6).

4.3 The Match-Cut, or Echo Transition

What it is: a gap crossed on a repetition. Something at the end of the outgoing unit recurs, transformed, at the start of the incoming one — a word, an image, an action, a sound, a syntactic shape — so that the join itself carries meaning.

Why it matters: this is the only technique in the taxonomy whose purpose is not orientation. It orients as a by-product; what it buys is an argument. Per §3.5, juxtaposition is a signifying operation: two things placed adjacent produce a third thing that is in neither, and a writer who controls the adjacency controls the third thing.

Textual carriers

Carrier Form Effect
Lexical repetition A distinctive word ends one unit and begins the next The plainest form; risks preciousness if the word is ordinary
Image rhyme Two visually or tactilely similar things The film match cut’s nearest prose relative
Action rhyme The same gesture performed by two people Very strong: characterises both parties at once
Aural rhyme A sound, a bell, a name called Underused in prose; works because sound is not localised
Syntactic rhyme Two units opening with the same construction Formal, cool, patterning; the reader feels design
Ironic inversion The same element, with the opposite valuation The most reliably effective version: the echo comments
Question and answer A question at the close of one unit, an unrelated answer at the head of the next Comic or grim depending on the pairing

Discussion

The match-cut has a specific and unusual risk profile: its failure mode is invisibility, not confusion. A reader who misses the echo has lost nothing they know about; they have simply read two units in sequence. The transition has therefore cost the writer effort and delivered nothing, and the writer — who cannot unsee the connection — has no way of detecting the loss.

This suggests a practical calibration rule, which §7.1 formalises as test 9:

An echo must be detectable on a first reading at ordinary speed by a reader who is not looking for it. An echo detectable only on rereading is a reward for rereading, not a transition.

The rule is a floor, not a ceiling. A novel may layer echoes at several depths: one that lands immediately, another that lands on the second reading. What it may not do is rely on the deep one to do the connective work.

Three things raise detectability without raising ostentation.

  1. Proximity. The echo must sit at the very end of one unit and the very beginning of the other. An echo separated by three intervening sentences is not a transition; it is a motif.
  2. Distinctiveness. Rhyming on an ordinary noun (door, hand, water) will not register. Rhyming on an unusual one, or on an ordinary one used unusually, will.
  3. Difference. The strongest echoes are not repetitions but transformations: the same object in a changed condition, the same gesture with a changed meaning. Pure repetition reads as a stutter; repetition with a difference reads as an argument.

The technique also has an unexamined structural cost. An echo asserts a relation between two units, and the reader will look for the relation to be honoured. A match-cut between two scenes that turn out to have nothing to do with one another is a promise defaulted on — the transition-scale version of the unredeemed detail described in Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail).

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the precious match-cut (§8.4). The connection is too faint to register, and the transition therefore does no work at all while the writer believes it is doing double.
  • Over: the match-cut at every join, which converts the book’s articulation into a display of ingenuity. Emphasis is relational; when every seam is significant, no seam is. There is also a specific over-delivery in which the echo is pointed at — a narrator who explains the rhyme has destroyed it.

Illustrations

Dickens, Bleak House (1853). Dickens uses the rhetorical match-cut with a directness modern practice has abandoned, and the most famous instance is the narrator’s question about connexion — what connexion, he asks in Chapter 16, can there be between the place in Lincolnshire, the house in town, and the outlaw with the broom on the crossing. The transition names its own device: the novel’s method is precisely the discovery of connexions between people separated by class and geography, and the narrator turns the mechanics of scene-joining into the statement of the book’s thesis. This is a match-cut executed at maximum volume, and it demonstrates that the technique’s ceiling of ostentation is much higher than contemporary convention allows.

Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925). The motor car and the aeroplane are the celebrated cases and are treated at §9.2. Note here only that Woolf’s echoes are almost always shared perceptual events rather than verbal repetitions, which raises detectability enormously: a reader does not have to notice a word to notice that two people are looking at the same thing.

Joyce, Ulysses (1922). The novel is built out of echoes across enormous distances — a phrase in the morning returning at midnight, the throwaway handbill drifting down the Liffey through “Wandering Rocks” (§9.3). Joyce’s practice is instructive as a limit: many of the echoes are undetectable on a first reading, and the novel accepts this, because it is written for rereading. A book that is not may not make the same assumption.

4.4 The Summary Bridge

What it is: connective narration that covers the interval itself, telling the reader in compressed form what happened between the two units. For the next three weeks nothing was decided. Dov came in on Saturdays; the stock did not move; twice their mother rang and did not leave a message.

Why it matters: the summary bridge is the only technique that can put content in the gap. The other five leave the interval empty and manage the crossing; this one narrates the crossing. Per Sternberg (§3.4), the position immediately after a break is the natural home of distributed exposition, and the summary bridge is the instrument that occupies it.

Textual carriers

Carrier Form Note
Singulative summary Plain compressed report of a bounded stretch Fast; nothing is felt
Iterative summary One narration of repeated events (every Thursday) Establishes the normal; characterises; crosses indefinite time
Enumerated interval A short list of what the interval contained Rhythmically strong; gives the reader a sense of duration by accumulation
Negative summary What did not happen (nothing was decided; nobody rang) Very efficient; conveys duration and mood together
Free indirect summary The interval reported in the character’s idiom and evaluation Discharges character while discharging time
Consequence-first summary The interval’s outcome stated, then briefly explained Puts the useful information first

Discussion

The summary bridge is the technique with the worst reputation and the strongest defence. Its reputation comes from a real failure — the bridge that reports at length on an interval nobody wondered about (§8.2) — and from a general contemporary preference for scene over summary that Chapter 1 §6.5 shows to be a period convention rather than a principle.

Its defence has three parts.

(1) Some intervals contain necessary material that does not deserve a scene. This is the ordinary case. Three weeks in which the shop did not sell, the solicitor did not reply and nobody spoke to their mother is material a reader needs and that would be inert dramatised.

(2) Summary is the only mode that can render duration. A reader’s sense that time has passed does not come from being told a date; it comes from being made to traverse something. This is why the negative and the enumerated forms are so effective: they give the interval a shape. A hard cut over three weeks tells the reader three weeks passed; a four-sentence bridge makes them feel the three weeks, at a cost of four sentences.

(3) Summary is the natural home of the narrator’s evaluation. A bridge is the one place in a scenic novel where the narration is licensed to say what things amounted to. In a book with any narratorial presence at all, the bridges are where that presence lives.

The technique’s real difficulty is not length but hand-over: a summary bridge must stop, and the moment it stops is a second transition, from summary duration into scene duration. Bridges that fail usually fail here — they compress an interval competently and then dissolve into the new scene without a clear point of entry, so that the reader cannot tell when the general has become the particular. The fix is mechanical and reliable: end the bridge on a specific occasion. …and twice their mother rang and did not leave a message. On the Thursday, Elena came in at four and found the drawer open. The last sentence of the summary is the first sentence of the scene.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: an interval that mattered, crossed with nothing, so that the reader later encounters consequences whose causes were skipped. The false ellipsis (§8.8) in its unintentional form.
  • Over: the throat-clearing bridge (§8.2) — a paragraph of interval before the new unit’s engine starts, spending the reader’s attention on material they would have inferred. Also the recap bridge (§8.6), which summarises material the reader has already read.

Illustrations

Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939), chapter 12. The Route 66 chapter is a summary bridge raised to the scale of a chapter — the road named as the mother road, the road of flight, and the migration rendered as an aggregate rather than as an episode. It crosses two thousand miles and an indefinite stretch of time, and it does so while making an argument about the collective nature of the event. It is also, formally, a travel montage (§8.9), and it is the counterexample that pattern requires.

Austen. Austen’s transitions between the set-piece scenes of her novels are frequently summary bridges of two or three sentences, executed with such speed that readers do not register them as a technique — a winter passes, an acquaintance ripens, a family removes to Bath. The instruction available to a student is exact: summarise what the reader can be trusted to imagine, and dramatise only what will surprise them.

Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878). The Levin chapters use extended iterative summary for the estate’s agricultural year, which is what makes the mowing scene land when it comes: the reader has been given the habit of the farm before being given an occasion on it. This is the iterative bridge doing structural work rather than connective work, and it is the answer to any student who believes summary is what a novel does when it cannot be bothered.

4.5 The Point-of-View or Location Handoff

What it is: a gap that changes whose consciousness the reader occupies, or where the reader is, or both — the standard connective device of any novel running more than one thread.

Why it matters: the handoff is the technique that makes multi-thread narration possible, and it is the most expensive item in the taxonomy. §3.3 supplies the reason: a handoff typically shifts protagonist, space, time and intentionality at once, and shift costs are largely additive. The reader is not doing one difficult thing; they are doing four ordinary things simultaneously, which is why the technique’s failure is a distinctive kind of exhaustion rather than a distinctive kind of confusion.

Textual carriers

Carrier What it does Cost
The proper name in the first clause Establishes protagonist before anything else can be misassigned Almost none; the strongest available handoff anchor
A chapter or section title naming the viewpoint Declares the shift paratextually, before the prose starts None to the prose; commits the book to a formal pattern
Free indirect diction The new consciousness announces itself by how the sentences are made Requires strongly differentiated voices to work
A changed register of attention What is noticed changes, and the change identifies the noticer Slow; readers need several sentences
The shared object Both threads touch the same thing (§4.6) Cheap and elegant; not always available
An explicit narratorial handover While this was happening at the shop, Dov was… Fast, unambiguous, and now period-marked

Discussion

Three practical points follow from the additive-cost finding, and together they constitute most of what can be taught about handoffs.

(1) Reduce the dimension count wherever the book permits. A handoff that changes protagonist but keeps time and place — two characters in the same room, the narration moving from one to the other — is cheap, and is the technique of Middlemarch and Mrs Dalloway. A handoff that changes protagonist, place and time is the technique of most multi-thread commercial fiction, and it costs three times as much. If the shift can be made at a moment when the threads share a place, a time or an object, take it.

(2) Anchor with a name, immediately. Of all the anchor types tabulated at §6.3, the proper name is the only one that resolves the protagonist dimension unambiguously in a single word, and the protagonist dimension is the one on which a reader’s misassignment is most costly to repair. A handoff that opens on a pronoun is asking the reader to hold an unbound variable at the exact moment they have the least capacity to do so.

(3) Declare the pattern early. This is Chapter 13’s territory and is stated here only in its transition-side form: a reader who knows the book alternates has a cheaper frame switch at every subsequent join, because they are performing frame recall rather than frame construction (§3.2). The first handoff is expensive; the twelfth, in a book with a legible pattern, is nearly free. This is the strongest structural argument for establishing multi-viewpoint structure in the first chapters rather than at page ninety.

The handoff also has a pacing use, which needs stating carefully. Cutting away from a thread at its point of maximum tension defers resolution and converts it into pull. This is the feuilleton technique, and it is treated in full in Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break), because it is a decision about where to cut rather than about how to cross. The transition-side note is that a deferral cut raises the anchoring stakes on the far side: a reader who has just been pulled away from something they wanted will be less patient with a slow arrival, not more.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the reader cannot tell whose head they are in for several paragraphs, or cannot tell whether the new thread is happening after the old one or alongside it (§8.10).
  • Over: the whiplash handoff (§8.5) — cuts so frequent that no thread accumulates enough continuous time for tension to build. Also the over-declared handoff, in which each section opens with a paragraph re-establishing a character the reader remembers perfectly well (§8.6).

Illustrations

Dickens, Bleak House (1853). The alternation between the third-person present-tense panoramic narrator and Esther Summerson’s retrospective first person is the most radical handoff system in the nineteenth-century English novel, and Dickens executes it almost entirely without bridging language, relying on the chapter division and on an absolute contrast of voice. Esther’s first chapter opens with a sentence that anchors person, vantage, medium and character at once — she has, she says, a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write her portion of these pages, for she knows she is not clever. §5.2 anatomises it; §9.8 treats the system.

Joyce, Ulysses (1922), “Wandering Rocks”. Nineteen sections, nineteen handoffs, with interpolated fragments from other sections cutting across each — the most concentrated handoff sequence in modernist fiction and the clearest available demonstration of what simultaneity costs. §9.3.

Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878). The novel’s two threads are handed off at chapter boundaries with no bridging and near-instantaneous anchoring. What makes the technique invisible is that Tolstoy varies the interval between handoffs according to the state of each thread rather than by rule; the alternation is never mechanical (§9.4).

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Bridging Object

This section proposes an addition to the taxonomy. Chapter 8 (Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture) adopts it at the scale of a book as a means of convergence by identity between strands, on condition that the object’s condition or custody changes between its appearances, which answers the identity objection at that scale (its Full Treatment, section 3.6).

The five techniques above divide the field by what the transition spends its words on: nothing (hard cut), the clock (time-stamp), a resemblance (match-cut), the interval (summary bridge), or a new vantage (handoff). One recurring practice fits none of them cleanly, is used constantly by good writers, and is at present taught as a special case of the match-cut, which it is not.

The bridging object. A physical thing that exists continuously on both sides of a gap, and whose presence in the new unit does the anchoring — the coat on the hook, the ledger, the key, the car in the street, the handbill on the water. The reader is oriented not by being told where they are but by recognising something they already know.

Why it is not a match-cut

The distinction is exact: a match-cut joins two units by resemblance; a bridging object joins them by identity.

Match-cut Bridging object
Relation across the gap Two different things that rhyme One thing, continuous
What the reader does Notices a similarity, infers a comparison Recognises a referent, retrieves a frame
What it delivers An argument about the two units Orientation, plus whatever the object means
Failure mode The rhyme is not detected (§8.4) The object is not distinctive enough to be recognised
Cognitive operation Interpretation Frame recall (§3.2)

The last row is the load-bearing one. A match-cut requires the reader to interpret; a bridging object requires them only to recognise, which is a faster, more reliable and much cheaper mental operation. This is why the bridging object is the most economical transition available when it is available at all: it discharges the orientation bill by triggering a retrieval rather than by supplying information.

Textual carriers

Carrier Form Note
The definite article The coat, not a coat The chief signal: the definite article asserts that the reader already knows this thing
Absence of description The object named and not re-described Re-describing a resumed object un-primes it; the reader wonders whether it is a different one
A changed condition The same object, altered Delivers elapsed time in the same word that delivers place
A transfer of custody The object in a new pair of hands Delivers a handoff and an anchor together
A physical trajectory The object moving through the world between scenes Makes the object itself the connective tissue

Discussion

Three properties determine whether an object can bear the load.

  1. Distinctiveness. The chair will not anchor; the chair with the mended leg will. The object must have been particularised earlier, which means bridging objects have to be planted — a dependency on Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) that gives that chapter’s argument a structural payoff.
  2. Persistence. An object that appears once and returns once is a motif. An object that appears repeatedly across a novel becomes a piece of narrative furniture the reader can be oriented by at any point.
  3. Semantic loading. An object accumulates meaning each time it is used, so a bridging object delivers, along with the orientation, whatever it has come to mean. This is the technique’s real prize, and the reason it belongs in the taxonomy: it is the only transition technique that gets better with repetition, where every other one degrades (§8.3).

The technique also has a use at the largest scale. A novel can hand an object from thread to thread as its principal connective device, so that the object rather than the calendar organises the book. This is the structure of a certain kind of nineteenth-century plot (the will, the letter, the diamond) and of a good deal of twentieth-century experimental fiction.

Illustrations

Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925). The motor car with its drawn blinds, moving up Bond Street, is a bridging object in the strict sense: not a resemblance between two scenes but one vehicle, physically continuous, passing through a succession of consciousnesses that are joined by having seen it. The aeroplane skywriting over London does the same at a larger scale. Woolf’s device is routinely described as a match-cut, and describing it that way misses what is actually happening — the reader is not comparing Clarissa’s response with Septimus’s, or not only; they are being handed from one to the other by a thing that is in both. §9.2.

Joyce, Ulysses (1922). The throwaway handbill announcing Elijah, dropped into the Liffey and tracked down the river across sections of “Wandering Rocks”, is the purest published instance of the technique: an object with a trajectory, used to stitch a chapter whose subject is the simultaneity of a city.

Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927). In “Time Passes”, the house itself is the bridging object across a ten-year ellipsis. Every human being leaves; the narration stays with the rooms, the airs, the shawl on the boar’s skull, the damp in the books. The object that persists is what makes a ten-year gap navigable at all (§9.1).

Dickens. Bleak House’s system of connexions is largely object-borne — a document, a copybook hand, a mourning brooch — and the novel’s plot is in one sense a chain of bridging objects passing between social worlds that have no other contact.

The argument against

A textbook proposing an addition to a taxonomy owes the reader the case for rejecting it. There are three arguments, and the second is strong.

(1) It is a carrier, not a technique. Every transition needs an anchor, and §6.3 lists twelve anchor types, one of which is the resumed object. On this view the bridging object is not a sixth way of crossing a gap but a way of anchoring that any of the five techniques may use: a hard cut can land on an object, a summary bridge can end on one, a handoff can pivot on one. Admitting it to §4 confuses the level of the taxonomy.

(2) The identity/resemblance distinction may not survive scrutiny. The claim that a bridging object is the same object is a claim about the fictional world, not about the reading experience. What the reader actually encounters is a second occurrence of a noun phrase; whether the referent is numerically identical or merely similar is a fact they infer, and they infer it by exactly the interpretive route the match-cut uses. If so, the two categories collapse, and the elegant table above is describing a difference in the story rather than a difference in the technique.

(3) There is no principled stopping point. If a recurring object earns a category, why not a recurring sound, a recurring phrase, a recurring weather, a recurring piece of music? Each has the same structure. The taxonomy would grow without limit and lose the compactness that makes it usable.

This chapter adopts the sixth technique — on the grounds that the cognitive operation is different (recall rather than interpretation), that the failure modes are different, and that the practical advice differs enough to be worth separating: plant it, particularise it, use the definite article, do not re-describe it. But the second objection is not answered here, and it should not be.

Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class: is the bridging object a transition technique, or an anchor type dressed as one?

4.8 Must a transition use only one technique?

No, and the taxonomy is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

(1) Compounding is normal and usually good. A great many published transitions braid two techniques: a time-stamp that is also an echo (Three days later the drawer was open again); a summary bridge that ends on a bridging object; a handoff anchored by a shared perceptual event. Compounding is the ordinary form of multiplicity at the join (§5).

(2) But compounding a technique with its own redundancy is the double anchor. A time-stamp followed by a paragraph of summary covering the same interval orients twice (§8.7). The test is whether the second element adds a dimension the first did not resolve.

(3) Some gaps need no technique at all. A paragraph break between two beats of a continuous scene is a gap of ninety seconds on one dimension. Nothing is required. Drafts that treat every white space as an occasion for craft produce a manuscript that is exhausting in a way its author cannot locate.

(4) The right question is never “which technique is best” but “what does this gap cost”. §7.1 test 2 makes the costing procedure explicit. The taxonomy’s job is to supply options once the bill is known.
