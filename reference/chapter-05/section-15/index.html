Chapter 5 · The Transition

15. Further Reading

Continue in Core

Annotated, grouped by what each source is good for. Starred items are the recommended first three.

15.1 On duration, ellipsis and the shape of narrative time

  • ★ Genette, Gérard. Narrative Discourse (1972; trans. 1980). The indispensable book for this chapter and the most useful single work in the series. The chapter on duration is short; read it with a novel open. The classification of ellipses into explicit, implicit and hypothetical is worth more to a working writer than any craft manual’s treatment of transitions.
  • Ricoeur, Paul. Time and Narrative (1983–85). The philosophical account of narrative’s relation to time. Long and demanding; the argument that narrative is the form in which temporal experience becomes intelligible is the deep justification for treating gaps as a craft object.
  • Mendilow, A. A. Time and the Novel (1952). Older, more literary-historical, and readable. Good on how the English novel’s handling of time changed between Richardson and the modernists.
  • Chatman, Seymour. Story and Discourse (1978). Clear on the story/discourse distinction on which the whole idea of an ellipsis depends, and useful on gaps.

15.2 On what readers do at a join

  • ★ Emmott, Catherine. Narrative Comprehension: A Discourse Perspective (1997). The best available account of what an anchor does, and the closest fit between theory and craft in this textbook. Contextual frames, frame switching, frame recall and frame repair are all here, with textual analysis rather than laboratory abstraction.
  • Zwaan and Radvansky, “Situation Models in Language Comprehension and Memory” (1998). A review article on situation models and the dimensions readers track. Read alongside Zwaan, Langston and Graesser (1995) for the event-indexing model itself and Zwaan et al. (1998) for reading-time experiments; none supplies a fixed cost per dimension.
  • Zacks, Jeffrey M., and Barbara Tversky, “Event Structure in Perception and Conception” (2001). For the argument that segmentation is a general feature of how experience is processed, not a literary artefact. Useful corrective to the assumption that boundaries are simply costs.
  • Iser, Wolfgang. The Act of Reading (1978). The theory of gaps and blanks from the reader-response side: what the reader is doing in the space the text does not fill.

15.3 On exposition and what may be withheld

  • ★ Sternberg, Meir. Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978). Formalist, demanding, and without a rival. The suspense/curiosity distinction reorganises how a writer thinks about what to put in a gap. Chapters 1–3 if not the whole.
  • Rabinowitz, Peter J. Before Reading (1987). The rules of notice explain why material placed at the head of a unit is read as significant — the mechanism behind the throat-clearing bridge’s second, hidden cost.

15.4 On segmentation, chapters and the history of the mark on the page

  • Dames, Nicholas. The Chapter: A Segmented History from Antiquity to the Twenty-First Century (2023). The standard modern study, and the corrective to any assumption that our units of division are natural. Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) uses it heavily; read at least the introduction before accepting §6.6’s typography table as anything other than a description of current practice.
  • Genette, Gérard. Paratexts (1997). For headings, intertitles and date-lines considered as apparatus rather than as narrative.

15.5 On the film analogy, used carefully

  • Bordwell, David, and Kristin Thompson. Film Art: An Introduction. The standard textbook, and the clearest available account of continuity editing. Read the editing chapter for the vocabulary, then read §3.5 of this chapter again for what does not transfer.
  • Eisenstein, Sergei. Film Form (trans. Leyda, 1949). For montage as an argument rather than as a joining device. The claim that juxtaposition produces a third meaning present in neither term is the theoretical parent of the match-cut.

15.6 Craft manuals, used critically

  • Burroway, Janet. Writing Fiction. The standard university textbook, with the most useful selection of quoted transitions in any craft book.
  • Le Guin, Ursula K. Steering the Craft. Excellent on sentence rhythm and on point-of-view movement; her exercises on “crowding and leaping” are, in effect, exercises in transition.
  • Swain, Dwight V. Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965) and Bickham, Jack M. Scene & Structure (1993). Mid-century American commercial pedagogy, mechanistic in idiom and structurally sound. Their treatment of the decision that ends a sequel is the origin of this chapter’s launching line.

15.7 Within this textbook

  • Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) — §6, the scene–summary continuum, and the twelve modes of narrating an event. Read it before this chapter’s §3.1.
  • Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) — the causal chain, and the Decision beat that is the strongest transition-preparation device in the repertoire.
  • Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) — planting and particularisation, without which the bridging object (§4.6) is unavailable.
  • Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) — where the gap goes, the placement question this chapter brackets; and the fullest treatment of serial forms.
  • Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) — who is on the other side; the rules governing a change of consciousness, of which §4.5 treats only the transition-side costs.
  • Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) — the transition backwards, and Genette on anachrony.
  • Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) — the re-anchoring with no ellipsis to manage; the rules of notice; the account of throat-clearing from which §8.2 is derived.
