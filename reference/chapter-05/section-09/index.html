Chapter 5 · The Transition

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Eight close readings and a revision case. The analytical apparatus is constant: identify the technique (§4), locate and type the anchor (§6.3), count the dimensions shifted (§3.3), and ask what the transition does besides orient (§5).

The selection is deliberately weighted towards violation. As §8 notes, the memorable transitions in fiction are almost all transgressive, and a chapter that illustrated only correct practice would have nothing to look at.

9.1 Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927), Part II — “Time Passes”

This is the centrepiece of the chapter, and it inverts every principle the preceding sections have established. It should be read as the standing refutation of any mechanical use of the orientation budget.

The structural situation. To the Lighthouse is in three parts. Part I, “The Window”, occupies a single September evening at the Ramsays’ house in the Hebrides and runs to roughly half the novel. Part III, “The Lighthouse”, occupies a single morning ten years later, in the same house. Part II, “Time Passes”, is the join: about twenty pages, covering the intervening decade.

By the accounting of §2.2, this is a transition of extraordinary size — a bridge whose length is a substantial fraction of the units it connects. By the accounting of §3.7, it is an absurdity: it costs more than it buys. It is also the most admired passage Woolf wrote, and the reason is that it is not doing what a transition does. It is doing something the chapter’s model has no vocabulary for, and the honest pedagogic move is to say so and then look carefully at what it is doing.

What occupies the foreground. The section’s narrated events are: darkness entering the house; wind moving through the rooms; the shawl on the boar’s skull loosening; damp in the books; a caretaker, Mrs McNab, coming to air the place; the garden encroaching; the house approaching collapse; and, at the end, the house restored and reoccupied. There is no consciousness in the human sense. The focalisation is closer to the house’s than to anyone’s, and the narration’s interest is in the indifference of the material world to the people who furnished it.

What occupies the background. Three deaths, an engagement, a marriage, a childbirth and the First World War. They arrive in square brackets, in subordinate clauses, as interruptions to the account of the weather.

[Mr Ramsay, stumbling along a passage one dark morning, stretched his arms out, but Mrs Ramsay having died rather suddenly the night before, his arms, though stretched out, remained empty.]

Mrs Ramsay — the consciousness at the centre of Part I, the novel’s organising intelligence, the woman whose dinner party occupies eighty pages — dies in a subordinate participial clause inside a bracket, and her death is grammatically a reason for something else: an explanation of why a man’s arms were empty. Prue Ramsay dies later in the section, in an illness connected with childbirth; Andrew Ramsay is killed by a shell in France, his death reported as mercifully instantaneous.

The inversion, stated precisely. Every transition principle in this chapter can be run through the passage and comes out reversed.

Principle (§§3–6) What “Time Passes” does
The transition should be shorter than the units it joins It is a third of the novel
The gap should not contain the important events It contains every important event
The ellipsis should be managed, its contents legible The contents are supplied and then typographically demoted
Orientation should be prompt The reader is never oriented to a human vantage at all
Foreground the story; background the interval Foreground the interval; bracket the story
Multiplicity: the transition should do a second job The transition is doing only a second job; the connective function is incidental

Why it works, in terms the chapter can supply. Three mechanisms are available.

(1) The brackets are a typographic anchor, used against itself. Per §6.6, a mark makes a claim about the material it encloses. Square brackets in English typography signal editorial interpolation — matter inserted by a hand other than the author’s, subordinate to the main text, added for reference. Woolf puts the deaths there. The claim the typography makes is this is marginal, and the reader knows it is not, and the gap between the claim and the truth is where the passage’s power sits. This is a transition device — a signal about the size and status of material — turned into a moral instrument.

(2) The bridging object is the house. Every human being leaves; the narration stays with the rooms. Per §4.6, a bridging object crosses a gap by identity rather than resemblance, and orientation by identity is orientation by recall. The reader is never lost in “Time Passes” despite the total absence of the ordinary anchors, because the place is one they have inhabited for half a novel. The house is the only continuity the passage offers, and it is enough.

(3) The ellipsis is not managed; it is inhabited. This is the point at which the chapter’s model runs out. Woolf does not skip ten years and re-anchor; she narrates the ten years at the only scale at which they can be narrated without a consciousness — the scale of dust, weather and decay — and she does so at a duration that is neither scene, summary nor pause in Genette’s sense, since story time passes enormously while discourse time is substantial and the relation between them is not proportional but rhythmic.

What a student should take from it. Not a technique; the passage is not reproducible. Three transferable observations:

  • The bracket is available. Typographic subordination of important material is a device, and almost nobody uses it. It is one of the few underexploited resources this chapter can point to.
  • The false ellipsis can be made honest by disclosure. Woolf’s gap contains everything, and she tells us. §8.8’s discrimination — a deliberate false ellipsis makes the reader aware the gap was loaded — is derived from this passage.
  • A gap can be given a protagonist that is not a person. Where a novel must cross an interval with no human witness, the question is not “how do I summarise this?” but “who or what was present?” The house was present.

A dissenting note, worth staging. “Time Passes” is not universally admired, and the objection is a transition objection. Some readers find that the section’s beauty is detachable from the novel — that it functions as a prose poem inserted between two social novels, and that the deaths in brackets are a technical coup that purchases its effect by withholding the scenes in which those deaths would have cost something to write. The counter-argument is that the withholding is the subject: the novel is about the disproportion between what a life feels like from inside and what it amounts to from the outside, and dramatising Mrs Ramsay’s death would have destroyed the demonstration. Seminar question 4 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class.

9.2 Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925) — the motor car and the aeroplane

Mrs Dalloway has no chapters. Its only divisions are occasional white-space breaks, and a large proportion of its transitions — between consciousnesses, between decades, between London and Bourton — occur inside continuous paragraphs. The novel is the chapter’s principal example of the frictionless manuscript succeeding (§8.12), and its two most famous devices are the clearest published instances of the bridging object (§4.6).

The motor car. Early in the novel, a car with drawn blinds backfires in Bond Street and then proceeds slowly through the West End. Nobody sees who is inside; the guesses run from the Prime Minister to the Queen. The narration moves from Clarissa to a florist to a crowd to Septimus Warren Smith by following the car. The car is not a metaphor and not a rhyme: it is one vehicle, physically continuous, passing through a series of minds that are connected by nothing except having looked at it.

The aeroplane. Immediately afterwards, an aeroplane skywrites an advertisement over London, and the narration performs the same operation at greater altitude and across a wider social range — from strangers in the park to Septimus, who reads the letters as a message addressed to him.

Why these are transitions and not merely images.

Element Transition function
The object is shared Both the outgoing and the incoming consciousness are attending to the same thing at the same moment, which resolves time and space at no cost
The object moves Its trajectory supplies a direction for the narration to follow, and a reason to arrive where it arrives
The object is public It licenses a shift between strangers, which is otherwise the most expensive handoff available
Interpretation varies What each consciousness makes of the car characterises them; the transition is doing characterisation while it crosses

The last row is where the multiplicity lives. A shared perceptual event is the ideal transition instrument for a novel about the incommensurability of minds, because the same stimulus produces different interiors, and the difference is the book’s argument. Woolf’s handoffs shift one dimension — protagonist — while holding time and space constant, which by §3.3’s accounting makes them the cheapest possible viewpoint transitions. The technical achievement is not the beauty of the writing; it is the structural discovery that a public object reduces a four-dimension handoff to a one-dimension one.

A caution for students imitating it. The device requires a novel whose subject is simultaneity within a shared space. Transplanted into a book whose threads are geographically separate, it becomes contrivance — two characters noticing the same moon on different continents is not a bridging object but a coincidence.

9.3 Joyce, Ulysses (1922) — “Wandering Rocks”

The tenth episode of Ulysses consists of nineteen short sections, each following a different Dubliner through the streets in the same hour of the afternoon, framed by Father Conmee’s walk at the start and the vice-regal cavalcade at the end. Into each section Joyce splices short fragments describing events occurring elsewhere at the same moment — sentences that belong to other sections, interpolated without explanation.

What it is doing, structurally. Three things at once, and it is the only passage in this chapter that does all three.

  1. Handoff at maximum frequency. Nineteen transitions in one episode, each shifting protagonist, space and intentionality. By §3.3 this is the most expensive sequence in the novel, and the expense is the point (§8.5).
  2. Simultaneity, marked. The interpolations are Joyce’s solution to the problem §8.10 describes: prose has no tense for “meanwhile”, so he creates one typographically, by inserting foreign material into a running paragraph. A reader who meets a sentence about a one-legged sailor in the middle of an unrelated scene learns, without being told, that the episode’s time is a single hour and its space a whole city.
  3. Anchoring at maximum speed. Each section opens with a named person, usually with a title and an action: the superior, the very reverend John Conmee, resetting his watch as he comes down the presbytery steps. This is anchor type 1 compounded with types 2, 4 and 5 — a name, a place, an object and an action in a single clause. The episode’s anchoring is faster and more conventional than anything else in the novel, and it has to be, because the shift costs are so high.

The lesson available to a working writer. Not the interpolation device, which is difficult and rarely appropriate, but the relation between the two halves of Joyce’s practice. The more expensive the shift, the more conventional the anchor should be. Joyce is the most formally radical novelist in the language and he opens each of these sections with a name and a place, because he has nothing left to spend.

9.4 Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878) — the Levin and Anna cuts

Anna Karenina runs two principal narratives that intersect socially and barely at all in plot. Tolstoy’s alternation between them is the most accomplished ordinary transition system in the nineteenth-century novel, and it is invisible because it is so consistently correct.

The technique. Chapter-level hard cuts with no bridging language. A chapter ends in Petersburg or Moscow; the next begins on Levin’s estate. There is no meanwhile, no summary of the interval, and — outside the Part divisions — no time-stamp.

The anchoring. Invariably immediate, and almost always a proper name in the first sentence, supported by a place-noun in the first or second. Tolstoy also exploits the social cue (anchor type 10) with unusual precision: Russian naming practice, with its patronymics, diminutives and formal address, identifies not only who is present but the relationship the narration is currently adopting towards them. A reader who knows that Konstantin Dmitrich is Levin in a formal social frame and Kostya is Levin at home has been anchored in a social register as well as in a person, before the sentence is over. This is largely lost in English, whatever the translation — one of the clearer instances of the series’ distinction between structural claims and texture claims.

The rhythm. The interval between visits to each thread varies according to the state of that thread, not by rule. Tolstoy stays with Anna through the crises and leaves Levin for long stretches when his agricultural year has nothing urgent in it; and — the more instructive half — he returns to Levin at moments calculated to comment on what Anna has just done. The alternation is a match-cut executed at the scale of chapters: the reader is not told that Levin’s marriage is a commentary on Anna’s, and the sequencing says it.

The critical disagreement worth naming. Critics have argued since the novel appeared about whether the two plots converge. They meet once, late, briefly. Tolstoy insisted the book had an architecture — famously, that its unity lay not in plot but in an internal connection — and readers have disagreed ever since about whether the transitions between the threads are structural or merely alternating. It is a real disagreement, and it is set as seminar question 5 (§12.2).

9.5 Faulkner, The Sound and the Fury (1929) — the unmarked shift within a section

Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) discusses the novel’s first paragraph as an instance of withheld orientation. The transition-side interest is different and lies inside the Benjy section, where the narration moves between at least half a dozen periods spread over thirty years without any conventional signal.

What triggers a shift. Not a break, a date or a stated recollection, but a sensory or verbal coincidence: a fence, a word, water, a smell, the sound of a name. Benjy has no concept of past and present, so his narration has no tense apparatus for distinguishing them; a stimulus in 1928 delivers 1898 with equal immediacy and no marker of relative time.

What anchors the reader. Faulkner supplies cues, and a first-time reader has to learn to read them:

Cue What it resolves
Which servant is present Period — Versh, T. P. and Luster attend Benjy at successive ages
Caddy’s presence or absence Before or after her departure; the section’s fundamental division
A recurring sensory tag Frame recall: the smell of trees; honeysuckle; the pasture
Italics Intended to mark shifts, applied inconsistently, and unreliable as a guide

The italics are worth a moment because they are a genuine and documented craft failure that a great novel survived. Faulkner used them to mark temporal shifts, could not apply them consistently to a text with dozens of shifts, and later said he had wanted different colours of ink for the different time-planes. A modern edition’s typography does not fully solve the problem. The lesson is that a typographic signal cannot carry a load the prose has not also carried — an unusually clean confirmation of §6.6.

Why the orphaned cut succeeds here. Because the reader’s disorientation is not a by-product of the technique but the content of the section. To follow Benjy without difficulty would be to have failed to enter his mind. The novel also has the structural honesty to pay the reader back: the second, third and fourth sections progressively supply the chronology the first withheld, so the reader’s initial confusion is retrospectively converted into knowledge. An orphaned cut is redeemable if the book eventually pays for it. Faulkner pays.

9.6 Rulfo, Pedro Páramo (1955) — transitions between the living and the dead

Juan Rulfo’s short novel opens with a young man arriving in the village of Comala to find the father he has been told lives there. Within fifty pages the reader discovers that the village is empty, that its inhabitants are dead, that the narrator is himself dead and speaking from a grave, and that a large part of what has been read as present narration is memory, or the murmuring of the dead, or both.

The transition system. There is none, in the sense this chapter has been using. Rulfo moves between speakers, periods and ontological conditions without headings, without attribution and frequently without a paragraph break. A voice begins; the reader must work out from internal evidence whose it is and when it is speaking; sometimes the evidence never arrives.

What replaces the anchor. Three things, and identifying them is the analytical work.

  1. The somatic cue (anchor type 7). Comala’s heat is the novel’s most consistent orientation device. Where the air is unbearable, the reader is in the frame narrative; the recollected Comala of the past is green.
  2. Register. Different voices have different rhythms, and the reader learns them. This is anchor type 11 doing the whole job, which is why the novel is slow for the first thirty pages and rapid thereafter.
  3. Deferred confirmation. Rulfo repeatedly allows a reader to build a wrong frame and then corrects it — the frame repair of §3.2 used deliberately, as a device rather than as a cost. The most famous instance is the point at which the reader learns that two speakers whose conversation they have been following are lying in adjacent graves.

Why it is not merely difficult. Because the disorientation is doctrinal. The novel’s proposition is that in Comala the categories a reader’s defaults depend on — living and dead, before and after, remembered and occurring — do not hold. A transition system that marked those boundaries would contradict the book.

A note on translation. Structural claims survive: the absence of attribution, the unmarked shifts and the ordering are the same in Lysander Kemp’s 1959 version, in Margaret Sayers Peden’s 1994 version and in Douglas J. Weatherford’s 2023 version. Texture claims do not: the three differ substantially in how much they smooth Rulfo’s colloquial Mexican Spanish, and a reader’s sense of how hard the transitions are is partly a function of which translation they hold.

9.7 García Márquez, One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967) — the temporal glide

Translated by Gregory Rabassa (1970). The novel covers a hundred years and seven generations, and its most distinctive formal property is that it does so almost without discrete transitions.

The technique. García Márquez narrates the great majority of his intervals in summary, in a chronicle register, and slides in and out of scene without marking the change. A paragraph may open in the iterative — this is how things were for years — modulate into a specific afternoon, and modulate back, with no signal at either boundary. The reader’s sense of duration is continuous rather than segmented, which is why the novel feels like a hundred years rather than like a hundred years’ worth of episodes.

The time-stamps. They are frequent, exact and used against exactness. Rain falls for four years, eleven months and two days; an insomnia plague, a banana-company massacre and a series of Aurelianos are dated with the confidence of a parish register. The precision does not orient; it asserts a chronicle whose contents are impossible, so that the reader is simultaneously given a firm chronology and prevented from believing it.

Why the glide does not produce the unmarked-simultaneity failure (§8.10). Because the novel’s proposition about time is announced in its first sentence — the famous three time-planes in one clause, discussed in Chapter 9 §4.6 — and reinforced by the repetition of names, the recurrence of situations and the family’s tendency to relive one another’s lives. The reader is trained, on page one, to expect that chronology in this book is circular. A formal contract declared early converts what would otherwise be a transition failure into a technique, which is the strongest available argument for Chapter 9’s claim that structural rules should be stated in the opening pages.

9.8 Dickens, Bleak House (1853) — alternation as a transition system

Bleak House alternates between two narrations: a third-person, present-tense, panoramic voice with the whole of England in view, and Esther Summerson’s retrospective, first-person, written account. The alternation is irregular — sometimes a chapter each, sometimes several chapters of one — and it is executed almost entirely without bridging language.

The three transition problems Dickens is solving.

Problem Solution
Handoff between narrations Absolute contrast of voice and tense; no bridge (§4.5, §5.2)
Movement between social worlds within the panoramic narration Explicit narratorial address, sometimes rhetorically enormous
Connecting plots the characters cannot know are connected The match-cut raised to a thesis (§4.3)

The first. Esther’s opening sentence — she has a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write her portion of these pages, for she knows she is not clever — is dissected at §5.2. Note what Dickens does not do: there is no heading, no announcement, no “Esther’s narrative”. The reader must infer the structure from the change of voice, which is the register shift (anchor type 11) operating at maximum strength. It works because the two voices share no feature at all: tense, person, temper, syntax and moral stance are all different.

The second. The panoramic narrator moves between Chancery, Chesney Wold, Tom-all-Alone’s and the Snagsbys’ by simply turning his attention, and he says so: he takes a glimpse of the world of fashion on the same miry afternoon; he asks what connexion there can be between the place in Lincolnshire, the house in town and the outcast sweeping the crossing. These are Victorian narratorial bridges of the type §3.6 describes, and they are the opposite of concealed: the seams are the performance.

The third, and the reason the novel belongs in this chapter rather than in Chapter 13. Dickens’s alternation is a transition system and not merely a viewpoint scheme, because the joins carry the book’s argument. A novel about a society whose parts do not know they are connected is written so that its cuts enact the connections its characters cannot see. Chancery’s fog and Lady Dedlock’s boredom are joined by a transition long before they are joined by a plot.

The complication worth teaching. By the criteria of §4.5, Dickens should fail: he does not declare his pattern before the reader has invested, since Esther arrives only in Chapter 3. He succeeds because the differentiation is so absolute that the switch reads as design rather than as slippage — which is the criterion §6.5 sets for drift, applied at the scale of a whole narration.

9.9 A revision case: the hard cut into the register drawer

This case works the series’ running example — the household of the shop, the ledger and the coat, whose continuity is maintained across the book. The canonical facts in play are established elsewhere: the register drawer that sticks on the K and comes free on the third try is planted in Chapter 10 (The Prologue); the changed locks are planted in Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange); Elena’s decision to drive to the shop rather than telephone their mother is the closing beat of the sequel worked in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel). The transition analysed here is the join between that sequel and the scene at the drawer, and it plants the smell of fresh ink (a ballpoint’s, since Dov has been writing in the ledger with a ballpoint from the same drawer; Chapter 13), which the later chapters treat as the disturbance that starts the investigation.

The outgoing unit ends:

She didn’t call. She drove to the shop instead, let herself in with the key, and went straight for the register drawer that stuck on the K.

Three days later, Elena drove to the shop. It was a Thursday, and the traffic on the ring road was worse than she had expected, so it was nearly four by the time she parked outside. The town had not changed. The greengrocer two doors down had put out the same trestle table it had put out for as long as she could remember, and the window of their own shop was still full of the things their father had never got round to marking down. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, thinking about the conversation in the doorway and about the new key in her pocket, which Dov had handed over as though it were a favour, beside the old one she had carried since she was twelve. Then she got out, crossed the pavement, unlocked the door and went in. The shop smelled of dust and cold. She stood in the doorway for a moment and let her eyes adjust. Then she went behind the counter and tried the register drawer, the one that had always stuck on the K.

1 — Anchor Anchored at word 1 (three days later) and again at word 4 (Elena) and again at word 7 (the shop). Over-anchored, not under-anchored
2 — Dimensions Space only. Time is stated but need not have changed at all; protagonist, causality and intentionality all continue. A one-dimension shift is being treated as a four-dimension one
3 — Launch The launching line is excellent and has been ignored. It named the destination, the means of entry and the object. Version A re-supplies all three
4 — Technique fit Time-stamp plus travel montage plus summary bridge, at a gap that required none of them

6 — Gap content Nothing. The three days contain no event the reader needs, which is why nothing is said about them
7 — Redundancy Severe. The key, the doorway conversation and the drawer are all re-established from the previous scene: a double anchor (§8.7) compounded with a recap bridge (§8.6)

Two observations about the diagnosis are worth drawing out, because they are the transferable part.

First, Version A is not badly written. The greengrocer’s trestle table is a decent detail; the unmarked-down stock is an efficient piece of characterisation of the father; the key that Dov handed over as though it were a favour is a fair summary of the previous scene. Every sentence would survive a line edit. The failure is entirely one of position: this is material that belongs elsewhere or nowhere, placed at the point of maximum reader impatience.

Second, the invented time-stamp is the root fault. Three days later is not merely unnecessary; it is actively damaging, and in a way that shows how a transition can break a book rather than merely slow it. The previous unit ended with a decision made in a state of anger, executed immediately: she drove to the shop instead. Inserting three days between the decision and the act destroys the causal link Chapter 2 spent a whole sequel constructing. The reader is now looking at a woman who was furious on Monday and went to the shop on Thursday, which is a different woman. A time-stamp is never neutral. It is a claim about causation as much as about the calendar.

Version B — the revision

⁂

The drawer came free on the third try, same as always, and the smell that met her wasn’t dust. It was ink, fresh ink, the sweetish smell of a biro, wrong for a till nobody had opened since March.

Analysis

Element What it does
Technique Hard cut. No bridging language, because the launching line had already done the orienting work
Anchor Word 2: the drawer. A resumed object (anchor type 4), with the definite article asserting that the reader knows it — orientation by frame recall, not construction
Dimensions shifted One (space), and even that was pre-announced
Elapsed time Unspecified, and correctly so: the reader will assume immediacy, and immediacy is what the causal chain requires
Second job The smell of fresh ink is at once an anchor (a till, a shop, a specific interior) and a plot disturbance: someone has used this drawer lately, and with a cheap pen, which should not be possible
Third job Since March dates the whole situation in two words, and does so as an inference from the object rather than as a stamp
Momentum The new unit’s business begins in the first clause. There is no transition and no scene opening; there is only the scene

What the revision did, procedurally. Six moves, in order of transferability.

  1. Trusted the launching line. Everything Version A re-established had already been established. A reliable transition revision is to read the last sentence before the gap and delete anything after the gap that repeats it.
  2. Deleted the interval. The three days had no contents (test 6) and cost the book its causal chain. Removing them restores the sequel’s decision as an executed decision.
  3. Deleted the journey. The drive contained nothing (§8.9). The gap goes at the departure and the anchor at the arrival; the road is not narrated.
  4. Anchored on a resumed object rather than a time-stamp. The drawer does in two words what three days later, Elena drove to the shop does in seven, and does it while starting the action.
  5. Made the sensory detail do the plot’s work. The smell is the anchor and the disturbance in the same breath — multiplicity (§5) at a join, which is where it is hardest and most valuable.
  6. Converted the date into an inference. Since March arrives as a property of the ink rather than as a heading, so the reader receives a chronology while attending to a smell.

What Version B has not solved. Four things, and a workshop should say so.

  • It is anchored for a reader who has just read the previous scene, and only for them. The definite article in the drawer presupposes the plant. A reader who has skipped a chapter, or who opens the book here, is not oriented at all. This is legitimate — novels are read in sequence — but it means the transition cannot be assessed in isolation, and it makes the passage a poor model for a stand-alone exercise.
  • It suppresses the interval entirely. The reader cannot say whether Elena arrived at the shop twenty minutes or two hours after leaving the doorway, and, more consequentially, cannot say how long the shop has stood empty except by inference from since March. That vagueness is affordable here. In a book keeping a tighter clock it would not be.
  • The technique is not repeatable at the next join. The hard cut works because the launching line aimed. The following transition — into the ledger and the changed hand, which is Chapter 6’s material — cannot use the same device, since Elena’s next move is not announced in advance. A transition solution is a solution to one gap. This is the practical content of the consistency test (§7.1, test 10) and the reason the taxonomy at §4 exists at all.
  • It states Elena’s inference as fact. A till nobody had opened since March is her belief in her idiom, and it is wrong: Dov has opened the drawer and written in the ledger since the evening of 9 March (Chapter 13). The sentence plants a misreading the book corrects much later, and whether the reader holds it against the narration depends on the retrospective distance the opening and viewpoint chapters establish.
