Chapter 17 · Series and Genre

Series and Genre

Promises Inherited and Promises Deferred: The Full Treatment

The Craft Behind the Story (Second Edition), Reference — Chapter 17. Part IV: Beyond the Book and Into Practice.

This chapter is new in the second edition, so its Reference layer is this single file rather than a corrected original. It extends the reader’s version in Core, Series and Genre, and can be read without it: the definitions it relies on are restated in section 1. Illustrative passages are quoted briefly for the purposes of criticism and study, and full citations appear in section 10. Where a work is read in translation, the translator is named. The analyses disclose the endings of the works they discuss.

1. Scope and working definitions

A genre, for a writer, is the set of expectations a reader brings to a book she has identified as belonging to a group of works treated as alike. It is known by its conventions, which divide into semantic elements (places, objects, figures, kinds of event) and syntactic elements (the relations among them and the order of events), after Rick Altman. The core promise of a genre is the convention whose violation its readers treat as a breach of contract; an obligatory scene is a scene its readers expect and miss if it is absent. A mode is a genre’s character carried, as an adjective, into a work of another kind, borrowing conventions without, by default, the core promise. A hybrid makes two core promises and keeps both. A convention can be kept, varied or declined; a declared decline is one shown inside the book early enough to be believed, with a replacement promise that the book keeps, and with the declined question kept in view.

A series is a sequence of separately published works that share characters, a world or a continuing story and invite reading as related. Each work is a volume; this edition calls a later volume a series instalment and keeps sequel for the reaction, dilemma and decision that follow a scene in Chapter 2. A volume has a volume question, which it must close, and may carry a series question, closed if at all by the last volume. The series hook is Chapter 16’s: a volume that declines to close a major question in order to bring the reader to the next. Re-entry is the opening of a later volume, which must serve a returning reader and a new one; dormancy across volumes is the absence of a carried thread from one or more volumes.

The governing claim is that a genre is a set of promises the reader brings to a book and a series a set she carries out of it, and that a book keeps faith with both when every convention it invokes is kept or visibly declined before the reader has staked much on it, and each volume closes its own question and leaves open only what it has shown to belong to the series. The claim concerns declarations, not compliance. A book that declines a genre’s central payment can satisfy that genre’s readers if it tells them in time; a book that pays everything can still fail them if it promised something else.

The boundary that matters most in revision is between a series and a set of books that share a setting. A set of books by one writer in one town, each with its own cast and none carrying a question, is a series only in a bookseller’s sense; it makes no promise across volumes beyond the place and the voice, and the chapter’s series tests do not apply to it. The relay series (section 3.2) lies at the threshold: it carries a world and its people, and a reader rewarded for reading in order has been made a small promise.

The chapter leaves the genre contract of the first pages to Chapter 9, the prologue’s genre test to Chapter 10, the serial’s chapter endings to Chapter 6, the declined reveal to Chapter 15, the last line and the series hook at a book’s end to Chapter 16, and the arrangement of times within a volume to Chapter 8. Market categories and publishing economics are outside its scope.

2. The theory at length

2.1 The horizon of expectations

Hans Robert Jauss’s inaugural address at Konstanz in 1967, published as “Literary History as a Challenge to Literary Theory” and translated by Timothy Bahti in Toward an Aesthetic of Reception (1982), argued that a work is received against a horizon of expectations formed by the reader’s experience of earlier works of its kind, its themes and the difference between poetic and practical language. A new work confirms, varies or breaks that horizon, and Jauss measured a work’s aesthetic distance by how far it departs from it. For a novelist the theory’s use is narrower and practical. The horizon is formed before the book is opened, largely by genre, and the book is judged against it from the first page. A writer cannot choose a reader’s horizon, but she can correct it early, and a book that leaves an inherited horizon uncorrected will be judged by it to the end.

2.2 Kinds and modes

Alastair Fowler’s Kinds of Literature (1982) argued that genres are better understood as families than as classes, linked by resemblances no single feature defines, and distinguished the kind, a genre with its full repertoire including its external form, from the mode, a selection of the kind’s features without its form, named by an adjective: comic, pastoral, gothic. The distinction answers a question writers ask often, whether a book that uses a genre’s furniture belongs to the genre. In Fowler’s terms it belongs to the mode, and the reader’s expectation of a core promise is the point at which mode and kind are confused. The household novel is a family novel in the mystery mode, and its draft jacket sold it as the kind.

2.3 The detective story’s two stories

Tzvetan Todorov’s “The Typology of Detective Fiction” (1966; translated by Richard Howard in The Poetics of Prose, 1977) described the classic whodunit as two stories: the story of the crime, complete before the book begins and absent from it, and the story of the investigation, present but slight in itself, whose task is to recover the first. He contrasted it with the thriller, which fuses the two and tells the story of the crime as it happens, so that curiosity gives way to suspense. The account explains why the mystery’s core promise is structural. The investigation story is not complete until the crime story has been told in full, so a mystery that declines its solution has left its own second story unfinished, whatever else it achieves. A family novel in the mystery mode can borrow the investigation without committing to the crime story, provided the investigator’s question is shown to be one the book will not finish.

2.4 The romance’s elements

The Romance Writers of America defined the genre by two elements, a central love story and an emotionally satisfying and optimistic ending, and added that a romance may have any tone or setting. Pamela Regis’s A Natural History of the Romance Novel (2003) described the form historically, from Samuel Richardson’s Pamela to the contemporary category romance, and found eight elements in it: a definition of the society the lovers must reform or leave, their meeting, the barrier between them, their attraction, the declaration, the point of ritual death at which the union seems impossible, the recognition that removes the barrier, and the betrothal. Regis’s list is the clearest available statement of a genre’s syntax, and it shows how much a romance can vary while keeping its promise: any element can be brief, displaced or ironised, so long as the betrothal, or its modern equivalent, arrives.

2.5 Formula and the iterative scheme

John Cawelti’s Adventure, Mystery, and Romance: Formula Stories as Art and Popular Culture (1976) defined a formula as a conventional structure of narrative a culture’s readers know, and argued that formula stories balance convention, which gives the pleasure of the known, against invention, which keeps the known from going dead. Umberto Eco’s “The Myth of Superman” (1962; translated by Natalie Chilton, 1972) described the iterative scheme of the serial hero, whose stories each begin as if the last had not happened, so that he never ages or uses himself up; Eco called the effect an oblivion of consecutiveness. In “Innovation and Repetition: Between Modern and Post-Modern Aesthetics” (1985) he argued that serial forms please two readers at once, the naive reader who enjoys the story and the competent reader who enjoys the variations worked within the scheme. Between them the two accounts give the episodic series its theory: its readers pay for the scheme, and its craft lies in what each volume does inside it.

2.6 Semantics and syntax

Rick Altman’s “A Semantic/Syntactic Approach to Film Genre” (1984) proposed that critics had defined genres either by lists of elements (the western’s horses, hats and frontier towns) or by structures of meaning (the western’s opposition of wilderness and civilisation), and that both were needed. Genres are most stable when a semantic set and a syntax coincide, and they change when one is carried over without the other. Altman’s later Film/Genre (1999) added that genres are made and remade by producers, critics and audiences using them, so a genre’s boundaries are always under negotiation. For the writer the semantic/syntactic distinction yields the chapter’s two common variations, new dress on an old syntax and an old dress on a new syntax, and its two common failures, the genre in costume (section 4.1) and the hybrid that keeps neither syntax.

2.7 Series and saga

Eco’s essay of 1985 also offered a typology of serial forms: the retake, which continues a successful story; the remake, which retells it; the series, in which a fixed situation and a fixed set of characters recur and each story is complete; the saga, in which the characters age and history accumulates; and intertextual dialogue, in which a work quotes others. Jason Mittell’s Complex TV: The Poetics of Contemporary Television Storytelling (2015) described a mode of television that combines episodic and serial storytelling, closing a story each week while carrying arcs across seasons, and made the viewer’s knowledge of the form part of its pleasure. The chapter’s four forms of series (section 3.2) cut across both typologies: the episodic series is Eco’s series, the serial is his saga told as one story, and the running arc is Mittell’s combination.

2.8 The evidence

Rolf Zwaan’s “Effect of Genre Expectations on Text Comprehension” (1994) gave participants the same texts with different instructions, telling some that they were reading literary stories and others that they were reading news. In the summaries the chapter has consulted, the first group read more slowly, kept more of the surface wording and built weaker models of the situations described; the expectation of a kind changed what readers attended to before any convention was kept or broken. The study used short texts and two broad categories, not genres of fiction, and the chapter takes from it only the modest conclusion that a reader’s belief about what she is reading governs how she reads it. Claims about how readers of one fiction genre treat another’s conventions, and about genres’ commercial shares, have not been measured here and are treated as impressions.

3. The extended catalogue

3.1 The five promises in detail

The Core chapter sorts genres by five core promises. Each is set out here with its usual conventions, its obligatory scenes and the variations its readers accept, since a writer revising in a genre needs to know which conventions are load-bearing.

The promise of solution. The mystery poses a question of fact, usually who committed a crime and how, and guarantees an answer from evidence the reader has been shown. Its conventions include the investigator, the closed circle of suspects, the clue, the false solution and the fair-play rule that the reader see what the detective sees, codified in Ronald Knox’s “Decalogue” (1929) and S. S. Van Dine’s twenty rules (1928). Its obligatory scenes are the discovery of the crime and the exposure. Its readers accept an unpunished culprit, a detective who fails at everything but the answer, and an exposure performed by someone other than the detective; they do not readily accept an unanswered question, unless the book has declared it.

The promise of union. The romance guarantees that the lovers at its centre end together and that the ending is emotionally satisfying and optimistic. Its conventions are Regis’s eight elements and, in contemporary category romance, a narrow viewpoint allocation, often alternating between the lovers. Its readers accept a union that is a beginning rather than a marriage, a barrier that is internal, and a point of ritual death that is quiet; they do not accept a death or a parting at the end, which moves a book to another shelf, as Me Before You, the Core chapter’s exception to its test 2, shows.

The promise of the threat met. The thriller, the horror novel and the adventure establish a danger and guarantee its direct confrontation. Their conventions include the clock, the escalation of stakes, the hero at the mercy of the threat and, in the thriller, a threat to many as well as to one. Their readers accept a hero’s death, a pyrrhic victory and a threat that survives in diminished form; they resist a threat that is never confronted, as the Core chapter’s exception to the undeclared decline, No Country for Old Men, shows at its cost.

The promise of the kept rule. Science fiction and fantasy declare a world unlike ours and guarantee that it will obey its declared rules, so that problems are solved within the world’s terms. The critic Darko Suvin called science fiction’s effect cognitive estrangement, a world strange enough to be seen freshly and consistent enough to be reasoned about. The conventions include the novum, the new thing that makes the world differ, and the cost of the rule. Readers accept a rule never explained, and resist one broken to resolve a plot.

The promise of significance. Literary fiction guarantees that its particulars will add up to more than a summary of its events could hold, and that the ending will alter the reader’s view of what preceded it. Its conventions include the open or partial ending, the domestic scale, the close attention to consciousness and the epiphany. Its readers accept the decline of any other genre’s payment, and resist an ending that pays a genre’s promise the book had not made, which they read as a concession: the novel that ends with a wedding nobody believed in, or a murder solved by a confession that arrives from nowhere.

3.2 Further series forms

The Core chapter names four forms of series: episodic, with a running arc, serial and relay. Two more need naming.

The anthology shares a premise, a title or a tone and nothing else: each volume has its own world and cast. Rod Serling’s The Twilight Zone (1959–64) is the classic screen case. It promises a kind of experience, not a continuity, and its volumes are judged as standalone works; its single convention across volumes is its frame, which is a formal contract in Chapter 9’s sense.

The companion is a book set in a series’ world whose question is unrelated to the series question, often centred on a minor character. A companion is a relay volume that makes no promise to the series and asks nothing of it; it must stand as a book.

3.3 Summary table

The table gives the six forms by what a single volume closes, what carries, and what the form promises the reader about the next volume.

Form A volume closes What carries What the form promises of the next volume
Episodic Everything The hero, the world, the hero’s rule The scheme again, varied
Running arc The volume question One line, a step per volume The scheme, and the next step
Serial A stage The story The story continued, in order
Relay The volume question The world and its people A new centre, in a known world
Anthology Everything A premise or a frame The kind of experience again
Companion Everything The world Nothing

The table’s last column is the practical one: a volume that breaks its form’s promise of the next, an episodic volume that ends on an open question or a serial volume that closes the story, has changed the contract and should say so.

3.4 Prequels and reading order

A prequel is a volume set before the others in story time, usually written after them. It is an analepsis at the scale of the series in Chapter 8’s sense, and it inherits that chapter’s strand problem: its reader knows how its story ends, because the later volumes have told her, so its question cannot be what happens. It must run on a question the later volumes leave open, how or why or at what cost, or on a character the later volumes could not show. The order of reading is then a decision with consequences. C. S. Lewis’s The Magician’s Nephew (1955) was the sixth Narnia book published and is set first; later editions renumbered the series in story order, and readers who meet it first read as a creation story what readers in publication order read as an answer to questions the first book had raised. Neither order is wrong, but the writer should know which reader she is writing for.

3.5 Genres that expect a dual timeline

Some genres take an arrangement of times as a convention. The dual-timeline mystery braids a present-day investigator, often a woman tracing her family, with a strand in the past, and its readers expect the strands to converge by identity: a person in the present found to descend from, or to be, a person in the past. Kate Morton’s The Forgotten Garden (2008) is an example. In such a book Chapter 8’s necessity test is answered by the genre before the book begins, and the convergence is the obligatory scene; the strand question test still applies, since a past strand that only explains the present fails readers of the genre as it fails anyone. The time-slip romance and the multi-generational family saga make similar promises, the first of a union across times, the second of a pattern completed in the youngest generation.

4. The extended catalogue of failure patterns

4.1 The five patterns of the Core chapter

The Core chapter treats five patterns, each with its symptom, mechanism, remedy and an exception. The undeclared decline invokes a core promise and declines it at the end without a sign inside the book (exception: No Country for Old Men). The genre in costume uses a genre’s semantic elements without its syntax, so readers apply a promise the book never makes (exception: The Buried Giant). The volume that owes its question leaves its own question open at the last page to bring the reader back (exception: 61 Hours, section 6.3). The deferred answer holds a series question across volumes without advancing it (exception: the Harry Potter novels and Snape’s allegiance). The recap at the door opens a later volume with summary (exception: the television Previously on sequence). Four more follow.

4.2 The reset hero

Symptom. A series hero who changed in one volume is found in the next as he was before, and readers complain that the previous book did not count. Mechanism. The reset hero has had a criterion changed, in Chapter 12‘s sense, and the change undone between volumes without a scene. It fails test 8. The cause is usually the episodic form reasserting itself over a running arc the writer did not mean to start. Remedy. Either show the reversal, as a scene in the new volume with its own price, or move the change in the earlier volume to another character, so that the hero’s criterion was never at stake. Exception. Seinfeld (1989–98), whose writers worked to Larry David’s rule “no hugging, no learning”. Its characters learn nothing because the series declared from the start that they would not; the reset is the form, and the comedy is the characters’ immunity to consequence.

4.3 The undone ending

Symptom. A series ends a character or a story conclusively, and a later volume reverses the ending. Mechanism. The undone ending fires Chapter 16’s retroactive lens on a volume and then withdraws it, so that the reader’s final judgement of the earlier book is cancelled. It reduces every later ending’s authority, since the reader has learned that endings in this series can be revoked. Remedy. Avoid a conclusive ending that may need undoing; if one must be undone, make the reversal cost something in the story, and give the earlier ending a reading that survives it. Exception. Arthur Conan Doyle’s “The Final Problem” (1893), in which Holmes and Moriarty go over the Reichenbach Falls, and “The Adventure of the Empty House” (1903), in which Holmes returns. The public outcry at his death was long and loud, and The Strand Magazine lost subscribers; the return succeeds because Doyle gives it a story, Holmes’s years in hiding from Moriarty’s men, and because readers had wanted the ending undone.

4.4 The escalation ratchet

Symptom. Each volume’s threat is larger than the last, until the series’ world can no longer contain it and the stakes stop meaning anything. Mechanism. The escalation ratchet treats Chapter 11’s rule that a middle must raise its prices as a rule across volumes, so that each volume must exceed the last. The threat met is a promise about confrontation, not scale, and scale has a ceiling. Remedy. Escalate by cost to the hero rather than by size of threat: a smaller danger that takes something the hero cannot spare raises the stakes without raising the ceiling. Exception. The Harry Potter novels, whose threat grows from a stolen stone to a war across seven volumes. The series commits the ratchet and succeeds because it pays each enlargement in cost to Harry, the deaths of people he loves, so that the stakes rise in him as well as in the world.

4.5 The overloaded last volume

Symptom. The final volume of a long series is longer than the others, slower in its last third, and disappoints readers who loved the earlier volumes. Mechanism. The overloaded last volume must close the series question and every thread carried across the volumes, at a length calibrated for one book, and the retroactive lens judges the whole series by it, as Chapter 16’s treatment of series endings argues. The deferred answer (section 4.1) is its commonest cause. Remedy. Close carried threads in the volumes before the last, so that the last carries only the series question and the threads that the answer needs. Exception. The Lord of the Rings, whose last volume closes its main question with several chapters still to come and spends them on returns, a scouring of the Shire and departures, which readers have found too long and many have come to love for that reason.

5. The diagnostic procedures

Test 1, shelf. Write two sentences of jacket copy from the first chapter alone and two from the last chapter alone, without reference to the rest. If they belong on different shelves, the book drifts; decide which shelf it is on and revise the other end.

Test 2, core promise. Name the genre the opening declares and its core promise, from section 3.1. Find the page where the promise is kept. If there is none, the book has declined it, and test 3 applies.

Test 3, declared decline. Draw up the promise inventory: a row for each convention the book invokes, with its genre, the percentage of the text at which it is first invoked, whether it is kept, varied or declined, and where that is shown. For each declined row, find the first sign of the decline inside the book; if it falls after the first quarter, move it or add one earlier. Then name the replacement promise and the page that keeps it.

Test 4, variation. Mark the convention the book varies most. Ask whether the book’s meaning, as its ending states it, lies in that variation. A formula book may pass this test by declaring its scheme; record the choice.

Test 5, volume question. Write the volume question in a line, find the scene that closes it and its percentage. If the scene is absent or falls after the last page, the volume owes its question.

Test 6, series step. For each volume, write in a phrase the step it takes on the series question: a fact learned, a possibility removed, a cost paid. Two successive volumes with no step are stalled.

Test 7, re-entry. Give the first chapter of a later volume to a reader who has not read the series, and ask her who the people are and what they want. Mark every sentence of summary; count the facts it delivers that the first chapter’s scenes need.

Test 8, criterion across volumes. Write the hero’s criterion, in Chapter 12’s form, at the end of the previous volume and at the start of this one. If they differ, find the scene that shows the change.

The volume table gathers tests 5 to 8: a row per volume, with columns for the volume question and where it closes, the series step, the threads carried and their dormancy in volumes, the hero’s criterion, the re-entry and the last page.

6. Further analyses

6.1 Bring Up the Bodies (Hilary Mantel, 2012): the middle volume of a serial with a known end

Mantel’s trilogy follows Thomas Cromwell from the 1520s to his execution in 1540, and its reader knows the end from history. The series question cannot be whether he falls but how a man so able comes to it, and each volume takes a step on that question by closing a historical stage: Wolf Hall (2009) with Cromwell’s rise and the death of Thomas More, Bring Up the Bodies with the fall of Anne Boleyn in 1536, The Mirror & the Light (2020) with Cromwell’s own. The middle volume is the hardest position in a serial, since it neither begins nor ends the story, and Mantel’s book, which won the Booker Prize as its predecessor had, shows the solution: it is the most closed of the three. Its volume question, how Cromwell will rid the king of Anne, is posed early and closed by her execution, and its step on the series question is that Cromwell, in doing so, makes the enemies who will destroy him. Its re-entry is a scene, not a summary: His children are falling from the sky, the first sentence says, and the children are falcons named for his dead daughters, flying at Wolf Hall in September 1535 with the king. A new reader learns that Cromwell grieves and serves, and a returning reader is reminded of the daughters the first volume lost, without a line of recap.

6.2 Beach Read (Emily Henry, 2020): a romance about the romance’s promise

Henry’s novel makes genre its subject and keeps its genre’s promise. January Andrews writes romances with happy endings and has stopped believing in them after her father’s death and what she learned about him afterwards; her neighbour for the summer, Augustus Everett, writes acclaimed literary fiction with dark endings. They agree to swap genres, each writing a novel in the other’s. The book’s syntax is Regis’s: the meeting, the attraction, the barrier (each lover’s distrust of the other’s kind of ending as well as of the other), the point of ritual death and the declaration. Its variation is that the lovers argue about the promise the book is keeping, so that the romance’s optimistic ending is earned against an objection the book has given its hero to make. It is a case of variation by making a convention the subject: the promise is kept and examined in the same pages, and readers of the genre were given what they came for.

6.3 61 Hours (Lee Child, 2010): a hook in an episodic series

The Reacher novels are an episodic series of the purest kind: a drifter arrives in a place, finds an injustice, ends it and leaves, with the hero’s rule unchanged. 61 Hours, the fourteenth, sends Reacher to Bolton, South Dakota, in winter, where he agrees to protect Janet Salter, an elderly librarian who is the only witness willing to testify against a methamphetamine operation. She is shot dead by the town’s police chief, and Reacher kills the chief and the operation’s head in an underground facility; the book ends as the tunnels’ fuel ignites and Reacher runs for the stairs, while a military officer in Virginia, Susan Turner, watches the news of the explosion. The volume question, the witness and the operation, is closed; the hook is on the hero’s survival, the one thing an episodic series promises to carry. The next volume, Worth Dying For, appeared later the same year with Reacher alive in Nebraska. The hook is a departure from the form, and it costs little because the form is so plainly declared: no reader of the fourteenth volume believed the series had ended, so the hook asked only how. The hook’s cost depends on the form: in an episodic series it postpones nothing the volume owed.

6.4 Twin Peaks (ABC, 1990–91): a series question answered and not replaced

David Lynch and Mark Frost’s series opens on the body of Laura Palmer and is organised around the question its first season made famous, who killed her. The question is the series question, and the show’s first season advanced it by steps while multiplying its town’s other mysteries. The answer came in the seventh episode of the second season, broadcast on 10 November 1990: Laura’s father, Leland, possessed by the spirit called BOB. The episode drew about 17.2 million households and the next about 13.3 million, and the series was cancelled the following year. The case is not that the answer was wrong; it was prepared and terrible. The series had no second question of equal force ready to take the first one’s place, so the answer closed the series question while the series continued, and the volume table, filled in for the second season, would show rows with no step. Lynch later said he had not wanted the question answered at all, which describes the alternative, a declared decline in the manner of The Leftovers.

7. The household case in full

7.1 The draft

The household novel is a contemporary literary family novel, quiet and domestic in register, set in a small British town with a ring road. Four months after the father’s death in March his three adult children reconstruct what he left. The book poses in its first chapter the question why their mother changed the shop’s locks, and declines to answer it; the mother is never focalised and never gives an account of herself. The draft under revision adds two things at the request of the writer’s agent, who wants a second book: a last page, after Nadia puts on the coat, on which the mother rings the shop and Elena answers; and jacket copy that reads In March their father died, and their mother changed the locks. Four months later, one of his children is going to find out why.

7.4 The revision

Version B removes the last page, so that the novel ends where it did, with Nadia, the coat and the silence kept in view, and proposes jacket copy that makes a promise the book keeps: Four months after their father’s death, Elena finds that her key no longer fits the shop, and that someone else has been writing in his ledger. The changed hand is a kept promise, answered for the reader at about 78 per cent and for the sisters at the convergence, and the locks appear as a fact, not a question. The text needs no new sign of the decline: the inventory shows the book declaring it by Dov’s refusal and by three decisions of Elena’s, in the truck, at the empty drawer and at the convergence, each choosing not to ask.

7.5 What the revision settles, and what it does not

It settles the book as a standalone with no series question, and fixes the genre declaration in the text rather than in the paratext. It leaves four things open. The first sign of the decline, in the truck, is legible as a decline only in retrospect, so the declaration is cumulative and a reader with a mystery’s horizon may hold the question to the end. The jacket is not the writer’s, and nothing in the book can stop a publisher printing the question again. The coat’s weekly brushing is an undeclared decline under the mystery mode, which a later chapter, or the author, must either pay, give a sign, or cut. And the agent’s request is answered only negatively.

8. The limits of the model

The model assumes a reader who arrives with one horizon and holds it, and readers bring several and change them as they read. It assumes that genres have core promises stable enough to name, and genres change: the romance’s optimistic ending is a definition its writers adopted, and the detective story’s fair play a set of rules written in the late 1920s; a writer in fifty years may find the five promises out of date. It treats the jacket as the paratext that matters, when readers also meet a book through reviews and recommendations. And it has nothing to say about why a reader chooses a genre, or about the readers who read across all of them, for whom a declined convention is a pleasure in itself. Its use is narrower: to let a writer see which promises her book makes, and to keep or decline each on purpose.

9. Glossary of the chapter’s terms

The definitions below are for the consolidated glossary.

Anthology. A series whose volumes share a premise, title or frame and nothing else.

Companion. A book set in a series’ world whose question is unrelated to the series question; it makes no promise across volumes.

Convention. A feature readers expect of a genre, semantic or syntactic.

Core promise. The convention whose breach a genre’s readers treat as a breach of contract: solution, union, the threat met, the kept rule or significance.

Declared decline. A convention declined with a sign inside the book early enough to be believed, a replacement promise that the book keeps, and the declined question kept in view.

Declined convention. A convention a book invokes and does not pay.

Deferred answer. The failure of a series that holds its question open across volumes without advancing it.

Dormancy across volumes. The absence of a carried thread from one or more volumes, measured in volumes and in the reader’s months.

Episodic series. A series whose volumes close everything and carry only the hero, his world and his rule.

Formula. A conventional story structure a culture’s readers know (Cawelti).

Genre. For a writer, the set of expectations a reader brings to a book she has identified as one of a group of works treated as alike.

Genre in costume. The failure of a book that uses a genre’s semantic elements without its syntax.

Horizon of expectations. The expectations, formed by earlier works, against which a reader receives a new one (Jauss).

Hybrid. A book that makes two genres’ core promises and keeps both.

Kind. A genre with its full repertoire of features, as distinct from a mode (Fowler).

Mode. A genre’s character carried, as an adjective, into a work of another kind, borrowing conventions without, by default, the core promise (Fowler).

Obligatory scene. A scene a genre’s readers expect and miss if it is absent (Coyne).

Paratext. Everything that surrounds and presents a text: title, cover, jacket copy, epigraph, shelf (Genette).

Prequel. A volume set before the others in story time; an analepsis at the scale of a series.

Promise inventory. A table of the conventions a book invokes, with where each is first invoked, whether it is kept, varied or declined, and where that is shown.

Re-entry. The opening of a later volume, which must serve a returning reader and a new one.

Recap at the door. The failure of a later volume that opens with summary of the earlier ones.

Relay series. A series that keeps a world and passes the centre to a new protagonist in each volume.

Reset hero. A series hero whose change in one volume is undone in the next without a scene.

Running arc. A line carried across an episodic series, advancing a step per volume while each volume closes its own question.

Semantic and syntactic elements. A genre’s building blocks, and the relations and order among them (Altman).

Serial. One story published in volumes, each closing a stage.

Series. A sequence of separately published works sharing characters, a world or a continuing story, which invite reading as related.

Series instalment. A later volume of a series; this edition’s term for what is elsewhere called a sequel.

Series question. The question that runs across a series’ volumes and is closed, if at all, by the last.

Undeclared decline. The failure of a book that invokes a core promise and declines it at the end without a sign inside the book.

Undone ending. A conclusive ending in one volume reversed by a later one.

Variation. Keeping a genre’s core promise while changing a convention: new semantics on an old syntax, a varied obligatory scene, or a scene performed by someone unexpected.

Volume. One work of a series.

Volume question. The question a volume is built on and must close.

Volume table. A table with a row per volume of a series, recording its question and where it closes, its step on the series question, the threads carried, the hero’s criterion, the re-entry and the last page.

Volume that owes its question. The failure of a volume that leaves its own question open at the last page to bring the reader back.
