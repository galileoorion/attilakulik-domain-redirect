12. Studio Work

Continue in Core

12.1 Practical exercises

Exercise 1 — The domain audit. Apply §5.5 to one chapter of your draft. Produce the table, with each domain letter annotated in four words. Write 250 words on what the distribution reveals about your habits rather than about the chapter.

Exercise 2 — Mode substitution. Take one scene and rewrite the same material in two other modes from §6.3, chosen for maximum distance from the original. Do not judge until all three exist. Write 300 words on what each buys and costs.

Exercise 3 — The iterative paragraph. Write 200 words of iterative summary (mode 6) conveying two years of a character’s habitual life. No scene, no dialogue, no single occasion. Then write the pseudo-iterative version (mode 7) and note what the added specificity gains and what it makes impossible.

Exercise 4 — Entry and exit. Take a scene and delete its first paragraph; read. Delete the first two; read. Continue until deletion causes real loss. Repeat from the end. Record where the scene actually begins and ends, and write 150 words on why the deleted material felt necessary while drafting.

Exercise 5 — The four-sentence scene. Write a complete scene in four sentences: a want, a resistance, a reversal and a consequence. Then write the same scene in eight hundred words. Compare which value shift is more legible.

Exercise 6 — The unresisted want, three ways. Write a scene in which a character asks for something and receives it immediately. Then rewrite it twice: once relocating the resistance inside the asker, once making the granting a disaster in the manner of §8.8. Identify which version your novel needs.

Exercise 7 — The interiorised scene. Write 800 words in which one character, alone, understands something. No dialogue, no second person, no external event. The thought must advance rather than circle — mark the stages in the margin. Compare against §9.8.

Exercise 8 — The reverse outline. Reverse-outline an entire draft, or 40,000 words of one, per §7.2. One line per scene, in the language of consequence. Submit the outline, not the draft, with 400 words on what it exposed.

Exercise 9 — Counterpoint. Following Flaubert (§9.2), write a scene in which two discourses run simultaneously and neither comments on the other — a private conversation against a public one, an argument against an announcement, a confession against a piece of routine business. The rule: no narratorial commentary of any kind.

12.2 Seminar questions

  1. Does the value-shift test describe narrative, or a period? §3.6 contrasts selected historical practices with a scenic preference in much modern craft advice. Test the claim by applying the protocol in §7 to three scenes from Tom Jones and three from a novel published in the last decade. What does the test measure in each case, and does it measure the same thing?

  2. Four domains or five? Argue for or against admitting the relational domain (§4.5) as a separate category. Use the Hunsford proposal as your principal evidence, and answer the parsimony objection: if a relation is only a pair of mental states, what is left for the domain to name?

  3. What shifts in Melville’s “A Squeeze of the Hand”? See §8.1. It has a place, a duration and a viewpoint, but little conventional plot movement. Ishmael says that his feelings toward the crew and their oath change. Does the chapter’s value-shift test capture that movement, or does it still understate what the passage contributes to the book?

  4. Whose conventions? The failure patterns in §8 are drawn from an Anglo-American workshop tradition of the last sixty years. Examine the scene construction of The Tale of Genji, Dream of the Red Chamber or a novel from a strong oral-narrative tradition, and determine which of §8’s prohibitions describe narrative and which describe a local literary culture.

  5. Eliot’s intervention. In Middlemarch chapters 20–21 the narrator interrupts a scene to instruct the reader in how to feel about Casaubon (§9.1). Lubbock’s scenic ideal (§3.2) would remove it; Booth would defend it. Which is right, and what would the chapter lose? Extend the question: is there a contemporary novel that could do this without being thought clumsy, and if not, why not?

  6. The ethics of the extraction test. A test that asks what a reader would lose treats the reader’s experience as the measure of a scene’s worth. Are there scenes whose value lies in something other than the reader’s gain — an obligation to a subject, an act of witness, a refusal to be efficient? Consider Hugo’s Waterloo (§8.5) and the iterative chapters of Things Fall Apart.

12.3 Assessment rubric

For a submitted scene (1,500–2,500 words) with a 750-word critical commentary.

Band Craft (60%) Commentary (40%)
Distinction A legible want under real resistance; a value shift that is charged and locatable; two or more domains actively served without strain; entry and exit chosen; information transacted rather than delivered; the mode is evidently a choice Names the scene’s mode and defends it against alternatives; identifies the scene’s own risks and what it has not solved; engages at least two theoretical sources critically rather than decoratively
Merit Want and resistance present; a real but under-charged shift; two domains, one of them thin; entry or exit slack Accurate self-analysis; some theoretical engagement; risks acknowledged if not resolved
Pass A scene exists; a want is discernible; one domain served competently; prose adequate Describes the scene accurately; theory referenced but not applied
Fail No engine; no locatable shift; one or more failure patterns unrecognised Description without analysis; no engagement with the model
