Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

15. Further Reading

Continue in Core

Annotated, grouped by what each source is good for. Starred items are the recommended first three.

15.1 On duration, and on what a scene is

  • ★ Genette, Gérard. Narrative Discourse (1972; Eng. 1980). The indispensable book for this chapter. Chapter 2 on duration and chapter 3 on frequency contain everything §§3.3, 6.2 and 6.3 are built from, and the analysis of Proust’s ratios is the best available demonstration that scene and summary are historical variables. Formidable in idiom, and worth the effort.
  • ★ Lubbock, Percy. The Craft of Fiction (1921). Read for an influential formulation of the vocabulary rather than for its conclusions. The scene/picture distinction is developed here in a careful form. Read the chapters on Madame Bovary and The Ambassadors.

15.2 On why a part must answer to a whole

  • Aristotle, Poetics, chapters 6–11. Twenty pages. The removability test, the necessary-or-probable connection, and the criticism of episodic plot are all here, and every craft manual since is a gloss on them.
  • Sternberg, Meir. Expositional Modes and Temporal Ordering in Fiction (1978). The suspense/curiosity distinction reorganises how a writer thinks about what a scene may withhold. Demanding, formalist, unrivalled.

15.3 On showing, telling, and the limits of the scenic ideal

  • ★ Booth, Wayne C. The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961/1983). The necessary corrective to “show, don’t tell”. Booth demonstrates that authorial effacement is a rhetoric with costs, not a neutral advance, and his account of distance is directly applicable to the scene. Long; the chapters on telling and showing and on the author’s voice are the relevant ones.

15.4 Cognitive work

  • Zwaan and Radvansky (1998). A review article, and the clearest statement of the dimensions readers track. Directly relevant to what a value shift consists of.

15.5 Craft manuals, used critically

  • McKee, Robert. Story (1997). The source of the value shift. Written for screenwriters, magisterial in tone, and best read as a rigorous articulation of one tradition rather than as a description of narrative. Its account of the scene, and of what McKee calls the “turning point”, is the sharpest in the craft literature.
  • Swain, Dwight V. Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965) and Bickham, Jack M. Scene & Structure (1993). The scene–sequel model in its original and its clearest expositions. Mechanistic, dated in idiom, structurally sound. Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) treats them at length.

15.6 Within this textbook

  • Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) — the rhythm this chapter defers: goal–conflict–disaster, reaction–dilemma–decision, and the causal chain.
  • Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) — the domain model applied one level down, with its own relational fifth.
  • Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) — focalisation as a structural choice; the answer to “whose scene is this?”
  • Chapter 14 (The Subplot) — the attention tax: this chapter’s argument about the cost of a scene, applied to page space across plots.
  • Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) — the scene with five additional contracts falling due at once.
  • Chapter 16 (The Ending) — the scene whose value shift must be the book’s.
