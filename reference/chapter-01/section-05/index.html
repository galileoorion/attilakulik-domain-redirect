Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

5. Multiplicity: The Economy of the Scene

Continue in Core

5.1 The principle, stated as a constraint

Multiplicity is the practice of making a single unit of text discharge several narrative functions at once. Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) treats it as an arithmetical necessity; at scene scale the pressure is lower, and the principle is best stated as an economic proposition.

The constraint. A novel has a finite number of scenes. Every scene costs the reader time and the book length. A scene serving one domain has spent a scene’s worth of the reader’s attention to buy one function; a scene serving three has bought three at the same price.

The instruction is not “add domains” but “notice which domains are already latent”. Almost every draft scene contains an unexploited second function. The pass looks for it rather than importing one.

The discipline is selection, not addition. The writer is not looking for a way to make one scene do five jobs; they are looking for the version of this scene in which the material already present does two more jobs.

5.2 Anatomy: the Hunsford proposal, phrase by phrase

The situation: Elizabeth Bennet is alone at the Collinses’ parsonage, having just learned that Darcy separated Bingley from her sister Jane. Darcy arrives unannounced, paces, sits, and says:

In vain I have struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.

Four sentences, twenty-nine words. The anatomy:

Textual element Functions discharged
In vain Character — the declaration opens on the vocabulary of defeat, so the reader learns before anything else that this man experiences love as a failure of self-government. Relational — the first word of the most important speech in the novel is about him.
I have struggled Character — pride, and self-regard, in two words. Plot — a struggle implies a duration; the reader retro-fits weeks of resistance and understands that the proposal has a history. Theme — the novel’s question about the relation of feeling to judgement is opened by a man who has treated his own feeling as an enemy.
It will not do. Voice / Texture — an idiom of practical dismissal, the phrase one uses about an unsatisfactory arrangement, applied to a passion. Character — the register in which he thinks about his own heart is administrative.
My feelings will not be repressed. Character — the passive construction removes him as agent; his feelings act, he does not. Relational — nothing has yet been said to or about her.
You must allow me Relational — the first appearance of you, and it arrives as an instruction. Plot — permission is being demanded, not requested, which shapes the refusal that follows.
how ardently Character — the one word in the speech in which feeling escapes control. Theme — the collision of ardently with it will not do is the whole man in one sentence.
I admire and love you Plot — the proposal is on the table; the scene’s engine is running. Relational — the sequence admire before love preserves the hierarchy the rest of the speech will make explicit.

Seven elements; four domains touched; not a word spent on a job another word could have done. Note what Austen has not done: no interiority is supplied, no gesture is described, and Elizabeth is not consulted. The reader’s understanding of Darcy’s condition is entirely a deduction from diction.

The scene’s second half performs the same operation in reverse. Elizabeth’s refusal culminates in a sentence that is simultaneously a plot turn, a character revelation, a relational rupture and the novel’s title in dramatic form:

…you could not have made me the offer of your hand in any possible way that would have tempted me to accept it.

The clause in any possible way is the load-bearing one. Elizabeth is not refusing this proposal; she is refusing the class of all proposals from this man — which is the strongest available statement, and which the novel will spend two hundred pages falsifying. The sentence also closes off her own options, since a woman who has said this cannot later accept without a change so large that the rest of the book is required to produce it. A single subordinate clause has generated the second half of a novel.

5.3 Where the multiplying work actually lives

Novice revision looks for multiplicity in metaphor, on the assumption that figurative language is where meanings stack. At scene scale that is almost never where the work is. Four humbler places account for most of it.

1. The physical business. What hands do while mouths talk. A character who goes on wrapping plates in newspaper during a confrontation is supplying texture (the domestic chore), character (the refusal to stop), relational information (he will not give her his attention), and plot (he is packing, which means he has already decided something). Choose the business, and four domains follow. This technique transfers most widely, since every scene has hands in it, and it is the reason experienced writers ask “what are they doing?” before “what are they saying?”

2. The object that changes hands. A key, a ledger or a plate passed, withheld or refused registers power between two people without a sentence of statement. When Dov gives Elena the new key in the doorway and keeps the reason (Chapter 3, The Dialogue Exchange), the key does the relational work and the withheld reason does the plot work.

3. What a character declines to say or ask. An unasked question is the cheapest relational carrier available, because it costs no words at all. When one sibling does not ask another where he was in March, the omission does character, relational and plot work simultaneously, and the reader performs the inference unaided. The corresponding craft error is the character who asks everything, producing a scene in which all information is extracted and none is withheld.

4. Placement against the reader’s knowledge. A scene the reader enters knowing more than the characters means two things in every line, so dramatic irony multiplies functions without adding a word. Placing a scene after a disclosure rather than before it is therefore itself a decision about multiplicity.

5.4 The ceiling

Multiplicity has an upper bound, and the failure at that bound is the overloaded scene. Four symptoms mark it.

Symptom What the reader experiences
Every line significant No rhythm, only effort; emphasis becomes impossible because emphasis requires contrast
Simultaneous wants Four characters each pursuing a distinct objective in one room; the reader cannot tell whose scene this is
Illegible primary action The reader can say what is being established but not what is happening
Symbolic saturation Every object turns out to mean something, so the reader stops believing in the room

The remedy is the counter-intuitive one Chapter 9 states for the opening page and which holds with equal force here: a novel needs scenes that are only doing one thing. They function as rests. A reader who is never allowed to relax cannot register emphasis, and a book in which every scene is a three-domain scene reads as relentless rather than rich.

5.5 Exercise: the domain audit

A practical procedure, and the most reliable teaching instrument in this chapter.

  1. Take a chapter of the draft and divide it into scenes by the functional criterion at §2.2 — a scene ends when the value it was moving stops moving.
  2. For each scene, write one line in a table: its number, its length in words, and the letters of every domain it actively serves: P (plot), C (character), T (theme), X (texture), R (relational).
  3. Beside each letter, write the specific change in three or four words. Not character, but she stops defending him. A letter you cannot annotate is a domain the scene occupies rather than serves; strike it.
  4. Add a final column: what the scene costs in words.

Interpretation:

Pattern Diagnosis First move
Most scenes carry one letter The draft is spending scenes serially. Length is being used where selection is needed Run the multiplicity pass (§5.3) on the three longest single-letter scenes
Most scenes carry four or five Over-pressurised; the reader has no rests Identify two scenes to simplify deliberately
A long scene carries X only The runaway set piece (§8.5), or a normal-establishing scene (§4.4) Ask which later scene it is load-bearing for. If none, compress
