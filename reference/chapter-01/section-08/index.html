Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern below is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what actually goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its remedy, and — most importantly — its exception: a published scene that commits the error and succeeds.

8.1 The Purposeless Scene

Symptom. The scene occupies page time and shifts no value in any domain. It is usually pleasant, often well observed, and impossible to summarise in the language of consequence.

Mechanism. A satellite has been given kernel resources (§3.4). The scene exists because it was written, and it was written because the writer needed it: discovery drafting requires passages that find the story, and those passages are apparatus rather than narrative. The writer needed to write it to find the story; the reader does not need to read it to receive the story.

Remedy. Test 4, run in both directions, then the ladder at §7.3: compress, fuse, relocate the one load-bearing sentence, cut.

The apparent exception: Melville, Moby-Dick (1851), chapter 94. “A Squeeze of the Hand” describes Ishmael and his shipmates squeezing lumps of spermaceti back into fluid, until Ishmael finds himself squeezing his companions’ hands and imagining universal fellow-feeling — let us squeeze ourselves universally into the very milk and sperm of kindness. No major plot event follows from the work in the tub. But Ishmael explicitly says he forgets the crew’s terrible oath and feels free of ill will. His emotional state changes, and he reaches towards his companions. The chapter therefore fails a plot-only test, not a test broad enough to include character, relation and theme.

It is also indispensable to this reading of the book. Here, texture carries an argument. Moby-Dick accumulates ways of attending to the whale and the work around it; the cetology chapters, the chapter on whiteness, the chapter on the mast-head and the chapter on the spermaceti offer more than plot information. Remove the squeezing and the novel loses a position as well as a felt change in Ishmael: his alternative to Ahab’s monomania. The extraction test must ask about that change rather than looking only for a new step in the hunt.

8.2 The Redundant Scene

Symptom. A second scene establishing something the reader already knows: another demonstration of the villain’s cruelty, another quarrel on the same terms, another refusal to answer the same question.

Mechanism. The value shift has already been banked. Once a reader has registered a change of state, the state is changed; a scene that re-performs the change is asking the reader to learn something twice, and readers experience this as the book losing confidence.

Remedy. Choose the better-placed of the two — usually the earlier, unless the later one is better executed — and delete the other. Do not attempt to differentiate them; a redundant scene made more interesting is a more interesting redundant scene.

The exception: Kafka, The Trial (1925), chapter 5. Josef K. opens a lumber-room door in the bank and finds two warders being flogged by a man in a leather costume, on account of K.’s own complaint about them. He intervenes ineffectually and leaves. The following evening he opens the same door and finds the same scene, unchanged: the same men, the same posture, the same beginning of the same cry. He shuts the door.

The same configuration recurs, though Kafka does not repeat the earlier passage verbatim. It is also one of the most frightening moments in the novel, and it works because repetition is part of the novel’s argument. In a world where progress is uncertain and the court’s reach is diffuse, the recurrence is a structural statement: K. confronts a punishment that seems not to have advanced since yesterday.

8.3 The Expository Scene in Disguise

Symptom. The scene has all the furniture of drama — a room, two people, dialogue, someone getting up to pour a drink — and exists solely to deliver information to the reader. Characters ask questions they would already know the answers to; one of them explains the situation with unusual fluency.

Mechanism. Information is being supplied rather than transacted. Per §7.1, test 9, facts arrive without cost to the person parting with them, which means the scene has no engine, which means no want, which means Chatman’s kernel is absent and the scene is a briefing wearing a satellite’s clothes. The secondary damage is to the character domain: a person who exists to explain becomes an instrument, and instruments are not believed.

Remedy. Give the information to someone who does not want to give it, in front of someone who should not hear it. The three components are: reluctance, an audience, and a cost. Any two will usually do.

The exception: Dostoevsky, The Brothers Karamazov (1880), Book Five. The Grand Inquisitor chapter is a scene in which one character recites to another a story he has composed, containing a long speech by a figure who explains, at length and without interruption, an entire theory of freedom, authority and human nature. It is exposition of the most undisguised kind: a monologue, delivered to a silent auditor, in a tavern, with no external action whatsoever.

It succeeds for three reasons, and each is transferable. First, the exposition is the crisis: Ivan is not informing Alyosha, he is attempting to break him, and the ideas are the weapon. Second, the listener is at risk, so the reader watches a person rather than an argument; Alyosha’s silence and his final kiss are the scene’s actual events. Third, the speaker is exposed by what he says — the poem is a confession, and the reader learns more about Ivan than about the Inquisitor.

8.4 The One-Note Scene

Symptom. The scene serves one domain only, competently, and nothing else. It is not wrong; it is under-used real estate.

Mechanism. No mechanism, strictly — this is a pattern of inefficiency rather than of failure, and it is included because at book scale inefficiency becomes a structural problem. Twenty single-domain scenes in a novel of sixty produce a book that is a third longer than it needs to be, and length spent without return is the commonest reason a competent manuscript is refused.

Remedy. The multiplicity pass at §5.3. Look for the latent second function in material already present: the object in the room, the question not asked, the physical business.

The exception: Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878), Part Three, chapters 4–5. Levin mows a meadow with his peasants for a full day. The scene serves one domain — texture — with total commitment: the rhythm of the scythe, the shifting of the line of mowers, the loss of self in labour, the moments when the work does itself and the moments when it does not. The plot does not move. Nobody’s relationship alters. No thesis is stated.

It earns its sustained attention by duration and by conviction. The chapter is long enough that the reader stops waiting for it to become about something else and begins to inhabit it, which is its subject: the experience of work that is not instrumental. It is also positioned against Anna’s chapters, giving the labour a thematic role at the level of the book. Unlike Melville’s chapter at §8.1, its most obvious movement is experiential rather than an explicit change of feeling.

8.5 The Runaway Set Piece

Symptom. A battle, a storm, a party, a chase, executed at spectacular volume, at great length, with every plot, character and relational value sitting where it started. Thrilling on its own; inert as soon as the reader turns the page.

Mechanism. Texture at maximum, everything else idle. The set piece has a specific seduction: it is the material a writer most wants to write, it demonstrates capability, and it produces a strong local reading experience that conceals the absence of consequence. The diagnostic is brutal and simple — could the following chapter be written without it?

Remedy. Put a kernel inside it. The standard technique is to give one character a small, specific, resisted want within the spectacle — get to the door, keep hold of the child, avoid being seen by a particular person — so that the reader has something to track. The spectacle then becomes the resistance rather than the subject.

The exception: Hugo, Les Misérables (1862), Part Two, Book One. Nineteen chapters on the battle of Waterloo, in a novel about a paroled convict in Paris, arriving before the plot resumes. It is the most notorious digression in the nineteenth-century novel and has been cut from abridgements for a hundred and fifty years.

It works — for readers who let it — by two devices. First, there is a kernel buried in it: in the final chapters, a scavenger robbing the dead on the field pulls a wounded officer from under a corpse, and the officer is Marius’s father, and the scavenger is Thénardier. Sixty pages of history exist to place one man in one debt. Second, the digression is the novel’s method rather than an interruption of it: Les Misérables is a book that argues history and individual life are the same substance at different scales, and it demonstrates this by refusing to distinguish them typographically.

8.6 The Errand Scene

This pattern is added to the original five.

Symptom. A character goes somewhere in order to do something, and the going is dramatised: the drive, the walk to the office, the queue, the arrival, the waiting room, the receptionist. The scene’s actual content occupies its last fifth.

Mechanism. Transitional material has been mistaken for scene because it is chronologically continuous with a scene. The plot function is advance, which is the one function summary performs better than scene (§4.1). The errand scene is the purest form of mode error (§6.1): correct material, wrong speed.

Remedy. Modes 5 and 11 (§6.3). She drove to the shop. Or nothing at all — cut from the decision to the arrival, and let the reader supply the road.

The exception: Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925). Clarissa walks from Westminster to Mulberry’s the florist to buy flowers, and the walk occupies a substantial part of the novel’s opening movement. It is an errand, dramatised at length, and it is one of the great passages in modernist fiction.

The mechanism of its success is exact: the errand is not the subject, it is the occasion. What is being narrated is a consciousness, and the street furnishes the stimuli — the squeak of the hinges, Hugh Whitbread, the memory of Bourton — that let a mind be exhibited. The motor car stops outside Mulberry’s as Clarissa is choosing flowers; the aeroplane sequence follows and moves among other observers. The value shifts during Clarissa’s walk are interior, and the walk is the mode of their delivery. Chapter 5 (The Transition) analyses Woolf’s use of the motor car and the aeroplane as connective tissue between consciousnesses; the scene-level lesson is that an errand becomes a scene when the interior is the event, and remains an errand when it is not.

8.7 The Meeting-to-Decide Scene

Added.

Symptom. Three or more characters convene to determine what to do next. They review what has happened, canvass options, disagree mildly, and reach a decision. The scene is often placed at the start of the second act.

Mechanism. Two failures compounded. First, the scene’s content is deliberation, which is a sequel function (reaction–dilemma–decision) staged as a scene; Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) explains why the two half-beats have different requirements. Second, and worse, the deliberation is generally between characters who all want the same thing, so the resistance is nominal — they are solving a problem, not opposing each other, and problem-solving is the least dramatic activity available to a group.

Remedy. Three, in order of preference. Give the decision to one person, made alone and imperfectly, and let the others find out. Make the meeting a fight about something else — the plan may be agreed while an unrelated betrayal is being conducted under the table. Or summarise it: by the time they had finished arguing it was two o’clock and they had agreed on the only thing they could have agreed on.

The exception: Stoker, Dracula (1897). The novel is substantially made of meetings. Van Helsing convenes the group repeatedly; documents are read aloud; typewritten transcripts are collated; plans are formed by consensus. By the diagnosis above the second half of the book should be inert.

It is not, for a reason specific to its form. Dracula is an epistolary and documentary novel, and its meetings are scenes about the assembly of knowledge in a book whose antagonist is defined by what cannot be known about him. The convening is the action: each conference visibly converts scattered private records into a shared account, and the reader watches a group of people build, out of diaries and phonograph cylinders, the only weapon available against a creature that leaves no reflection. The meetings also carry a continuous relational charge — Mina is admitted, then excluded, then admitted again, and her exclusion is what allows the Count to reach her.

8.8 The Unresisted Want

Added.

Symptom. The viewpoint character wants something specific and gets it, at no cost, from someone who has no reason to refuse. The scene passes test 1 and fails test 2.

Mechanism. Want without resistance produces activity, not scene. The reader’s engagement in a scene is a function of uncertainty about the outcome, and an unresisted want has no outcome to be uncertain about. The scene is therefore experienced as administration: something is arranged, and the book proceeds.

Remedy. Three genuine options. Relocate the resistance inside the character — she gets the key, and the asking costs her something she was not prepared to spend. Change what is wanted: she came for the key and leaves wanting to know why he would not look at her. Or grant the want and make the granting the disaster, which is the strongest of the three and leads directly to the exception.

The exception: Dickens, Great Expectations (1861), chapter 18. Jaggers finds Pip and Joe at the Three Jolly Bargemen, then speaks with them in the parlour. He informs Pip that he is to inherit a handsome property and be brought up as a gentleman. Pip wants this, and Jaggers does not oppose that wish. The transaction nevertheless has conditions, and Joe resists Jaggers’s manner and refuses payment for releasing Pip from his apprenticeship.

The scene is one of the pivots of the novel: receiving what he wants begins Pip’s estrangement from Joe and the forge. Dickens registers the cost in Pip’s response to the news and in Joe’s exchange with Jaggers. Biddy’s pointed silence belongs to a later conversation with Pip in the same chapter. The resistance is partly in the transaction and partly in what the reader can foresee of its cost.

8.9 The Scene That Resolves at Its Own Climax

Added.

Symptom. The scene builds to a confrontation, the confrontation occurs, the matter is settled, and the scene closes on the settlement. Everything the scene raised, the scene answers. The reader has no reason to turn the page.

Mechanism. The scene has been treated as a complete narrative rather than as a unit of a larger one. Its value shift is real, but it discharges rather than generates: the dramatic question is closed at the same moment it is answered, so no forward pressure is transmitted to the next unit. Swain’s insistence that a scene should end worse than it began (Chapter 2) is a crude version of this observation, and it is crude because “worse” is not the requirement — continuation is. A scene may end in triumph and still transmit pressure, provided the triumph creates a new problem.

Remedy. Test 8’s exit half. Locate the moment the question is answered, and then ask what the answer makes possible or necessary. Frequently the correct exit is one line later than the resolution, and consists of a small fact that reopens it: the person who was not supposed to be listening, the object that is missing, the concession made too easily.

The exception: Flaubert, Madame Bovary (1857), Part Three, chapter 8. Emma’s death is narrated at length and closes her own story. It leaves consequences for others unresolved: three chapters follow, in which Charles discovers the letters, deteriorates and dies, and Homais receives the croix d’honneur.

The scene works as a close to Emma’s story near the end of the book because its resolution opens the question of what follows her death. Emma’s story ends; the novel’s does not, because the novel’s subject was never only Emma. Flaubert’s structure makes the point the diagnosis needs: a scene may resolve completely, provided the book has a question larger than the scene’s. Where a draft’s scene resolves completely and the book has no larger question, the fault is not in the scene.

8.10 The Second-Draft Scene

Added.

Symptom. A scene that is very well written, that every reader compliments, and that does nothing. It has usually been revised more than any other scene in the manuscript. The prose is the best in the book.

Mechanism. Revision has been applied at sentence level to a structural problem, with the result that the scene’s inertness has been polished into invisibility. The better the prose, the harder the fault is to see — and the more expensive the fault becomes, since a beautifully written inert scene is the last thing a writer will agree to cut.

Remedy. Test 7, applied ruthlessly, and then test 4 run forwards. Ask the question that sentence-level revision cannot answer: if this scene were merely adequate, would I still keep it? If the answer is no, the prose is holding up a scene rather than the scene supporting the prose.

The exception: Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927), Part One. The dinner at which the boeuf en daube is served has no plot function that could not be summarised in a line, contains no confrontation, and is composed almost entirely of surfaces: candles lit, a dish uncovered, awkwardness managed, Lily Briscoe deciding not to say what she thinks, Mrs Ramsay conducting the table. It is also the most heavily worked passage in the novel and one of the most celebrated in English fiction.

It is not inert, and the discrimination matters. What the scene produces is a momentary coherence — Mrs Ramsay’s sense that here, now, something has been made that will outlast the evening — and the whole of the rest of the book is the loss and partial recovery of that coherence. The value shift is real, is registered by one consciousness, and is stated with great precision at the moment of leaving the room, when the scene has already become the past. Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) analyses the dish; the scene-level point is that Woolf’s dinner passes test 3 while appearing not to.
