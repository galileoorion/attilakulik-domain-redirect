Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A scene is a unit of story, rendered in something approximating real time, in which a character pursues a concrete, immediate objective against resistance, such that the state of the story is measurably different at its end from what it was at its start.

Three elements of that definition carry weight, and each is developed later.

  • “Concrete, immediate objective.” Not the story-goal (find out why the locks were changed; get out of the marriage; win the war) but the scene-goal: get him to give me the key; get through this dinner without being asked; find out whether he already knows. Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) makes the same distinction for page one, where the immediate want is what carries a reader who does not yet care about the story-goal.
  • “Against resistance.” The most frequently missing component in drafts, and the subject of test 2 (§7.1). A want that meets no obstacle produces activity rather than scene; the resistance need not be a person, and is often the character’s own reluctance, the situation’s shape, or time.
  • “Approximating real time.” This is the technical heart of the definition and is treated at §3.3. Dramatisation is not a matter of dialogue, or of physical action, or of sensory detail. It is a matter of duration: the rate at which narrative time passes relative to story time.

2.2 How long is a scene?

There is no principled word count, and any number given is a convention. Three delimitations are in circulation and answer different questions.

Delimitation Typical extent Answers the question
The beat 20–300 words What is the smallest exchange in which the balance of power shifts?
The scene 400–3,000 words What is the smallest unit that could be extracted and still read as a story?
The sequence 3,000–12,000 words What is the smallest unit that completes a movement of the plot?

A scene ends when the value it was moving stops moving, or when time, place or viewpoint breaks.

2.3 The term most often confused: summary

The commonest terminological error in workshop discussion is the treatment of summary as a failed scene. It is not a failure mode; it is a different narrative speed with different uses, and the relationship between the two is the subject of §6.

Summary is narrated report: a stretch of story time compressed into a shorter stretch of narrative time, told rather than enacted. “For the next three weeks she did not go near the shop” covers three weeks in twelve words.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the scene
Beat The smallest unit of change: one exchange, one action, one shift in the balance A scene is a sequence of beats; the term comes from acting and screenwriting practice
Sequel In Swain’s model, the reflective half-beat following a scene: reaction, dilemma, decision Treated in full in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel); see §6.6
Set piece An extended scene of spectacle: a battle, a ball, a storm, a party A scene distinguished by scale and by the reader’s expectation of it; see failure pattern §8.5
Scene (screen) A dramatic unit in more or less continuous time and space; it may contain many shots and cuts Often narrower in time and place than the prose sense; the difference is analysed at §10.3
Kernel / satellite Chatman’s terms for events that branch the story and events that amplify without branching The precise vocabulary for what the extraction test (§7.1, test 4) measures

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • The sequel and the scene–sequel rhythm — Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel). This chapter’s §6.6 gives one paragraph and a cross-reference.
  • The internal mechanics of dialogue — Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange), which adopts the four domains and adds a relational fifth of its own.
  • Movement between scenes — Chapter 5 (The Transition) for how a gap is crossed, Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break) for where it is placed, Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) for who is on the other side.
  • Scenes set in the past — Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback), which treats the additional burden a cutaway scene must carry to justify the interruption.
  • The allocation of scenes between plots — Chapter 14 (The Subplot), whose “attention tax” is the page-space form of the argument made here about the cost of a scene.
  • The special obligations of first, prefatory and final scenes — Chapters 9, 10 and 16.
