Chapter 1 · The Purposes of a Scene

9. Worked Analyses

Continue in Core

Eight close readings and a revision case. The analytical apparatus is the same throughout: identify the engine (want, resistance), name the value shift, count the domains, and locate the entry and exit points. Where a work is read in translation, the translator is named, and structural claims are separated from texture claims.

9.1 Eliot, Middlemarch (1871–72), chapters 20–21 — the scene in which nothing happens twice

The situation. Dorothea Brooke, six weeks married, sits weeping in her lodgings in the Via Sistina in Rome. Her husband Casaubon is at the Vatican library. She does not know why she is crying.

The engine. Dorothea’s want is unusually hard to state, which is the scene’s subject rather than its defect: she wants to be given something to do — to be admitted to her husband’s work, to be of use, to have her devotion accepted. The resistance is Casaubon’s temperament, and the scene’s genius is that the resistance is not opposition. He does not refuse her. He fails to notice that anything is being offered.

The value shift. At the start of chapter 20 Dorothea believes her disappointment is her own fault and a failure of understanding on her part. By the end of chapter 21 she has said the unsayable thing aloud — she asks him when he will begin to write the book — and has been answered with a coldness that converts a private unease into a public fact between them. What was a feeling has become a condition of the marriage.

What the chapter is doing that the model cannot name. Eliot inserts, between Dorothea’s tears and the quarrel, a narratorial intervention of the kind Lubbock’s scenic ideal (§3.2) exists to eliminate: the famous passage about the roar on the other side of silence, and the observation that we are all of us born in moral stupidity, taking the world as an udder to feed our supreme selves. Both are commentary; both interrupt the scene’s isochrony; both are essential. What the commentary does is perform the complication of sympathy for Casaubon that no dramatised action could achieve, because it is a matter of the reader’s interpretive stance rather than of any event. The narrator turns and asks the reader to consider that Casaubon also has an inside.

9.2 Flaubert, Madame Bovary (1857), Part Two, chapter 8 — the agricultural show

Read in English; the translations most used are Eleanor Marx Aveling (1886), Francis Steegmuller (1957), Geoffrey Wall (1992) and Lydia Davis (2010). The structural analysis below survives all of them; claims about Flaubert’s rhythm and diction do not.

The situation. The agricultural fair has come to Yonville. In the upper room of the town hall, Rodolphe Boulanger conducts the first serious stage of Emma’s seduction. Below in the square, a government official delivers a speech, and prizes are announced for livestock and manure.

The device. Flaubert intercuts the two discourses without narratorial comment, so that Rodolphe’s declarations arrive punctuated by the announcements from the square. Seventy francs. Manures. Nothing else in the scene is asked to carry meaning; the meaning is generated entirely by the adjacency, and the reader performs the entire operation unaided.

The value shift. Emma passes from a woman who is being approached to a woman who has permitted the approach. Nothing is consummated and nothing is refused. The change is that a boundary has been crossed silently, and the silence is what makes it irreversible.

Domains — this is the definitive multi-domain scene.

Domain What the scene does
Plot Advances the affair by exactly one stage and establishes Rodolphe’s method, which the novel will use twice more
Character Rodolphe is exposed by his fluency — a man who has said this before; Emma by what she is prepared to hear
Relational The pair pass from acquaintance to complicity; the shared secret of the interrupted speech is their first possession in common
Theme The novel’s central argument — that romantic language is a manufactured commodity indistinguishable from other agricultural produce — is made without a word of statement
Texture The provincial world at its fullest: crowds, dung, brass, officials, and the ordinary boredom that makes Emma’s hunger comprehensible

9.3 Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813), chapter 34 — the Hunsford proposal

§5.2 anatomises this scene’s sentences. This section analyses its architecture.

Entry. Austen begins with Elizabeth re-reading Jane’s letters and thinking about Darcy’s part in Jane’s distress. The doorbell rings later in the opening paragraph, and Darcy enters in the next. The brief preparation gives Elizabeth’s refusal a motive before the confrontation begins.

The engine. Darcy wants to be accepted, and expects to be. Elizabeth wants him gone, and — more importantly — wants to make him answer for Bingley and for Wickham. The resistance is mutual and total, which is why the scene generates more heat per word than anything else in Austen.

The structure. The scene has three movements, and the reader can feel each turn:

Movement What happens The shift
1. The declaration Darcy proposes, at length, dwelling on the objections he has overcome Elizabeth passes from astonishment to anger
2. The refusal Elizabeth refuses, then names Jane and Wickham Darcy passes from confidence to incomprehension to fury
3. The exchange of charges Each accuses the other of a failure of manners and of justice Both pass from certainty to a wound they cannot yet name

The value shift. Stated flatly: at the start, each believes their view of the other is correct and complete. At the end, each has been told, by the only person whose opinion they cannot dismiss, that it is not. Nothing is resolved. Both are wrong about most of it. The scene’s shift is from certainty to injury, and the injury is what makes the rest of the novel possible.

Why it is the standing example for the relational domain (§4.5). Ask the character-domain question — has either person changed? — and the honest answer is no. Ask the relational question and the answer is that everything has. A model without a relational category is obliged to describe the most consequential scene in the novel as a scene in which nothing happened to anybody.

9.4 Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1878), Part Two — the steeplechase

Translations in common use: Constance Garnett (1901), Louise and Aylmer Maude (1918), Rosamund Bartlett (2014), Marian Schwartz (2014), Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky (2000). Structural claims survive; the famous plainness of Tolstoy’s Russian does not survive uniformly.

The situation. Vronsky rides his mare Frou-Frou in an officers’ steeplechase. Anna watches from the stand, beside her husband. At the last obstacle Vronsky makes an ill-timed movement in the saddle, comes down badly, breaks the mare’s back, and she is destroyed. Anna’s reaction in the stand is visible. Karenin observes it, and observes that others have observed it. On the drive home, Anna tells him she is Vronsky’s mistress.

The double structure — the scene’s principal lesson. Tolstoy narrates the race twice: once from Vronsky’s side, as an experience of speed, judgement, and a physical error; and once from Karenin’s side, as a social occasion during which he watches his wife’s face. The two narrations are of the same minutes and are entirely different scenes.

Narration Focaliser Want Resistance Value shift
The race Vronsky To win The course; the horse; his own body Confidence → a shame he cannot discharge
The stand Karenin To prevent a public scene Anna’s face; the presence of society Suspicion → certainty, and then the requirement to act on it

Domains. Plot (turn — the confession follows directly and irreversibly). Character (Vronsky’s carelessness is exhibited in the one domain where he is competent, which is far more damaging than a scene of social carelessness would have been). Relational (two relations move in opposite directions in the same ten minutes). Theme (mirror: a man destroys a beautiful creature that trusted him, by a moment’s misjudgement, and receives sympathy for his distress).

9.5 Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment (1866), Part One, chapter 7 — the murder

Read here in Constance Garnett’s translation (1914); Pevear and Volokhonsky (1992), David McDuff (1991) and Oliver Ready (2014) are also in common use. Structural claims survive translation; the celebrated feverishness of the Russian syntax does not survive it evenly.

The engine. Raskolnikov wants to carry out a plan. The resistance is almost entirely material and procedural: a door that must be got through, a woman who must be got past, a bolt, an axe under a coat, the interval before someone comes upstairs. Dostoevsky’s decision to make the resistance practical rather than moral is the scene’s central technical choice, and it is why the chapter reads faster than anything else in the novel.

The value shift, and why it is not the obvious one. The obvious answer — he was not a murderer, and now he is — is true and insufficient. The scene’s operative shift is that the crime he committed is not the crime he planned. Lizaveta is the pawnbroker’s harmless half-sister; she walks in; he kills her because she is there. The theory that authorised the first killing has no account of the second, and Raskolnikov spends the rest of the novel unable to tell himself a story in which he is the man he intended to be.

9.6 Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958), chapter 7 — the killing of Ikemefuna

The situation. Ikemefuna, a boy who has lived in Okonkwo’s household for three years and calls him father, is taken out of the village to be killed, in accordance with an oracle’s decision. Okonkwo has been warned by an elder to have no hand in it. He walks with the party.

The engine. The scene’s want belongs to Ikemefuna, and this is Achebe’s crucial choice: for most of the walk the reader is with the boy, who is thinking about his mother, wondering whether she is still alive, and playing a childhood game in his head with the rhythm of his own footsteps. He wants to get home. The resistance is invisible to him and known to everybody else, including the reader.

The mechanism: dramatic irony as the engine. The scene generates almost unbearable pressure with no conflict whatever, because the reader holds information the focaliser does not. Nothing needs to be dramatised except walking. This is the strongest available refutation of the belief that a scene requires opposed wants; here there is one want, no opposition, and the resistance is the reader’s knowledge.

The value shift. Two, in three lines. Ikemefuna passes from alive to dead. And Okonkwo passes from a man who has been told not to do a thing to a man who has done it — and Achebe supplies the reason immediately and without commentary: hearing the boy call him father and run to him, he cut him down, because he was afraid of being thought weak.

Domains. Character (reveal and test, in a single act). Theme (the novel’s argument about the cost of a masculinity defined negatively — as the absence of everything the father was — is made in one clause). Plot (turn: the act is the origin of Okonkwo’s decline). Relational (a father-son relation is destroyed by the person who held it, which is why it registers as more than a killing).

9.7 Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), chapter 5 — the reunion at Nick’s cottage

The situation. Gatsby has arranged, through Nick, for Daisy to come to tea. It is raining. Gatsby has had the lawn cut and sent flowers. He waits.

The engine. Gatsby wants an outcome he has spent five years and an enormous fortune constructing. The resistance is, of all things, embarrassment — his own. The scene’s daring is that its obstacle is not Daisy, not Tom, not the class barrier, but the fact that a man of formidable competence cannot get through the first ten minutes of the thing he has organised his life around.

The clock. It is the best single object in any scene discussed in this chapter, and it earns its place by not being a symbol first. It is a physical accident, produced by a man leaning too far back, and it is funny. Only afterwards does the reader register that a man attempting to reverse five years has knocked over a stopped clock and caught it before it fell. Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail) treats the general principle; the scene-level lesson is that an object multiplies when its literal function is complete.

The value shift. Gatsby passes from anticipation to possession, and Fitzgerald’s decisive move is to make this a loss. Nick’s observation near the chapter’s end — that there must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams, not through her own fault but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion — converts a scene of fulfilment into the novel’s first statement of its thesis.

9.8 James, The Portrait of a Lady (1881), chapter 42 — the vigil

Why this scene is the chapter’s central example. It has no dialogue, no second character, no physical action and no external event. A woman sits down by a dying fire and does not get up until dawn. By every surface criterion the workshop would apply, it is not a scene at all. It passes every test in §7.

The situation. Isabel Archer, having discovered Madame Merle and her husband in a room together — Osmond seated, Madame Merle standing, a configuration whose impropriety is entirely a matter of familiarity — sits alone after the guests have gone, and thinks.

James on the chapter. In the preface to the New York Edition, James identified this chapter as one of the best things in the book, and described it as a representation simply of her motionlessly seeing, an attempt to make the still lucidity of her act as interesting as the surprise of a caravan or the identification of a pirate. He noted that it was designed to have all the vivacity of incident and all the economy of picture, and claimed that it throws the action forward further than twenty incidents would have done. This is the most useful single statement by a novelist about the scene of pure interiority, and it should be read alongside Lubbock (§3.2), whose whole system was built out of James’s practice and which has no comfortable place for this chapter.

The engine. Isabel wants to understand what she saw. The resistance is her own resistance: the understanding is unbearable, and the chapter proceeds by her repeated approach to and retreat from it. This is the purest available case of resistance located inside the focaliser (test 2), and it is why the chapter has more forward pressure than most chases.

The value shift, stated in the required form.

Start: Isabel believes her marriage is a private failure of feeling, and that her choices were her own. End: Isabel knows her husband hates her, that the hatred is settled and will not change, and suspects that her freest act was arranged for her.

Duration. In Genette’s terms (§3.3, §6.3) this is the interiorised scene, mode 9: story time is a few hours, narrative time is a chapter, and the isochrony is maintained not by dialogue but by the pace of thought. James’s achievement is to make consciousness move at a legible rate — the chapter has stages, each with its own recognition, so that the reader experiences progression rather than accumulation. A draft that attempts the interiorised scene and fails almost always fails here: the thought does not advance, it circles.

The chapter’s use in this textbook. It is the standing refutation of every mechanical reading of the model. A student who has concluded that scenes require dialogue, opposition, movement, event or a second person should be sent to chapter 42 and asked to explain what is missing. Everything the model requires is present; only its usual carriers are absent. The distinction between a function and its carrier is the distinction on which this chapter’s tests rest, and §9.8 is where it can be seen most clearly.

9.9 A revision case: the ledger scene

A note on placement. In the household novel the ledger is first planted as an object in Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail), and Elena discovers its changed handwriting in the register drawer at a chapter break in Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break). The scene worked below imagines a later confrontation, in which Nadia, told of the discovery by Elena, confronts Dov at the shop. It is an exercise and not part of the novel’s sequence, and nothing in it binds the novel.

Version A (draft)

Nadia had been at the shop since nine. She had counted the receipts in the drawer twice, which took a while because the drawer stuck on the K and only came free on the third try, the way it always had. Dov arrived at eleven with a roll of bin bags and started on the shelves.

Elena had found the ledger the previous week and rung her about it. Their father had kept it for eleven years, in his careful slanting hand with the crossed sevens — prices and repairs and the ordinary arithmetic of keeping a small shop alive. But after the ninth of March the writing changed. It was looser, and it was not his.

Nadia had thought about this a great deal. She had known for some time that her relationship with her brother was not what it had been. Since their father’s death, things had been difficult between all three of them, and she found it hard to trust Dov the way she once had.

“You’ve been writing in it,” she said at last. “Haven’t you.”

Dov admitted that he had. He explained that he had not wanted to worry her or Elena, and that he had only been keeping up the accounts because somebody had to. Nadia said she understood. They finished the shelves together and went out for lunch, and neither of them mentioned it again that day.

Diagnosis

Test Result
1 — Want Fails. Nadia is at the shop; she is not trying to get anything. The passage never states an objective, and the scene begins two hours before anything is at issue
2 — Resistance Fails. Dov concedes immediately and explains himself fully. Nothing is in the way
3 — Value shift Marginal. Start: Nadia suspects Dov continued the ledger. End: Nadia knows Dov continued the ledger. A suspicion is confirmed, which is the weakest available shift, and it is confirmed by his simply saying so
4 — Extraction Backwards: the reader loses one fact, already available from the changed handwriting. Forwards: nothing later becomes unearned
5 — Domain count X only, and thinly. Character is asserted, not evidenced; the relational shift is stated (“she found it hard to trust Dov”), which is the domain’s characteristic under-delivery (§4.5)
6 — Redundancy Fails. The reader learned that the hand changed after March the ninth at the chapter break; this scene re-delivers it
7 — Summary substitution Nadia asked Dov about the ledger. He said he had not wanted to worry them. Nothing is lost
8 — Entry and exit Fails both. Enters at nine in the morning with a receipt count; exits after lunch
9 — Pressure Fails. Every fact is delivered as briefing: eleven years, the crossed sevens, the date, the state of the siblings’ relations. No one is reluctant to say anything
10 — Register Reflective, in a position that calls for action
— Patterns present: the errand scene (§8.6) at the opening; the expository scene in disguise (§8.3) in paragraph two; the unresisted want (§8.8); the second-draft scene in embryo (§8.10) — the prose is not bad, which is why the fault is hard to see

Version B (revision)

The ledger was in the register drawer, open at the ninth of March. Nadia took it out and held it up. “You’ve been writing in it.” Dov went on wrapping the shelf stock in newspaper, not looking up. “Somebody had to. I knew something was wrong.” He held out his hand for it. She watched his hands, steady, too steady, and put the ledger back in the drawer and shut it, and kept her palm flat on the wood. He didn’t argue. That was the part that frightened her.

Eighty-nine words. The domain audit:

Domain What the scene does
Plot Opens a question (what did Dov see on the ninth of March that told him something was wrong?) which the novel answers only in Dov’s own chapter (Chapter 13, The Viewpoint Shift). A plant, placed without emphasis
Character Dov is revealed by a physical tell rather than by report: hands that are steady when they should not be, and a man who goes on working through an accusation. Nadia is revealed by what she does with the ledger rather than by what she says
Theme If the novel’s question is what the living owe the dead’s silences, the scene stages it: two people negotiating over a document neither will read aloud
Texture The domestic chore — wrapping stock in newspaper — sets a register of ordinary business interrupted, not of melodrama
Relational The scene’s principal transaction. The outstretched hand is refused, and the ledger goes back into the drawer under Nadia’s palm. Afterwards neither of them holds it, and the right to it is open between them in a way neither would have predicted a week earlier

Apply the value-shift test in the required form:

Start: Nadia believes Dov’s silence is evasion, and expects him to deny it. End: Nadia knows he will neither deny it nor explain it, has kept the ledger from him, and has discovered that his not arguing frightens her more than a lie would have.

What the revision did, procedurally

  1. Entered at the last possible moment (test 8). The receipt count, the arrival, the bin bags, the two hours of shelving are gone. The scene begins with the ledger already open in the drawer and the accusation already formed.
  2. Made the concession the problem. Version A’s fatal move is Dov’s full explanation. Version B gives him one sentence that concedes the small fact and withholds the large one — Somebody had to. I knew something was wrong — so that what he says increases the reader’s uncertainty rather than resolving it. This is the pressure test (test 9) satisfied by making the speaker reluctant.
  3. Replaced a stated relation with a transacted one. “She found it hard to trust Dov” becomes an outstretched hand and a refusal. The relational shift is now an event with a location in the room. This move transfers most readily to other scenes.
  4. Converted exposition into physical business (§5.3). The wrapping of stock does character, texture, relational and plot work simultaneously — a man packing a shop is a man who has already decided to sell it — without an expository sentence.
  5. Exited on the last moving beat. Version A continues through lunch. Version B stops on that was the part that frightened her, which is the moment the value stops moving, and leaves the sequel — Nadia alone with the ledger — to the next unit (Chapter 2, Scene and Sequel).

What Version B has not solved

First, it is too tidy. Eighty-nine words containing five domains, a plant, a physical tell and a closing line of interpretation is close to the density ceiling (§5.4), and the last sentence — that was the part that frightened her — is the narration telling the reader how to read the preceding gesture. A more confident version would end on He didn’t argue, and trust the reader. Whether the interpretive line is a weakness or a legitimate act of narration is arguable; a workshop should argue it.

Second, the redundancy is unresolved. If Chapter 6 has already given the reader the changed handwriting, then Version B’s value shift is not Nadia learns Dov continued the ledger but Nadia learns that Dov will not defend himself. That is a real shift, but it is a narrower one than the scene’s staging implies, and the scene should probably be rewritten so that the accusation is not about the handwriting at all.
