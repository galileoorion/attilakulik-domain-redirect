Chapter 14 · The Subplot

4. The Domains of Purpose

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The domain model asks a single question of a secondary chain: what does this thread supply that the main plot, by its own nature, cannot? It is the operational form of the attention tax. A subplot that cannot answer has not failed aesthetically; it has failed to justify its page-share, which is a different and more tractable complaint.

The model’s value is diagnostic. It converts this subplot feels like filler into a set of separable questions with separable remedies: the thread is doing pacing relief and nothing else; the thread is doing thematic counterpoint but the counterpoint duplicates rather than opposes; the thread is doing structural convergence but the convergence arrives before the reader has invested.

Its dangers are two, and both should be named before the taxonomy begins.

The retrospective alibi. Any thread can be described, after the fact, as serving some domain. “It’s thematic counterpoint” is the universal solvent of workshop discussion, and it is unfalsifiable as usually deployed. §8.13 is built on this failure, and §7.1, test 4 is worded to close the loophole: the requirement is not that the thread has a thematic relation but that a reader can say what the relation asserts and what would be lost if it were not asserted.

The checklist reflex. A writer who learns six domains will attempt to serve six. The result is the over-freighted subplot — a thread carrying so many declared functions that it has no shape of its own. §4.8 addresses this directly.

Five domains follow, then a proposed sixth at §4.6. Each is set out with: what it supplies; its carriers — the structural features that realise it; discussion; characteristic under- and over-delivery; and illustrations from published fiction.

4.1 Thematic Counterpoint

What it supplies: a second treatment of the book’s governing question, conducted by other people under other conditions, so that the main plot’s answer is legible as an answer rather than as the only one available.

Carriers

Carrier What it looks like in the text Levin’s category
The parallel dilemma A secondary character faces a structurally similar choice at a different social level or in a different register Analogy
The road not taken A character does what the protagonist refused, or refuses what the protagonist did Contrast
The inverted outcome The same pressure produces the opposite result — the marriage that works beside the one that fails Contrast
The differently-priced success A character achieves what the protagonist wants and finds it costs more, or less, than expected Analogy with variation
The shared object or institution A single thing — a lawsuit, a shop, a will, a house — that both threads must deal with, differently Analogy made concrete
The rhyming phrase or gesture A repeated verbal or physical figure appearing in both threads Forster’s rhythm (§3.1)

Discussion

Thematic counterpoint is the domain the tradition values most highly and the domain most often claimed without being delivered. The distinction that separates delivery from claim is the distinction Levin gives us between analogy and duplication.

An analogical relation restates the main plot’s question in different terms, so that the difference in terms is itself informative. A duplicative relation restates it in the same terms, so that the reader learns that the book believes what it already said. The first produces the effect Empson describes — a meaning belonging to neither thread alone. The second produces §8.4, the redundant mirror.

The practical test is a subtraction. Ask: what does the book assert with this thread that it could not assert without it? If the answer is “the same thing, again, about someone else”, the thread is duplicative. If the answer names a qualification, a cost, an alternative or a limit, the thread is counterpoint.

A second discrimination worth having. Counterpoint may operate at the level of outcome or at the level of terms. Outcome-counterpoint gives two characters the same problem and different endings; it is the easier form and the more visible. Terms-counterpoint gives two characters the same ending reached by incompatible routes, or the same route to incompatible ends, and it is where the domain does its finest work — because it prevents the reader from converting the book into a proposition.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the decorative rhyme. The thread resembles the main plot without asserting anything about it. A reader notices the resemblance, waits for it to mean something, and is not repaid. Symptom: the writer can point to the parallel but cannot say what it argues.
  • Under-delivery: the unmarked analogy. The counterpoint is present and the reader misses it, because eighty pages separate the two halves of the comparison. This is a braiding-rhythm failure (§6.6) masquerading as a thematic one, and it is far commoner in novels than in plays for the reason given at §3.2.
  • Over-delivery: the thesis thread. A thread that exists visibly to make a point, populated by people who are positions. The reader experiences this as being lectured by the architecture. Symptom: the thread’s characters have no wants that are not illustrative.
  • Over-delivery: the underlined rhyme. The book states the parallel — a character notices that their situation resembles someone else’s, or the narrator points it out. Occasionally superb (Eliot does it deliberately and with irony); usually a confession that the writer does not trust the structure.

Illustrations

Tolstoy, Anna Karenina (1875–77). The definitive case, analysed in full at §9.2. Two chains — Anna and Vronsky; Levin and Kitty — treat one question: what is a life organised around love actually worth, and what does it cost to want the truth about it? The threads are joined causally at the start (Vronsky’s pursuit of Anna releases Kitty, whom Levin eventually marries) and touch once, late, to no consequence. Everything else that connects them is counterpoint, and it is the most sustained example of the domain in the European novel.

Shakespeare, King Lear (c. 1606). The Gloucester action repeats the Lear action in a different key: a father who misjudges his children, is betrayed by the one he trusts, and is redeemed by the one he cast out. Levin’s analogy in its purest form; the play is discussed at §9.8 because the entire modern theory of the double plot was built on it.

Eliot, Middlemarch (1871–72). Four treatments of vocation and its compromise: Dorothea’s thwarted desire for a life of consequence, Lydgate’s medical ambition destroyed by domestic economy, Fred Vincy’s slow acquisition of a usable trade, Bulstrode’s piety financed by a concealed fortune. The counterpoint is at the level of terms: each character discovers a different relation between what they wanted and what a provincial society will fund. See §9.1.

Thackeray, Vanity Fair (1847–48). Becky and Amelia are set as contrast in its most schematic available form — the adventuress and the sentimentalist — and the novel’s achievement is that the schema does not hold. See §9.5.

4.2 Character Testing Ground

What it supplies: pressure of a kind the main plot cannot apply, revealing facets of a character that the primary conflict has no occasion to reveal.

Carriers

Carrier What it looks like in the text What it reveals
A different register of pressure Money where the main plot has love; family where the main plot has war; the body where the main plot has the mind How the character behaves when the stakes are of a kind they have not rehearsed
A relationship the main plot has no room for A sibling, a subordinate, a child, an old friend Behaviour unobserved by the people the main plot cares about
Command or responsibility The character is put in charge of something What they do with power, which the main plot may only let them seek
A smaller failure A defeat with low stakes, early A rehearsal that lets the reader calibrate the character before the large defeat
A situation with no audience The character acts where no one who matters is watching The gap between performance and conduct — Chapter 3 (The Dialogue Exchange) supplies the vocabulary

Discussion

The main plot tests a character in the terms of its own conflict, and those terms are necessarily narrow: a novel about a murder tests its protagonist’s capacity for concealment and its detective’s capacity for attention, and has little occasion to discover whether either is kind to their mother. A subplot can apply a pressure of a different kind, and the value of the different kind is that it produces information the main plot cannot fake.

There is a structural point underneath this that is easy to miss. In the main plot, a character’s behaviour is always partly explicable by the stakes: they lied because their life depended on it. Under the smaller stakes of a subplot, behaviour is unexcused, and unexcused behaviour is the most efficient characterisation available to prose. A man who is brave under fire has told the reader about the fire. A man who cannot tell his sisters he has sold a shop has told the reader about himself.

The domain’s structural requirement. For a testing-ground thread to pay its tax, the trait it reveals must be load-bearing later. A thread that reveals an interesting but inconsequential facet has delivered characterisation without consequence, which is texture at subplot prices. The revealed trait should be the trait on which a main-plot decision eventually turns.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the anecdote. The thread reveals something and nothing is done with it. Symptom: the character’s behaviour in the subplot could be reversed without altering a single main-plot scene.
  • Under-delivery: redundant testing. The subplot applies the same pressure as the main plot at lower stakes, so the reader learns what they already knew. This is the redundant mirror (§8.4) in its character-domain form.
  • Over-delivery: the personality showcase. A thread constructed so obviously to exhibit a quality that the exhibition becomes the thread’s only content. Frequently identifiable by the absence of a real reversal: the character is tested and passes, which is a demonstration rather than a test.
  • Over-delivery: fragmentation. So many different pressures applied that the character reads as inconsistent rather than as complex. Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) treats the viewpoint version of this problem; the plot version is that a character with three subplots has no centre.

Illustrations

Tolstoy, War and Peace (1865–69). Nikolai Rostov’s card game with Dolokhov is the model instance. The war has tested Nikolai’s courage and found it real; the card table tests something the war cannot reach — his relation to his family’s money, his shame, and his inability to say no to a man he admires. He loses an enormous sum to a man who has just been refused by Sonya, and must go home and tell his father. Nothing in a battle chapter could have produced the scene in which the old count says he will find the money.

Dostoevsky, The Brothers Karamazov (1880). The Ilyusha thread — Alyosha and the schoolboys — puts Alyosha under a pressure the parricide plot cannot apply. In the main action he is a mediator among adults whose positions are fixed; among the boys he must actually do something, and the novel’s argument about active love is tested where it can fail. Discussed at §9.7.

Cervantes, Don Quixote (1605, 1615). Sancho’s governorship of Barataria is a testing ground in the purest sense: a comic subplot that takes the novel’s least reliable judge of reality and gives him authority. He governs well. The main action could not have discovered this, because the main action is organised around his subordination.

Dickens, Bleak House (1852–53). Richard Carstone’s slow ruin is a testing ground for John Jarndyce as much as for Richard: the reader learns what Jarndyce’s benevolence can and cannot do, and learns it under a pressure the Esther plot never applies to him.

4.3 Pacing Relief

What it supplies: a change of register, rhythm or stake that allows the main plot’s tension to be sustained without the narrative stalling — and, more narrowly, that allows the reader to be released without the story being suspended.

Carriers

Carrier What it looks like in the text Effect
Lower stakes The subplot’s worst outcome is survivable Permits the reader to relax without abandoning the book
A different tonal register Comic beside tragic, domestic beside political, warm beside cold Restores the reader’s capacity to feel the main register
A different tempo Slower scenes, longer sequels, more description Chapter 5 (The Transition) and Genette on duration supply the vocabulary
Deferral across a break The main plot is left mid-crisis while the secondary thread runs Converts relief into suspense; see Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break)
A different social world Servants, children, animals, strangers Relief by change of company as well as change of stake

Discussion

Pacing relief is the domain that most often appears alone, and a thread serving only this domain is the shape readers correctly identify as filler. But the domain itself is not disreputable, and the reason should be stated carefully, because craft advice tends either to dismiss it or to over-credit it.

Tension is a relational quantity. A narrative held at maximum pressure for two hundred pages does not read as unbearable; it reads as monotonous, because the reader loses the contrast against which pressure is felt. This is the same argument the model chapter makes about density at the sentence level in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) §5.4: emphasis requires unstressed material around it. At book scale, relief is what supplies the unstressed material.

Relief is not the same as pause. A descriptive pause, a digression or a chapter of exposition also lowers the pressure, but does so by stopping the story. A subplot lowers the pressure while a story continues to run, which is why the domain exists at all. The reader is released from one anxiety into another, smaller one, rather than into nothing.

The interruption dividend. There is a second and less obvious mechanism. Cutting away from the main plot at a moment of maximum pressure and running a secondary thread converts the relief into suspense: the reader wants to return, and the wanting is a form of tension that the main plot could not have generated by continuing. This is the braided version of the deferred decision from Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) §6.2, and it is the most efficient available use of the domain — but it depends entirely on the interruption being short. §6.6 gives the interval.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the holiday. The thread is pleasant, undemanding and unconnected. The reader relaxes so completely that returning to the main plot requires effort. Symptom: the reader can skip the thread and lose nothing they can name.
  • Under-delivery: relief without a chain. Material that lowers the pressure but has no goal, conflict or consequence of its own. This is a digression (§2.4) and should be judged as one.
  • Over-delivery: the tonal breach. Relief so far from the main register that the book appears to have changed genre. A comic thread inside a grief novel is not automatically wrong — but the reader must be able to hold both, and the test is whether the comedy knows about the grief.
  • Over-delivery: relief as the dominant experience. The reader begins to prefer the relief. This is one route into the main-plot hijack (§8.3), and the more insidious one, because it is produced by a domain the writer believed to be modest.

Illustrations

Dickens, Bleak House. The Bagnet household — the old soldier, his wife, the birthday dinner at which the meat is always overcooked — is pacing relief performed by a master, and it is instructive that it is not only relief. The Bagnets stand surety for George, which converts a warm domestic runner into a mechanism by which Chancery-like debt reaches the one household in the novel that seemed safe from it. Relief that turns out to be plot is the domain at its highest value.

Tolstoy, War and Peace. The alternation of the title is, at one level, a pacing structure: the domestic Rostov chapters exist so that the campaign chapters can be felt. What prevents this from being mere alternation is that the peace chapters carry the novel’s argument about where life actually happens, so the relief is also the thesis. See §9.4.

Shakespeare, Henry IV. The tavern scenes are the canonical instance of comic relief that is not relief, and Empson’s reading (§3.3) is the reason the phrase “comic relief” should be used with care. See §8.7.

4.4 Structural Convergence

What it supplies: an eventual intersection with the main plot — the subplot’s chain complicating, obstructing, accelerating or resolving the primary one — so that the reader’s separate investment is repaid by integration rather than left stranded.

Carriers

Carrier What it looks like in the text Chatman’s terms (§3.4)
The supplied object The subplot possesses a thing the main plot needs — a document, a key, a witness, a name The subplot’s kernels become the main plot’s
The supplied constraint The subplot imposes a deadline, a cost or a prohibition on the main plot Direct linkage in Levin’s sense
The supplied person A character formed in the subplot enters the main plot changed Convergence by transfer
The shared secret One fact belongs to both chains and its exposure resolves both Bleak House’s method
The collision The two chains’ goals turn out to be incompatible The strongest form; convergence as conflict
The revealed identity A person in one thread turns out to be a person in the other Powerful, and the most easily abused (§8.9)

Discussion

Structural convergence is the only domain of the six that is inherently obligatory in a hierarchical book: some form of convergence is required, or the subplot is a second story running in parallel. This chapter accepts the requirement and immediately loosens it in three directions.

First, convergence may be thematic rather than literal. This is Levin’s central contribution (§3.2): a relation of analogy can integrate a work whose actions never meet. A thread that never touches the main plot in event but is inseparable from it in argument has converged, in the only sense that matters to a reader’s sense of unity. Anna Karenina is the great demonstration, and §9.2 argues that its refusal of literal convergence is a positive achievement rather than a defect tolerated.

Second, convergence is not a moment but a gradient. Threads converge by degrees: a shared minor character, then a shared location, then a shared consequence, then a shared crisis. Manuscripts that treat convergence as a single late event usually have a braiding problem rather than a convergence problem — the thread has not been touching the main plot at all, and then touches it decisively, which reads as contrivance however well prepared the contrivance is.

Third, the timing of convergence is a design variable, not a rule. Early convergence lets the subplot feed the main plot’s machinery for the rest of the book; late convergence generates a larger payoff and requires a larger investment to have been paid in. §6.4 tabulates both, with their costs.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the parallel line. No convergence of any kind, literal or thematic. §8.1.
  • Under-delivery: convergence by proximity. The threads end up in the same room, and this is treated as convergence. Nothing about either chain has changed; the characters have merely met. §8.10.
  • Over-delivery: the mechanism thread. A subplot that exists solely to deliver a plot component — the key, the letter, the witness. Everything it does is instrumental; nobody in it wants anything for themselves. Symptom: the thread ends the moment it has handed over the object.
  • Over-delivery: total convergence. Every thread resolving into one, at one time, in one place, with everyone present. The nineteenth century did this routinely and got away with it; contemporary readers have a lower tolerance, and the effect is of a form closing rather than a story ending. Chapter 16 (The Ending) treats the general problem.

Illustrations

Dickens, Bleak House. The convergence apparatus of English fiction, and unsurpassed. Three chains — the Chancery suit, the Dedlock marriage, Esther’s search for her origin — converge on a single fact: that Esther is Lady Dedlock’s daughter by the law-writer whose handwriting Lady Dedlock recognises, in one shiver, in Chapter 2. The recognition is planted before the reader knows there is anything to recognise, and every subsequent thread runs towards it. See §9.3.

Dickens, Great Expectations (1861). Magwitch is the textbook case of the deferred convergence: a thread apparently closed in the first fifty pages returns to reveal that it was the main plot all along. The structure is discussed as a braiding option at §6.4, structure 9.

Thackeray, Vanity Fair. Becky’s production of George Osborne’s letter to Amelia — a note written years earlier, kept, and finally used — is a convergence that resolves Amelia’s chain using an object generated by Becky’s. It is also, characteristically, an act of cruelty performed as a kindness. See §9.5.

4.5 World Expansion

What it supplies: access to territory — social, geographical, institutional, historical — that the main plot’s protagonist and goal have no occasion to visit, delivering scope without diverting the primary chain.

Carriers

Carrier What it looks like in the text Risk
A different class or caste The thread’s characters occupy a social position the protagonist does not Condescension; the picturesque poor
A different institution Court, hospital, army, church, market, school The thread becoming an essay about the institution
A different place Another city, another country, the road between Travelogue
A different faction The antagonists’ side, seen from inside Sympathy leakage; see §8.3
A different scale of event The national, the historical, the geological, against the domestic The historiographical digression (§9.6)
A different species of knowledge Trade, craft, law, medicine, whaling Research displayed rather than used

Discussion

World expansion is the domain most associated with genre fiction and most maligned in literary craft advice, and the association is a historical accident rather than a principle. The nineteenth-century social novel used it constantly and for serious purposes: a novel arguing that a society is a single connected system requires the reader to see more of the system than one life touches. Dickens’s method is unintelligible without it.

The domain’s structural difficulty is that it is the only one of the six that can be served without a chain. A writer wanting scope can simply describe the other territory, and the describing will be a digression rather than a subplot. This is why world expansion is the domain most often served by material that fails §2.1’s definition — and why §9.6 treats Les Misérables as the limit case of the whole chapter.

The discrimination that makes the domain usable. Ask whether the new territory is entered by someone who wants something there. A thread that visits a hospital because a character needs a hospital expands the world as a by-product of pursuing a goal. A thread that visits a hospital so that the reader may see one has left narrative for exposition, and should be judged by the standards Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) applies to expository material.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the tour. The reader is shown a new part of the world and nothing happens in it. Symptom: the thread has a setting and no reversal.
  • Under-delivery: the isolated territory. The new world never affects the old one. Scope without consequence, which reads as padding at the moment the writer feels most expansive.
  • Over-delivery: the world eats the book. The expansion is more interesting than the main line, and the reader begins to resent returning. See §8.3.
  • Over-delivery: research on parade. The thread exists to carry what the writer learned. The diagnostic is Chekhovian: if the material would be equally at home in a different novel, it is research rather than world.

Illustrations

Dickens, Bleak House. Jo the crossing-sweeper and Tom-all-Alone’s exist so that the novel’s argument — that Chancery’s corruption is continuous with the slum’s contagion — can be made as narrative rather than as assertion. Jo has a chain: he wants to be let alone and to move on, is prevented, and dies of it. The world expansion and the thematic counterpoint are the same material.

Tolstoy, War and Peace. The most ambitious use of the domain in the tradition, and the one that most tests its limits, because Tolstoy’s expansion eventually leaves narrative altogether for historiography. §9.4 separates the two operations.

Melville, Moby-Dick (1851). The crew of the Pequod — Queequeg, Stubb, Flask, Pip, the try-works, the mates’ watches — supplies world without supplying a second chain, and the cetology chapters supply world with no narrative at all. Discussed as digression at §8.11.

Hugo, Les Misérables (1862). The Friends of the ABC and the barricade are world expansion that becomes structural convergence: a political thread introduced for scope turns out to be the mechanism by which Valjean’s chain and Marius’s chain are resolved together. See §9.6.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: Temporal Counterpoint

The five domains above are all defined by content — theme, character, register, structure, world. There is a sixth relation available between two chains that none of them names, because it is a relation of rate rather than of substance.

Temporal counterpoint. A subplot running on a clock different from the main plot’s — slower, faster, or located in another period — whose function is to make the main plot’s pace legible by contrast, and to let the book represent a duration it could not otherwise contain.

The argument for admitting it

First: pace is invisible without a comparison. A novel whose events all move at one rate has no way to tell the reader that they are moving fast, or slowly, because the reader has nothing to measure against. Introduce a second chain running at a different rate and both become perceptible at once. Chapter 5 (The Transition) establishes that duration is a manipulable variable at the level of the join; temporal counterpoint is the same variable operated at the level of the book.

Second: it lets a novel hold two durations at once. Some subjects require a long clock and some require a short one, and a single chain must choose. A novel about a family across three generations cannot also render three days in detail; a novel about three days cannot also render three generations. A second thread on a different clock is the only structure that lets a book do both without leaving narrative for summary. This is what the domain supplies in the strict sense the model requires: it is a capacity the main plot structurally cannot have, because a chain has one rate.

Third: it is already in the tradition, unnamed. Bleak House runs Esther’s months against Jarndyce and Jarndyce’s generations, and the discrepancy is the novel’s argument about institutional time. Wuthering Heights runs two generations against each other, so that the second is read as a slowed and survivable version of the first. Contemporary dual-period fiction — Possession and The Hours are familiar instances — is built entirely on this relation, and the standard craft account of it, which treats the earlier period as backstory, misdescribes it: the past-period thread is a concurrent narrative line with its own chain, not an analepsis serving the present one.

Fourth: it explains a common failure the other five domains cannot. Manuscripts frequently contain a secondary thread that is competently constructed, thematically related and properly converged, and still reads as inert. A recurring cause is that both threads run at the same rate, so the braid produces no rhythmic variation and the crossings feel arbitrary. That diagnosis is unavailable without the domain.

Carriers

Carrier Realisation
Different scene-to-summary ratio One thread rendered scenically, the other in summary; Genette’s duration, applied thread by thread
Different elapsed time per page The main plot covering days, the secondary thread covering years
Different period A concurrent chain set in another era, braided rather than flashed back to

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under-delivery: the rates differ but nothing is made of the difference; the reader registers unevenness rather than counterpoint.
  • Under-delivery: the slow thread is slow because it lacks a chain, and “different clock” is being used to excuse an absent engine. This is the domain’s own version of the thematic alibi (§8.13), and it is the strongest argument against admitting the domain at all.
  • Over-delivery: the discrepancy becomes the book’s whole content, and the two threads stop being stories about people.
  • Over-delivery: the slow thread’s rate makes it unreadable in isolation, so it survives only as an idea about structure.

The argument against admitting it

The case against is serious and should be put at full strength.

  1. It may be a mode rather than a domain. The other five name things a subplot supplies to the reader. Temporal counterpoint names a property of the telling. One could argue it belongs with the braiding structures of §6.4 — a way of executing a thread — rather than with the reasons for having one.
  2. It rarely appears alone. Every example above also serves thematic counterpoint. If a domain never occurs unaccompanied, it may be an aspect of other domains rather than a domain in its own right.
  3. It licenses a bad thread. Admitting a domain defined by rate gives every slow, inert secondary line a defence. The alibi risk is higher here than for any other domain in the taxonomy.
  4. The dual-period novel may be a genre, not a structure. If temporal counterpoint is really the description of a fashionable form, elevating it to a domain confuses a period style with a narrative capacity.

The chapter’s own position is that (1) and (2) are answerable and (3) is not. The domain is admitted, with the standing requirement that a thread claiming it must also demonstrate a chain — and the reader should treat §4.6 as this chapter’s most contestable paragraphs rather than as settled doctrine. Chapter 8 (Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture) adopts the domain at the scale of the whole book as a property of the arrangement, claimed only where a crossing shows the difference of rate, which answers the objection this section could not; for threads in one time, the condition above stands (its Full Treatment, section 3.6). It adopts Chapter 4’s proposed temporal domain at the same scale.

Seminar question. Is temporal counterpoint a sixth domain of purpose or a braiding technique misfiled? Argue the case using Bleak House’s Chancery clock, Wuthering Heights’ two generations, and one contemporary dual-period novel of your choosing. A strong answer will consider whether the same argument would force the admission of spatial counterpoint as a seventh domain — and if not, why not.

4.8 Must a subplot serve all six?

No, and the attempt is a recognisable pathology.

The domains are not a checklist but a budget, in the sense Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) uses for its contracts. What is required is not that all six be served but that the total be worth the page-share — and that at least one domain be served in a way the main plot could not have served itself.

Three working propositions, offered as rules of thumb and marked as such.

  1. One domain alone is rarely enough, and the domain that most often appears alone is pacing relief. This chapter endorses the claim in its practical form: a thread that can only claim relief is a candidate for trimming, or — better — for being given the second job it could do in the page count it already occupies.
  2. Two domains is the working minimum, and thematic counterpoint plus structural convergence is the most durable pair. It is the combination readers describe as “essential” rather than “tolerated”, because the thread means something and does something.
  3. More than three declared domains usually indicates a co-plot, not a subplot. A thread doing four jobs is not an efficient subplot; it is a second novel that has not been recognised as one. When the audit at §5.5 returns four or more domains, the right question is §7.1, test 10 — the promotion question — rather than a further round of tightening.

And the anti-checklist qualification, which should be stated as firmly as the model itself:

A subplot that serves one domain magnificently beats a subplot that serves four adequately. The domains describe what a thread can do, not how much it must do. The Ilyusha thread in The Brothers Karamazov would score poorly on a mechanical audit of world expansion and pacing; it closes the novel.
