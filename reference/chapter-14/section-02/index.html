Chapter 14 · The Subplot

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion of subplots is unusually loose, and the looseness is not innocent. “Subplot” is routinely used for four different things — a secondary chain, a recurring minor character, a thematic motif, and any material that is not the main line — with the result that the advice attaching to it is incoherent. A writer told to “cut the subplots” and a writer told to “add a subplot for texture” are being told to operate on different objects.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A subplot is a secondary chain of causally linked scenes possessing its own goal, conflict and consequence, distinct from the main plot’s, running alongside it in story time, usually sharing characters or world with it, and competing with it for the same finite page count and the same finite reader attention.

Four elements of that definition carry weight.

  • “Chain.” The requirement is causal linkage in the sense established in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel): each unit descends from the one before it, so that the thread reads as things that had to happen next rather than things that also happened. This is the whole of the distinction between a subplot and a recurring character, and it is developed at §2.3.
  • “Its own goal, conflict and consequence.” A subplot is not a smaller version of the main plot’s engine; it is a separate instance of the same engine. Its goal belongs to its own characters and is measured against its own stakes. A thread whose only goal is help the protagonist is not a subplot; it is main-plot machinery distributed across other people.
  • “Alongside it in story time.” A subplot runs concurrently. Material set entirely in the past is treated in Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback) as analepsis, not here — though §4.6 proposes a genuine borderline case, the subplot running on a different clock, and argues that it belongs to this chapter.
  • “Competing for the same finite page count.” The competitive relation is definitional rather than incidental. It is what generates the attention tax, and it is why the removal test (§7.1, test 9) is the chapter’s most brutal and most useful instrument.

2.2 Scale: how much of a book is a subplot?

There is no principled threshold, and any number is a convention rather than a discovery. But the ranges below are stable enough across the tradition to be useful in diagnosis, and a manuscript that falls outside them is usually doing something it has not decided to do.

Extent (share of total pages) What the reader experiences Typical designation
Under 3% A recurring detail or a runner; no chain is legible Motif; running gag; texture
3–8% A thread the reader tracks but does not weigh Minor subplot; the “C-plot” of screen practice
8–20% A second story with its own stakes and its own arc Subplot proper; the classic “B-plot”
20–35% A co-plot; the reader is not certain which line is primary The double plot; Vanity Fair, Bleak House
Three or more threads of 15–35% each No thread is primary; the relation between them is the subject The multi-plot novel; Middlemarch, War and Peace

Two inferences follow from the table.

The 8–20% band is where the attention tax bites hardest, because it is large enough for the reader to notice the diversion and too small for the book to have declared itself a multi-plot novel. In this chapter’s estimate, most diagnosable subplot failure occurs here.

The bottom two rows are not larger subplots; they are a different form. A book in which four threads each take a quarter has not multiplied its subplots; it has abolished the category. This is the qualification §3.8 develops, and it is the reason this chapter’s title is faintly misleading about a third of the novels it discusses.

2.3 Presence is not a plot

A frequent error in manuscript diagnosis is to mistake a recurring character for a subplot. The distinction is exact and can be settled in one operation.

Test. Write down the character’s goal at their first appearance and at their last. If the two are the same goal in an unchanged state — or if there is no goal to write down — there is no chain. There is a person who keeps arriving.

Both configurations are legitimate. A recurring character with no chain of their own may be doing indispensable work: supplying information, embodying a social world, providing the friction against which the protagonist is defined, carrying a motif. What such a character cannot do is discharge the domains at §4 that require a chain — structural convergence in particular, which has nothing to converge.

The confusion persists because the surface features are similar. Both produce scenes away from the protagonist’s central pursuit; both recur; both feel, in draft, like a second story. The difference is invisible at scene level and obvious at book level, which is why the diagnosis belongs here rather than in Chapter 1.

A corollary for revision. A recurring character who is nearly a subplot is one of the most economical opportunities in a manuscript. The scenes already exist and are already paid for. Supplying the character with a goal, an obstacle and a reversal — converting presence into chain — frequently costs a few hundred words and converts inert page-share into a functioning thread. §7.1, test 1 is designed to find these.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the subplot
Main plot The chain carrying the reader’s primary investment The subplot’s competitor for attention, and usually its eventual destination
Double plot Two chains of comparable weight, neither clearly subordinate Empson’s and Levin’s object; a subplot that has grown into a co-plot (§2.2)
Underplot Older English term, especially in Renaissance drama criticism, for a socially “lower” second action Levin’s material; often but not always comic
Counterplot A second chain constructed as the inversion of the first Contrast (Levin) as a whole design; §6.4, structure 8
B-plot / C-plot Screen-writing terms for secondary and tertiary lines within an episode Useful for proportion; misleading in prose because they presuppose episode-length resolution (§10.4)
Episode A single self-contained incident that does not recur or build Fails the chain requirement; a detour, not a subplot
Digression Non-narrative material — essay, history, catalogue — inserted into the narrative Has no chain at all; treated as the subplot’s limit case at §6.4, structure 14, and §9.6
Frame narrative An enclosing story within which the main narrative is told Structurally outside rather than beside; see Chapter 10 (The Prologue) and §6.4, structure 10
Parallel plot Two chains that never intersect A subplot that has failed to converge, or a deliberate structure; §8.1
Intercalary chapter A chapter of general or collective material alternating with the narrative The Grapes of Wrath; a braid one of whose strands is not a chain (§8.12)
Runner A repeated small piece of business, comic or otherwise, with no chain Texture; below the subplot threshold (§2.2)
Motif A recurring image, object or phrase Carries thematic relation without narrative causality; see Chapter 4 (The Descriptive Detail)
Foil A character constructed to contrast with another A relation between characters; a subplot is a relation between chains
Kernel / satellite Chatman’s distinction between events that open alternatives and events that do not The chapter’s sharpest instrument; §3.4

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • Backstory and flashback. Material set before the present narrative is analepsis and belongs to Chapter 7 (Backstory and Flashback), and the arrangement of several timelines across a whole book to Chapter 8 (Non-linear and Multi-timeline Architecture). The borderline case — a second thread running in an earlier period as a concurrent narrative line — is claimed for this chapter and defended at §4.6.
  • Prologues and frames. A separated preliminary unit is treated in Chapter 10 (The Prologue). Where a frame converges by revelation it appears here as a braiding structure (§6.4, structure 10), but the frame’s own justification is Chapter 10’s problem.
  • Viewpoint allocation. Which character narrates a thread is Chapter 13’s (The Viewpoint Shift) question. This chapter assumes the allocation and asks what the thread does. The two questions are near-inseparable in practice, and §9.3 on Bleak House is where they are treated together.
  • Transitions between threads. The mechanics of the crossing itself — how a reader is carried from one line to another without disorientation — belong to Chapter 5 (The Transition). This chapter is concerned with the rhythm of the crossings (§6.6), not their execution.
  • Series instalments and series arcs. A plot line spanning several volumes is discussed at §10.3; the structural problems of the series as a form belong to Chapter 17 (Series and Genre).
  • Ensemble narrative without chains. A novel that distributes attention across many characters without giving any of them a secondary chain — much of Mrs Dalloway, much of Ulysses — is not a multi-plot novel in this chapter’s sense. §10 returns to this.
