Chapter 14 · The Subplot

12. Studio Work

Continue in Core

12.1 Practical exercises

Exercise 1 — The thread chart. Produce the chart described at §6.5 for a completed draft or for a published novel: one row per chapter, one column per thread, a mark where each is active. Do not annotate it. Then write 300 words on what the shape alone reveals — where the braid tightens, where a thread disappears, whether the crossings accelerate.

Exercise 2 — The page-share and domain audit. Run the full procedure at §5.5 on a draft. Produce the table. Write 400 words on the two largest discrepancies between the audit and your prior sense of the book.

Exercise 3 — The engine conversion. Find a recurring character in your draft who has no chain. Give them a goal with an object and a deadline, one obstacle, and one reversal, using only scenes that already exist. Write the three sentences of test 1. Then write 200 words on what the conversion cost in new material — the answer is usually startlingly little.

Exercise 4 — Substitution testing. Take one troubled thread and re-chart it under three different structures from §6.4 — for example a block, an alternating braid, and a repeated intersection. Do not write prose. Write 400 words comparing what each arrangement buys and costs for this particular material.

Exercise 5 — The convergence hunt. For a published multi-plot novel of your choice, locate the exact point at which each secondary thread’s kernels become the main plot’s (§3.4). Give page references. Then write 300 words on how the author prepared each convergence, and how far in advance.

Exercise 6 — The analogy sentence. For every thread in your draft, complete the sentence at §7.1, test 4, and name the two scenes between which the understanding is produced. Any thread for which you cannot name the two scenes has a braiding problem; propose the crossing that would fix it.

Exercise 7 — The dormancy repair. Identify your draft’s longest dormancy interval. Write the 200-word crossing that would halve it — a scene, not a summary — and place it. Then compare the resumption as originally written with the resumption after the insertion.

Exercise 8 — Levin’s classification. Take a novel with two clear chains and classify their relation as analogy, contrast, direct linkage, or a mixture, giving textual evidence for the classification and identifying the point at which the relation changes. 600 words. Strong answers will find a novel whose relation changes more than once.

Exercise 9 — The removal, performed. Cut your draft’s largest subplot on the outline. Write the resulting book’s synopsis in 500 words. Then answer, in writing: what is lost, in which domain, and could it be supplied in one scene? Keep the answer; do not act on it for a month.

12.2 Seminar questions

  1. Does Anna Karenina converge? §9.2 sets out three positions: that the plots are causally independent and the novel therefore fails a unity criterion; that Levin’s analogy is a sufficient form of unity; and that the unity question is malformed because it imports a dramatic criterion into a form built against drama. Take a position and defend it against the other two. Tolstoy’s own defence of the book’s architecture may be used as evidence but not as authority.

  2. Is temporal counterpoint a domain? §4.6 proposes a sixth domain and states four objections to it. Argue for or against admission, using Bleak House’s Chancery clock, Wuthering Heights’ two generations and one contemporary dual-period novel. A strong answer will address whether the same argument would force the admission of a spatial counterpoint as a seventh.

  3. Does the terminal kernel redeem Waterloo? §9.6 puts two positions on the Waterloo digression in Les Misérables. Is a hundred pages of history justified by the paragraph in which Thénardier robs Marius’s father, or is the justification a critical courtesy extended to a writer we have decided to admire? What would settle the question?

  4. Whose lives get plots? §3.5 argues that the distribution of chains across a cast carries an argument about whose experience is consequential. Test the claim against two novels of your choosing in which a large group of people appears constantly and is never given a chain. Is the absence a formal necessity, an ideological fact, or both — and does the distinction survive examination?

  5. Is the attention tax a description of narrative or of a market? §3.6 argues that contemporary suspicion of subplots is partly an artefact of book length, and book length is an artefact of publishing economics. If that is right, what remains of the tax as a narrative principle? Test the model against one novel from before 1900 and one from after 1990.

  6. Should the main plot always win? §7.1, test 10 asks whether the book would be better with the subplot promoted. Consider Vanity Fair, in which the subordinate thread takes the book and the novel is improved, and one contemporary novel in which the same thing happens and the novel is not. What distinguishes the two cases — design, management, or something the reader supplies?

12.3 Assessment rubric

For a submitted structural analysis of a braided narrative (2,000–2,500 words) accompanied by a thread chart, or for a portfolio of two subplot chapters (3,000–4,000 words) with a 1,000-word critical commentary.

Band Craft / analysis (60%) Commentary (40%)
Distinction The thread has a chain with at least two kernels; domains served are plural and inseparable; convergence is located by page and is a consequence rather than a coincidence or an encounter; braiding rhythm is designed and the dormancy intervals are affordable; the relation between chains asserts something the main plot cannot assert about itself Names the relation in Levin’s terms and defends the classification from the text; identifies the thread’s own risks, including the promotion question; engages at least two theoretical sources critically rather than decoratively; distinguishes what the model shows from what it cannot see
Merit A chain is present with a clear reversal; two domains served, though the second is thinner; convergence located and prepared; braiding competent, with one avoidable dormancy Accurate self-analysis; theory applied rather than cited; risks acknowledged if not resolved
Pass A chain exists; one domain served; convergence present but late or lightly prepared; braiding regular but unexamined Describes the structure accurately; theory referenced but not applied
Fail No chain — presence mistaken for plot; no convergence of either kind; page-share unrelated to contribution; one or more failure patterns unrecognised Description without analysis; the thematic alibi offered as justification
