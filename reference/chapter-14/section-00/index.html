Chapter Abstract

A subplot is the largest optional unit in the novel. A scene can be cut in an afternoon; a subplot may occupy eighty pages, six chapters and a third of a book’s emotional weather, and cutting it is a structural operation rather than an edit. That scale is what makes the subplot the hardest unit in this textbook to teach, and the one on which the most confident craft advice is the least reliable.

This chapter formalises the problem in four movements.

  1. Theory — six independent bodies of work that convert a craft intuition into an analytic. Aristotle’s unity of action supplies the oldest objection and E. M. Forster’s pattern the oldest defence. Richard Levin’s The Multiple Plot in English Renaissance Drama (1971) supplies the vocabulary of plot relation — analogy, contrast, direct linkage — and the crucial argument that a thematic relation can integrate a work whose plots never physically meet. William Empson’s “Double Plots” (1935) supplies the strongest available defence of the second chain: that the relation between two plots generates meaning that neither carries alone. Seymour Chatman’s kernels and satellites (1978) supply the sharpest single instrument in the chapter. Alex Woloch’s character-system, approached here from the plot side rather than the character side, supplies the political dimension. A historical strand establishes that the multi-plot novel is period-specific and materially conditioned, and a cognitive strand establishes that braiding rhythm is not a free variable.
  2. Taxonomy — the five domains a subplot can serve (thematic counterpoint, character testing ground, pacing relief, structural convergence, world expansion), each anatomised in terms of its textual carriers and its characteristic under- and over-delivery, together with a proposed sixth: temporal counterpoint, the subplot running on a different clock.
  3. Method — multiplicity applied at book scale; a taxonomy of fourteen braiding and convergence structures with their exemplars, purchases and costs; an account of braiding rhythm — how long a thread may be left dormant and what its resumption costs; and a ten-test diagnostic protocol for revision.
  4. Application — thirteen failure patterns, each paired with a published work that commits the fault and succeeds; eight close readings; a full revision case worked on this book’s running example; and an apparatus of exercises, glossary, sources and further reading.

The chapter’s governing claim is stated as a levy:

Every scene spent on a subplot is a scene not spent advancing the reader’s primary investment. A subplot earns that tax only by supplying something the main plot structurally cannot supply on its own.

The claim is deliberately harsh, and §3.8 and §16 both qualify it, because the tax metaphor presupposes a single primary investment. That presupposition is accurate for most commercial narrative and false for the tradition that produced Middlemarch, Bleak House, War and Peace and Vanity Fair, in which no thread is the plot and the others are not deductions from it. Middlemarch does not have a subplot in the sense this chapter defines. It has four plots, and the relation between them is the novel.

A note on the examples. The illustrative works are chiefly novels likely to remain in print and in discussion, with two plays where the theory of the double plot was built on the stage. A concordance mapping each concept to the work that best displays it appears at §10.6. The second edition’s post-2000 and screen cases are listed at §14.5.

A note on drama. This is a chapter about novels, but the theory of multiple plots was built on the Renaissance stage, and two of its three principal sources (Levin, Empson) are studies of plays. King Lear is therefore analysed at §9.8 as the origin of the critical discussion rather than as a novel-substitute, and the chapter states plainly where the transfer from stage to page requires adjustment.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:

  • Distinguish a subplot from the units most often confused with it — the recurring character, the episode, the digression, the frame, the parallel plot and the motif — and explain why presence is not a plot.
  • Trace a secondary thread’s own goal–conflict–disaster chain in the terms established by Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel), and identify where its causal links break.
  • Apply Chatman’s distinction between kernels and satellites to determine what a given thread is doing inside its own system and inside the main plot’s.
  • Classify the relation between two plots using Levin’s categories of analogy, contrast and direct linkage, and defend the classification from the text.
  • Identify which of the six domains a subplot serves, and diagnose under- and over-delivery in each.
  • Select a braiding and convergence structure from the fourteen tabulated at §6.4, and state what the choice buys and costs.
  • Audit a manuscript’s braiding rhythm, calculating dormancy intervals and estimating reinstatement cost.
  • Evaluate the limits of the attention-tax model itself, including its dependence on a single-investment assumption and its origin in a particular publishing economy.

How to Use This Chapter

Reader Route Purpose
Student in a seminar §§1–6 in order, then §9, then §12 Conceptual grounding, worked demonstrations, guided practice
Writer in revision §7 (diagnostics) → §8 (failures) → §6.6 (braiding rhythm) → §11 (checklist) Triage of a live manuscript at structural scale
Teacher or workshop leader §4, 6.4, 8 for material; §9 for close-reading models; §12.1 for assignments; §12.3 rubric A ready-made teaching sequence with counterexamples built in

Three cautions before beginning.

First, this chapter’s instruments operate at a scale where mistakes are expensive. A diagnosis that a scene is not working costs an afternoon to act on. A diagnosis that a subplot is not working costs a rewrite. The protocol at §7 is therefore designed to produce descriptions rather than verdicts, and the chapter recommends running it twice, several weeks apart, before acting on it.

Second, the chapter’s central metaphor is contestable and the chapter says so. The attention tax is a good instrument for the book that has one main plot. Applied to the multi-plot novel it produces confident nonsense — that Middlemarch should have cut Lydgate, that War and Peace should have chosen a family. §3.8, §5.4 and §16 return to this.

Third, none of the taxonomies here is a formula. They are instruments for noticing, and their proper output is a question. A model that answers “this subplot fails test four” has done nothing until the writer asks why the book might want it to.
