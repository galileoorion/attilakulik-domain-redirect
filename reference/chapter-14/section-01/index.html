Chapter 14 · The Subplot

1. Introduction: The Second Story

Continue in Core

1.1 A structural asymmetry, stated concretely

Consider two chapters of the same novel, adjacent in the printed book.

Chapter 14 belongs to the main plot. A reader arriving at it has three hundred pages of accumulated investment behind them, a question they want answered, and a character whose fortunes they have been tracking since page one. The chapter can be slow, oblique, even inconclusive, and the reader will stay, because staying is what they were already doing.

Chapter 15 belongs to a secondary thread. The reader arriving at it must do four things that Chapter 14 did not ask of them. They must suspend the question Chapter 14 raised. They must reinstate a set of characters, locations and stakes they last encountered forty pages ago. They must re-establish why this material matters. And they must do all of it while a live question is being held unanswered somewhere behind them, which is a cognitive load in itself.

None of this is fatal, and thousands of novels perform the manoeuvre routinely. But the asymmetry is real, and it is not a matter of the second chapter being less well written. The two chapters are held to different standards by the reader, and the difference is structural. Chapter 14 is spending trust; Chapter 15 is asking for a fresh appropriation.

Two further features make the subplot the hardest unit in this textbook.

It is large. The units treated in Parts I and II are measured in paragraphs, pages or, at most, a chapter. A subplot is measured in chapters — sometimes in a quarter or a third of a book. When a subplot is wrong, the error is not local, and it cannot be corrected by revision at sentence level. This is why the diagnostic protocol in §7 belongs to structural editing rather than to line editing, and why it is best applied to a completed draft.

It is optional in a way no other unit is. Every novel has an opening, an ending, transitions, scenes and, if it has more than one character, dialogue. Not every novel has a subplot, and many excellent ones do not. The Stranger, The Death of Ivan Ilyich, The Remains of the Day and Things Fall Apart run one line each — the last with an ensemble around it, but not a second chain. The subplot is thus the one large unit whose presence must be argued for, and the argument is what this chapter supplies.

1.2 The claim in its strong form

A subplot is not free because it is good. It is taxed against the story the reader actually came for, and the tax has to be worth paying.

The metaphor of a levy on attention will be used throughout, and it should be clear what it does and does not claim.

  • It does claim that reader attention is finite and rivalrous — that a page given to one thread is not available to another, and that the reader’s willingness to be redirected is a resource with a floor.
  • It does claim that the standard a subplot must clear is comparative, not absolute. The question is never is this good? but is this better than what the same forty pages could have done for the main line?
  • It does not claim that subplots are suspect. The tax is a standard, not a prohibition. Most of the greatest novels in the European tradition pay it many times over.
  • It does not claim that the main plot is more important than the subplot in some literary hierarchy. It claims only that the reader arrives with an investment already formed, and that a second thread must build its own.
  • It does not apply, or applies only with heavy adjustment, where there is no single primary investment. This is the qualification developed at §3.8, and it is the qualification on which the rest of the chapter depends.

The attention tax is the same argument that Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) makes about identification under the name of the empathy budget. The two chapters share a theoretical source in Alex Woloch and should be read as one argument applied at two scales: Chapter 13 asks who the reader is permitted to become; this chapter asks whose story the reader is asked to follow. A reader will tolerate a great deal of the second. The first is a scarcer faculty and a larger request.

1.3 Where this chapter sits in the book

It belongs to Part III, The Architecture of the Whole, whose chapters take the whole book rather than a unit within it as their object. Its dependencies run backwards through the book and are load-bearing rather than decorative.

Two relations deserve to be stated rather than tabulated.

The relation to Chapter 2 is definitional. This chapter’s entire definition of a subplot — a chain, not a presence — is borrowed from the six-beat engine. A character who recurs without a goal, meets no resistance and suffers no reversal has no chain and therefore no subplot, however often they appear. Everything in §2 follows from that borrowing, and a student who has not read Chapter 2 should read it before this one.

The relation to Chapter 5 is mechanical. Every crossing from one thread to another is a transition, and it incurs the orientation costs Chapter 5 sets out: the reader must be re-anchored in time, place, viewpoint and stake. Chapter 5 handles how the gap is crossed; Chapter 6 handles where the break is placed; Chapter 13 handles who is on the other side. This chapter handles the fourth question those three leave open — whether the thing on the other side deserves to exist at all.
