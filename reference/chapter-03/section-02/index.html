Chapter 3 · The Dialogue Exchange

2. Definitions and Boundaries

Continue in Core

Craft discussion of dialogue is prone to a specific terminological collapse: the word dialogue is used indiscriminately for the material inside quotation marks, for the technique of representing speech, and for the structural unit. These are three different things with three different failure modes, and the chapter begins by separating them.

2.1 The unit under study

Working definition. A dialogue exchange is a passage of spoken interaction between two or more characters, each pursuing a want through what they say, in which the balance of information, power or feeling between the speakers is measurably different at the end than at the start.

Four elements of that definition carry weight.

  • “Spoken interaction.” The unit is the interaction, not the speech. A page of one character talking at another who does not respond is a speech, not an exchange, and it obeys different rules — see §2.3 and, for the limit case, the analysis of the Grand Inquisitor at §9.3.
  • “Each pursuing a want.” Both sides. An exchange in which only one party wants something is an interrogation or a briefing; those are legitimate forms, but they are one-engined, and the diagnostic protocol will find them.
  • “Through what they say.” The want is pursued by means of the talk. If the talk is incidental to how the want is pursued — two people negotiating while the real action is that one of them is stealing something — the exchange is a texture layer over a different unit.
  • “Measurably different.” The value-shift test from Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene), at the smaller scale. What has changed may be small: a fact has passed, a claim has been conceded, a form of address has altered.

2.2 Scale and extent

There is no principled length for the unit, and any number given is a convention rather than a discovery. Three ways of delimiting it are in circulation, and they answer different questions.

Delimitation Extent Answers the question
The turn One speaker’s uninterrupted contribution What does this line do that the last one did not?
The exchange A bounded stretch of turns on one matter, with a beginning and an outcome Has anything moved?
The dialogue scene An entire scene whose action is talk Does this scene earn its page count?

This chapter treats the middle delimitation as primary, because it is the unit at which purpose becomes visible. A single turn is too small to shift a value; a whole scene is large enough that a failing exchange inside it can hide behind a functioning one elsewhere. In practice an exchange in the sense used here runs from roughly six to sixty turns, and its boundaries are marked by a change of topic, a change of participants, or the arrival of the outcome that the exchange was pursuing.

A useful rule of thumb for revision: the exchange begins at the first turn that could not be cut without loss, and ends at the last one. Most drafted exchanges have three or four turns of approach at the front and two of retreat at the back; both are the writer’s runway, not the reader’s requirement. The equivalent observation about openings appears in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), where it is called throat-clearing.

2.3 The term most often confused with this one: conversation

The distinction a reviser most needs is between conversation and exchange.

Conversation is a social activity whose primary function is frequently the maintenance of the relationship rather than the transfer of anything. Much of it is what Malinowski called phatic communion: talk whose content is nearly irrelevant to its purpose, which is to establish and confirm that the channel is open and the relationship is intact. Greetings, weather, the ritual enquiry after health, the closing sequence — these are enormously important socially and almost inert narratively.

An exchange, by contrast, is conversation under load. The distinction is not a matter of subject: two people can discuss the weather in an exchange and their own divorce in a conversation. It is a matter of whether anything is being contested.

Conversation Exchange
Primary function Maintain the channel and the relationship Pursue incompatible wants
Ends when The participants are done Someone gets something, or is refused
Value at stake None, or the relationship as a standing condition A specific value in a specific domain (§4)
Narrative use Establishing register; marking time; providing the baseline against which deviation registers Carrying a scene
Diagnostic Removable, with only texture lost Removable only at identifiable cost

Novels use both, and the error is not writing conversation; it is mistaking conversation for exchange — which is the dialogue-specific form of the error Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) identifies when it distinguishes a scene from scenery.

2.4 Adjacent terms

Term Meaning Relation to the dialogue exchange
Turn One speaker’s contribution, bounded by the transitions on either side The exchange’s smallest constituent; the unit of conversation analysis (§3.2)
Adjacency pair A two-turn sequence in which the first makes a particular second relevant — question/answer, offer/acceptance, greeting/greeting The mechanism by which a missing second turn becomes meaningful; the technical basis of the evasion mechanisms in §6.4
Speech (as a unit) Extended uninterrupted talk by one character Not an exchange, though it may sit inside one; §9.3
Monologue Sustained speech, addressed or unaddressed Distinct: Cohn’s quoted monologue is interior, not spoken
Reported speech Speech rendered by the narrator rather than quoted — she said she would not come A compression option, and the alternative any removal test must consider (§7.1)
Free indirect speech Speech or thought rendered in the narrator’s grammar and the character’s diction, unquoted The boundary case; see Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) and §9.4
Attribution / tag he said, she asked — the identification of a speaker A technical apparatus with its own history; §3.5, §7.1 test 9
Action beat An action inserted between turns in place of a tag (called simply a beat in the first edition; this edition keeps beat for Chapters 1 and 2) Carries the relational domain more often than writers notice; §4.3
Subtext What is wanted, feared or withheld beneath the line The chapter’s central mechanism; §6

2.5 What this chapter excludes

  • Interior monologue and the representation of thought — Cohn’s territory, and Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift). This chapter treats thought only where it interacts with speech, chiefly as the narrator’s licence to expose the gap between the two channels.
  • Prose rhythm and sentence craft in the narration — the surrounding prose, as distinct from the speech, belongs to the study of voice.
  • Dramatic dialogue as such. Plays and screenplays are discussed comparatively at §10.4, but the chapter’s model is built for prose fiction, where a narrator stands between speaker and reader. That narrator is the single largest formal difference between novelistic and dramatic dialogue, and most craft advice imported from screenwriting mislays it.
  • Dialect and sociolinguistic variation as a linguistic subject. §3.4 and §8.9 treat its representation as a craft and ethical problem; the linguistics of variation is a field of its own.
  • The chapter break placed inside an exchange — a real technique, and the property of Chapter 6 (The Chapter Break).
