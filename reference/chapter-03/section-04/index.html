Chapter 3 · The Dialogue Exchange

4. The Domains of Purpose

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) established four domains of purpose — plot, character, theme and texture — as lenses for asking what a stretch of prose is actually for. The model’s virtue is that it converts the unusable instruction make this scene better into a set of separable questions with separable remedies.

Dialogue inherits all four and adopts the fifth that Chapter 1 proposes. The relational domain — the negotiation of standing between speakers — could in principle be filed under character, and the usual four-domain model files it there, as realign a relationship. Chapter 1 argues for separating it; in dialogue it will not stay filed. Speech is the primary medium through which humans conduct status, and an exchange can leave both characters unchanged as individuals while permanently altering what each may say to the other. That is a value shift, it is invisible to the four-domain model, and it is what a very large proportion of memorable dialogue is actually doing. Goffman’s face-work and footing (§3.3) are its scholarly form.

Five domains are set out below, followed in §4.6 by a proposed sixth. For each: a definition, why it matters, the textual carriers through which it is realised, a discussion, the characteristic under- and over-delivery, and illustrations from published fiction.

Two warnings about the model before it is used.

  • It is a diagnostic instrument, not a specification. Its correct use is retrospective: applied to a drafted exchange to discover what it is doing. Applied prospectively — this exchange will serve plot and theme — it produces the overstuffed, visibly purposeful dialogue described at §8.11.
  • The domains overlap by design. A single line frequently serves three of them, and the point of §5 is that the best lines always do. Categorising a line is not the goal; noticing what a passage is systematically failing to do is.

4.1 Plot — information and decisions change hands

Definition. The exchange moves the causal chain: a fact passes, a commitment is made, a plan is set or broken, a turn occurs.

Why it matters. Dialogue is the novel’s most efficient mechanism for transferring information between characters, and therefore the mechanism most likely to be abused. Almost every failure pattern in §8 is a plot-domain failure in disguise: the writer needed the reader to know something and used two characters as a delivery system.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
Information under pressure A fact arrives because someone is forced to give it up The speaker’s reluctance is visible; the fact costs something to say
The forced decision A character commits to something on the page, in front of a witness A future-tense first-person sentence with a named consequence
Negotiation Terms offered, refused, countered Explicit conditionals; the vocabulary of exchange
The turn Confession, exposure, a lie caught Usually a single short turn after a long one
The revised plan Characters agree, disagree, or alter what happens next Specificity sufficient to bind: a time, a place, a name

Discussion

The plot domain’s central craft problem is that information is most needed by the reader at the moments when it is least plausible for a character to supply it. The solution the whole tradition converges on is not to remove the information but to make it cost something, and the mechanism has three parts:

  1. Give the fact to someone who does not want to say it. Reluctance converts transmission into an event.
  2. Give the need to someone who already suspects part of it. A listener who is testing rather than receiving changes the exchange from a briefing into an interrogation, and the reader watches a contest instead of overhearing a summary.
  3. Let the fact arrive incomplete. Information delivered in full is spent; information delivered as a fragment that raises a further question is, in Sternberg’s terms, converted from an expense into an asset — the same economy Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) applies to exposition.

Note that none of this alters the information. What changes is that a relational value moves at the same time (§4.3), a character is revealed by what the disclosure costs them (§4.2), and the reader has been made to work.

A second, less discussed plot function is the binding commitment. A decision made in a character’s head can be revised silently; a decision made aloud to another character has a witness, and the witness is what makes it structural. This is why the dialogue exchange is the natural site for the Decision beat that closes a sequel in the model treated in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel): saying it aloud is what converts a resolution into an obligation.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the exchange in which nothing passes. Two characters discuss a situation both fully understand, and the reader learns only what they already knew. Diagnosed by test 1 (§7.1).
  • Over: the briefing. Every turn carries a fact; nobody resists; the reader is being brought up to date. This is the As-You-Know-Bob exchange (§8.1) and the Expository Scene in Disguise identified in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene).

Illustrations

1. Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813) — the fact extracted by an antagonist. Lady Catherine de Bourgh arrives at Longbourn to demand that Elizabeth deny an engagement to Darcy. The plot information at issue — whether an engagement exists — is a fact Elizabeth does not possess; what she possesses is a refusal to promise. The scene’s plot work is therefore performed by an absence: Elizabeth declines to give the assurance demanded, Lady Catherine carries the refusal to Darcy, and Darcy reads it as encouragement. A fact is created by a conversation that transmits no facts. It is the most elegant piece of plot machinery in the novel, and it works entirely through what one character will not say.

2. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — information delivered as a weapon. When Miss Kenton informs Stevens of her engagement, the plot content is a single fact with a date attached. Its function is not informational but tactical: the announcement is made in order to be responded to, and Stevens’s failure to respond is the event. The plot domain and the relational domain are here the same act seen from two sides, which is the normal condition of good dialogue rather than an unusual achievement.

4.2 Character — the speaker is revealed

Definition. The exchange discloses who is speaking: through diction, through what they avoid, through what they claim about themselves, and through the distance between the claim and the evidence.

Why it matters. Speech is the most concentrated characterisation the novel possesses, because it is the only element in which the character rather than the narrator selects the words. Everything else in a novel is filtered; dialogue is, by convention, unfiltered — which is why a false note is so much more damaging in speech than in narration.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
Differentiated diction Vocabulary, register, syntax specific to this speaker The line misfits in any other mouth (§7.1, test 8)
Characteristic sentence shape Length, subordination, whether the speaker finishes Consistent across scenes; the most reliable single marker
Evasion What the speaker deflects, minimises or declines A question left unanswered; an adjacency pair left open
The tested value A stated belief put under direct pressure by another speaker An assertion in one scene; a choice under it in another
Self-description The speaker’s account of themselves, measurable against the evidence Any sentence beginning I’m not the sort of person who
The register shift A change in how a character speaks when the audience changes Same character, two scenes, two idioms

Discussion

The last carrier is the most under-used and the most powerful. A character who speaks one way to a superior and another way to a subordinate has been characterised twice over: once by each register, and a third time by the fact of the switch. Goffman’s footing (§3.3) is the formal account; the craft observation is simply that a single consistent voice per character is a beginner’s target, and the professional target is a consistent repertoire. The reader who notices that a character’s sentences get shorter in a particular person’s presence has been told something no description could supply.

Evasion deserves separate emphasis because it inverts the intuitive relation between speech and disclosure. What a character will not say is more characterising than what they will, for the structural reason set out at §3.2: a question makes an answer conditionally relevant, so a non-answer is a noticeable absence and the reader is licensed to interpret it. This is the character domain and the silence domain (§4.6) meeting, and it is why the silence test (§7.1, test 5) is among the most productive in the protocol.

The self-description carrier is the character domain’s cheapest reliable effect and its most easily overplayed one. A character who says I’ve never been any good at this has supplied the reader with a claim, and the reader will spend the rest of the book measuring it. The cost is that the claim must then be measured: an unexamined self-description is a wasted line, and a self-description the book endorses without testing is a character speaking the author’s notes aloud.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the interchangeable line. Grammatically clean, plausibly spoken, attributable to any of six characters. This is the ventriloquist exchange (§8.2), and it is a common defect in drafted dialogue.
  • Over: the tic. A character marked by a repeated verbal signature — a catchphrase, a dialect spelling, a habitual oath — which substitutes a label for a person. Dickens is the great exponent and the great cautionary case: Mrs Micawber’s I will never desert Mr Micawber is a triumph of comic characterisation and a demonstration of how little such a device can be made to yield in a serious register.

Illustrations

1. Hurston, Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937) — character established by what a speaker forbids. When Janie is called on to make a speech at the lighting of the lamp, Joe Starks answers for her: his wife knows nothing about speech-making, he never married her for anything like that, and her place is in the home. Three short clauses characterise Jody completely — the proprietary mah wife, the retrospective justification, the appeal to a general principle — and characterise Janie by the silence they impose. The domain of character and the domain of relation are indistinguishable in the line, and the silence domain (§4.6) is doing at least as much work as either.

2. James, The Portrait of a Lady (1881) — self-description as a philosophical position. Madame Merle’s account of the self to Isabel — that there is no isolated person, that the self overflows into the house one lives in, the clothes one wears, the company one keeps — is simultaneously a worldly woman’s practical creed and the novel’s central question posed as social observation. Isabel’s reply insists on the opposite: that nothing belonging to her is any measure of her. Neither woman is stating a position for the reader’s benefit; each is defending a way of living against the other. That is the difference between a philosophical exchange and an essay read aloud by two speakers, and it is a difference of want, not of content.

3. Dostoevsky, The Brothers Karamazov (1880; trans. Constance Garnett) — the value tested by its own advocate. Ivan tells Alyosha that he accepts God but returns the ticket — that no future harmony can justify the suffering of one tortured child. The passage is character work rather than theme work in the first instance, because what it reveals is a man who has constructed an unanswerable argument and cannot live inside it. Alyosha’s response is not a counter-argument; it is a question about who Ivan is. The theme domain is served because the character domain is served first.

4.3 Relational — the standing between speakers changes

This chapter adopts the fifth domain proposed in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene).

Definition. The exchange alters the balance of power, intimacy, obligation or permission between the speakers — what each may now say to the other, and on what terms.

Why it matters. Status is transacted in talk more than anywhere else, and the transactions are minutely observable: who initiates, who yields the floor, who is permitted to change the subject, who apologises, who is corrected, who uses whose first name. A reader tracks these continuously and unconsciously, which means a writer who manages them deliberately is operating on a channel the reader is already monitoring.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
Address and naming Titles, first names, diminutives, full names, the refusal to use any A change in form of address mid-scene is always an event
Turn-allocation Who speaks next, who interrupts, who is permitted to finish Interruption marked by dash; overlap; a turn abandoned
The floor Who holds it, who takes it, who gives it up Long turns versus short; the length ratio across the exchange
Question rights Who is permitted to ask, and who must answer Sustained one-way questioning is a status structure
Correction Other-correction of a fact, a word, a title Rare in real talk (§3.2); therefore aggressive on the page
Physical position Standing, sitting, in a doorway, behind a desk The action beat, which carries relational value far more often than writers notice
Permission and deference Asking versus assuming; the granting or withholding of an exit May I against I’m going to
Shared reference Private shorthand, allusion, the pointed exclusion of a third party An unglossed reference is a claim of intimacy

Discussion

The relational domain is the reason the doorway is the most useful piece of furniture in fiction. A character who stands in a doorway controls access; a character who must ask to pass has already lost something. Physical arrangement is not setting, in an exchange; it is the diagram of the status relation, and moving a character across the room is a relational move that costs no dialogue at all.

Three practical principles.

1. Status is a change, not a state. An exchange in which the powerful character is powerful throughout has not served this domain; it has illustrated a fact already established. What the domain requires is a movement, or a movement resisted under visible pressure. The status test (§7.1, test 3) exists to catch this.

2. Partial shifts are more interesting than total ones. A character who yields the ground but keeps the one thing that mattered has produced a more legible and more useful shift than one who capitulates. The worked example at §9.9 turns entirely on this: Dov surrenders the doorway and the key and retains the reason.

3. The strongest relational moves are the smallest. A shift from surname to first name; the withholding of a courtesy that has been given in every previous scene; an unanswered greeting; an answer addressed to a third party instead of to the questioner. These outperform declarations because they are acts rather than statements, and because they engage the reader’s inferential machinery (§3.1) rather than reporting a conclusion to it.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the static-status exchange (§8.4). Competently voiced, well paced, and going nowhere, because the same person holds the upper hand at the last line as at the first and nothing has been contested.
  • Over: the status contest as sole content. Two characters engaged in visible dominance display, with no plot fact and no relational history underneath — a dialogue that reads as posturing, common in genre pastiche and in workshop exercises written to demonstrate the concept.

Illustrations

1. Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813) — status inverted inside a proposal. Darcy’s proposal at Hunsford opens from a position of total assumed advantage: he has come to confer something, and the manner of the conferring is itself an assertion of rank. Elizabeth’s refusal removes it. The exchange is analysed in full at §9.1; what matters here is the mechanism, which is that the relational shift is produced by the form of Elizabeth’s reply as much as its content. She answers his declaration with a remark about what is customary on such occasions — meeting a claim of feeling with a point of etiquette, which refuses the frame he has offered before refusing the offer inside it.

2. Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847) — relation determined by an unratified participant. Catherine’s confession to Nelly Dean that it would degrade her to marry Heathcliff, and that she is Heathcliff nonetheless, is a two-person exchange with a third party present whom one of the two speakers does not know is there. Every relational value in the novel is set by this arrangement. The point is developed at §9.5; here it exemplifies Goffman’s participation framework (§3.3) doing structural rather than decorative work.

3. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — status maintained at ruinous cost. Stevens’s professional register is a status instrument: the formality is what allows him to decline every intimacy Miss Kenton offers without ever refusing one. The relational value that shifts across the novel is not power — Stevens’s position is secure — but permission: what Miss Kenton may say to him narrows, scene by scene, until the exchange in which she tells him she is to be married is the last one in which she tries.

4.4 Theme — the argument changes

Definition. The exchange stages the book’s governing question, either by voicing competing positions or by generating irony between what a speaker means and what the reader hears.

Why it matters. Dialogue is the only place in a novel where a proposition can be advanced by someone the author is not answerable for. This is Bakhtin’s polyphony (§3.4) in its practical form: a novel can hold two incompatible positions in genuine suspension because two characters hold them, and the narration is not obliged to adjudicate.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
The staged dialectic Two positions the book is weighing, voiced by two speakers in real tension Neither speaker is a straw figure; both have their best case
The thesis under pressure A character argues for what the book seems to believe, and might lose The narration does not intervene to help
Dramatic irony A speaker’s words mean more, or other, to the reader than to the speaker The reader knows something the speaker does not
The unremarked question The book’s central question posed in miniature and never named Neither speaker notices what the exchange is about
The uncorrected error A false claim allowed to stand The narration’s silence is the comment

Discussion

The theme domain has the highest failure rate of the five, because its failure mode is so attractive. A writer with something to say gives it to a character; the character says it; the domain is served. What has actually happened is that the book has stopped and a position has been announced, which is the difference Bakhtin draws between the monologic and the polyphonic.

The discriminating question is not does someone say the thematic thing? but does the exchange put the thematic thing at risk? A staged dialectic requires that the position the author holds might lose the argument on the page, and that the reader can feel it might. Three tests of whether a dialectic is real:

  1. Is the opposing case made in its strongest form? If the opponent’s argument is one a competent adherent would disown, no dialectic is occurring.
  2. Does the narration refrain from arbitrating? A narrator who tags one speaker’s contributions with he said reasonably and the other’s with she snapped has decided the question.
  3. Does either speaker want something other than to win the argument? Pure argument is essay; the exchange becomes fiction when the argument is instrumental — when one of them is arguing in order to be forgiven, or to postpone, or to make the other stay in the room.

The third is the one workshops most often miss. Ivan Karamazov’s argument with Alyosha is unbearable not because the philosophy is powerful — though it is — but because Ivan is a man trying to make his brother agree with him, and Alyosha is a man trying not to lose his brother.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: thematic decoration. A single line that name-checks the book’s subject in an exchange doing other work, which Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) correctly refuses to count as serving the domain at all.
  • Over: the therapeutic exchange (§8.10) and the mouthpiece. Characters articulating the book’s argument, or their own psychology, in terms the book has provided rather than terms they would have found.

Illustrations

1. Dostoevsky, The Brothers Karamazov (1880; trans. Garnett) — the dialectic staged and left open. The Grand Inquisitor chapter is the extreme case in the European novel, and it tests this chapter’s model to destruction; it is analysed at §9.3.

2. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958) — the argument conducted in the vocabulary of the culture that is being argued about. After the killing of Ikemefuna — a scene which Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) analyses as a scene — Okonkwo and Obierika discuss what was done. Obierika’s position is that the act, though sanctioned by the Oracle, is the kind of thing for which the earth punishes whole families; Okonkwo’s is that a man does not question what the Oracle requires. Neither speaks the language of the novel’s eventual reader. The dialectic is entirely internal to Umuofia, which is what makes it a dialectic rather than an anticipation of the colonial judgement the book will later refuse to endorse. A staged dialectic is strongest when both positions are indigenous to the world.

3. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989) — theme produced entirely by irony. Stevens’s disquisitions on dignity are advanced with total sincerity and heard by the reader as a diagnosis. No opposing position is stated in the novel’s dialogue; the opposition is supplied by the reader from the evidence Stevens supplies against himself. The theme domain is served without a dialectic, by irony alone, which demonstrates that the two carriers are independent.

4.5 Texture — the experience changes

Definition. The exchange calibrates how the passage feels: its tone, its pace, its register, and the reader’s sense of where and among whom they are.

Why it matters. Dialogue changes the visual density and rhythm of a page. Short turns and paragraph breaks can make an exchange feel brisk, while longer turns can slow it. This makes texture a pacing choice in every exchange: the question is whether its rhythm serves the book at that point.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
Turn length and rhythm Short exchanges accelerate; long turns decelerate The visual shape of the page, before a word is read
Register Formal, demotic, technical, ritual Vocabulary and syntax, not orthography
White space The proportion of page given to speech A structural rather than a stylistic decision
Attribution density Frequency and elaboration of tags Heavy tagging slows; stripped attribution accelerates and destabilises
Comic relief A warm or funny moment set against surrounding tension A shift in register, usually by one speaker only
World signal Dialect, jargon, idiom, terms of address Tells the reader where and among whom they are without narration

Discussion

The most consequential and least discussed carrier is white space. Short dialogue turns break up the page and can create an impression of speed and immediacy. They can also make the passage feel more like scene in Genette’s sense — narrative time approximating story time, the closest fiction comes to isochrony. A writer can use dialogue as a pacing instrument independently of its content and ask whether a tonally heavy passage needs a change in rhythm.

Register as world signal is the texture domain’s largest resource and its most fraught. The register a community speaks in tells a reader where they are more efficiently than any description — the courtesies, the ellipses, the technical vocabulary handled without explanation, what is treated as unsayable. It is fraught because the most obvious way to signal register is orthographic — spelling speech as it sounds — and that technique has a difficult history, treated at §8.9 and §9.4.

The general principle comes first: dialect is carried by syntax, idiom and lexicon far more reliably than by spelling. A character who says I don’t have but the one has been placed regionally and socially without a single apostrophe of elision.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: register-neutral speech. Everyone speaks a mild contemporary standard, and the reader learns nothing about the world from how it talks. This is the ventriloquist exchange (§8.2) seen from the texture side.
  • Over: the dialect showcase, in which orthographic representation becomes so dense that reading slows to decipherment, and the technique starts to signal the writer’s ethnographic effort rather than the speaker’s speech.

Illustrations

1. Saramago, Blindness (1995; trans. Giovanni Pontiero) — texture produced by the removal of an apparatus. Running dialogue into unbroken paragraphs, with speaker changes marked only by capitalisation, produces a specific reading experience: speech becomes continuous with narration, individual voices become hard to separate, and the reader must actively work to attribute each turn. In a novel about the collapse of individual distinctness under catastrophe, the texture is the argument. §9.6 develops this.

2. Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847) — dialect as world signal and as barrier. Joseph’s speech is rendered in a dense Yorkshire orthography that many readers cannot parse without effort, and Emily Brontë’s editors have periodically softened it. The unreadability is not incidental: Joseph is a figure of obstruction, and the reader’s difficulty with him is the same difficulty every character has. This is the strongest available defence of the dialect showcase, and it is a defence available only where the opacity is doing work.

3. Hemingway, “Hills Like White Elephants” (1927) — texture as the whole of the technique. Short turns, minimal attribution, no interiority, and a rhythm of question and deflection produce a surface so uniform that the reader is forced to attend to the small deviations from it. The story is discussed at §9.8.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Silence Domain

This section extends the five-domain model.

The five domains above all describe things that speech does. But a great deal of what a dialogue exchange accomplishes is accomplished by speech that does not occur, and the five-domain model can only describe such effects as a technique within one of the other domains — evasion as character work, interruption as relational work, the unspeakable as theme.

This chapter proposes that the category is large enough, structurally distinct enough and consequential enough to be named.

The silence domain. The exchange does its work through what is not said: through refusals to answer, through interruption and its prevention, through pause and delay, through characters who are present and do not speak, and through characters who are excluded from the exchange altogether.

Carriers

Carrier Description Signal on the page
The unanswered question A first pair-part left without its second The next paragraph belongs to a different topic, or to an action
The pause A measured delay before a turn An action beat, a paragraph break, an explicit notation of silence
Interruption A turn terminated by another speaker The dash; the abandoned clause
The refusal An explicit refusal to speak I’m not going to answer that — the marked case, and the weakest
The silent participant A ratified participant present throughout who never speaks Their presence is stated at the start and not mentioned again
The unratified participant Someone who hears the exchange without being party to it The overhearer, the eavesdropper, the servant
The absent party Someone discussed and not present, or excluded from a conversation about them Third-person reference sustained across an exchange
The unspeakable A matter the exchange organises itself around and cannot approach Repeated approaches and swerves; circumlocution

The argument for the domain

Three arguments, in increasing order of strength.

1. Its effects are not reducible to the other domains. A character who is in the room and does not speak for eight pages of dialogue is producing an effect that is not characterisation (we learn nothing about them), not relation (they are transacting nothing), not plot and not theme. It is a structural effect: it makes the exchange a performance with an audience, and everything said in it is designed for that audience. Goffman’s participation framework (§3.3) names this, and no other domain has the vocabulary for it.

2. It has its own failure modes and its own remedies. The over-explaining exchange, in which every character says outright what they think, is a silence-domain failure: the diagnosis is not too much characterisation or too much plot but nothing is being withheld, and the remedy is specific. The silence test (§7.1, test 5) exists because the failure is not visible from within the other five.

3. It is where a great deal of the novel’s most serious content lives. What cannot be said in a given society, by a given person, to a given hearer is a description of that society’s structure. Morrison’s Beloved organises an entire novel around a matter its characters cannot approach directly. Ishiguro’s Stevens is constituted by the sentences he does not complete. The exclusion of women, servants and children from conversations that determine their lives is not a theme of the nineteenth-century novel; it is a dialogue structure the nineteenth-century novel repeatedly builds scenes out of. To treat these as instances of “character revealed through evasion” is to describe a cathedral as a load-bearing wall.

The argument against

It should be given its full force, because it is a good argument.

Silence is a technique, not a purpose. The five domains answer the question what value has changed? — a plot value, a character value, a relational value, a thematic value, a textural value. Silence answers a different question: by what means? And the means is available to all five. An unanswered question shifts a relational value; an interruption shifts a relational value; an excluded party is a fact about the plot or the world. On this account the proposed sixth domain is a category error: it belongs in §6 with the other mechanisms, alongside evasion and the changed subject, and elevating it to a domain confuses the taxonomy of ends with the taxonomy of means.

There is a second, subtler objection. A domain, in the model inherited from Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene), is a register in which something can change. It is not obvious what a silence-domain value shift would look like. If the answer is “what may now be said between these people has narrowed”, that is a relational value; if it is “the reader now knows what cannot be spoken of”, that is theme.

The chapter’s position

This chapter adopts the sixth domain for diagnostic purposes while conceding that the objection has not been answered. The pragmatic justification is that writers do not look for what is absent unless they are prompted to, and a taxonomy that files silence inside five domains of presence will not prompt them. The theoretical justification is weaker and rests on the participation framework: the composition of the room — who may speak, who may hear, who is excluded — is a fact about the exchange’s structure that the other five domains cannot express, whatever may be true of the other carriers.

Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class, and it is a real disagreement rather than a comprehension check.

Illustrations

1. Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847). Heathcliff leaves the kitchen before Catherine says I am Heathcliff. Nelly notices his departure and tells Catherine to hush, but withholds a clear account of what he heard. Two silences — one a departure, one a withholding — determine the novel’s entire subsequent plot. Neither is speech; both are dialogue.

2. Hurston, Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937). Janie’s exclusion from the store-porch talk is enforced by Jody, and her eventual entry into it — the moment she speaks in the store, in public, against him — is the novel’s central reversal. The exchanges Janie is not in are structurally necessary for the exchange she finally enters.

3. Hemingway, “Hills Like White Elephants” (1927). The story’s subject is never named by either speaker. The exchange is organised around a hole, and the reader’s construction of that hole is the entire reading experience.

4.8 Must all six be served at once?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

  1. One domain served fully outperforms five gestured at. The Hemingway story serves texture and silence at maximum and the other four thinly; it is a masterpiece. What the model catches is not the number of domains but the case where the honest answer is none.
  2. Exchanges have a primary domain, and it should be identifiable. In revision the useful question is not how many? but which one is this exchange for, and is that domain actually moving?
  3. Domains have a natural sequence across a novel. Early exchanges lean on character and texture, because the reader needs the voices before the contests. Late exchanges lean on relation and theme, because the accumulated history is what makes a small move legible. An exchange that serves the wrong domain for its position reads as misplaced even when it is well written — the dialogue equivalent of the register mismatch treated in Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel).
  4. The domains are lenses, not slots. A line that does three things does not do them separately. Jody Starks’s refusal on Janie’s behalf is one act, and calling it character, relational and silence work is three descriptions of it, not three components of it.
