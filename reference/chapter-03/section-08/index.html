Chapter 3 · The Dialogue Exchange

8. Failure Patterns

Continue in Core

Each pattern is presented with its symptom, its mechanism (what actually goes wrong, as distinct from what the surface looks like), its persistence (why competent writers keep producing it), its remedy, and — mandatorily — its exception: a published work that commits the error and succeeds.

The exceptions are the intellectual point of the section. A pedagogy of prohibitions produces writers who can avoid faults and cannot make choices. In every case the pattern fails because of a mechanism, not because of a surface feature, and where the mechanism is absent the surface feature is harmless.

8.1 The As-You-Know-Bob Exchange

Symptom. Characters tell each other things they would both already know, so that the reader can overhear them. As you know, Bob, our father left the shop to the three of us.

Mechanism. The line gives more information than the speaker has a reason to supply. With no motive available in the fiction, the reader notices the author’s need to convey the fact rather than inferring an intention on the character’s part. The illusion that the characters exist independently of the book collapses at that point, and it is expensive to rebuild.

Note that the failure is not informational. The information may be necessary and the reader may need it. What fails is the delivery mechanism: a briefing wearing dialogue’s clothes.

Persistence. Dialogue is the most vivid available channel, and writers reasonably prefer to deliver in it. The prohibition is also easy to evade at the surface without addressing the mechanism, by adding a plausible reason for the conversation. Plausibility is not the missing ingredient; cost is (§7.1, test 4).

Remedy. Three moves, in order of reliability. Give the fact to someone who does not want to say it. Give the need to someone who already suspects part of it, so that the exchange is a test rather than a transfer. Deliver the fact incompletely, so that what arrives is a fragment raising a further question. Where none of the three is available, the information belongs in narration, where no one has to pretend.

The exception. The naive listener convention, which the novel inherited from drama and has used continuously for three centuries. Wuthering Heights is a major case: Lockwood knows little of the household, has been ill, and asks; Nelly Dean knows much of its history and has a captive audience and her own reasons for shaping the account. Her account to Lockwood supplies much of the novel’s exposition, though Lockwood’s own narration supplies some too. Frankenstein also places extended accounts inside a frame of listeners and speakers. Dracula uses a different solution: an assembly of journals, letters and other documents.

The discrimination is exact. As-You-Know-Bob fails because the listener already knows. The naive listener does not, and the convention is not a loophole but the correct solution — with the standing cost that the writer must then make the listener interesting, which Emily Brontë does by making Lockwood a fool whose misreadings the reader can measure.

8.2 The Ventriloquist Exchange

Symptom. Every character reasons, jokes and phrases things the same way — the author’s way. The exchange passes the eye and fails the transcript test (§7.1, test 7) on every line.

Mechanism. The novel’s central resource, in Bakhtin’s account (§3.4), is heteroglossia: the staging of the actual stratification of a language into competing social discourses. A novel in which every character speaks one idiolect has not merely made its speakers hard to distinguish; it has collapsed the social world into a single position and forfeited what the form is for.

The practical mechanism is simpler. Writers hear dialogue in their own inner voice, and the inner voice has one vocabulary, one rhythm and one relation to a joke.

Persistence. It is invisible from inside, because each line individually sounds right — it sounds right to the person who thinks in that idiom. It also survives line-editing indefinitely, since the fault is not in any line.

Remedy. Structural, not lexical (see the warning at §7.2). Assign each principal a different average sentence length, a different relation to questions (who asks, who answers, who deflects), and a different default subtext mechanism from §6.4. Then run the transcript test again.

The exception, and it is a serious one. Ivy Compton-Burnett wrote twenty novels in which every character — parents, children, servants, tyrants and victims — speaks the same impossibly lucid, epigrammatic, syntactically formal English, at length, saying outright what they mean about matters no one would say aloud. The novels are almost entirely dialogue. They are not a failure of the technique; they are a different technique, in which the uniform register functions as a convention of the form, like verse in a Racine tragedy, and the differences between characters are carried by what they are willing to say rather than by how they say it. Within a page the reader accepts the convention and stops noticing it.

Wilde is the second case and the more familiar one. In The Importance of Being Earnest everyone is witty in the same way, and the uniformity is the joke: a society in which the capacity for epigram is universal and moral seriousness is not. The exception generalises to a principle: an undifferentiated register is a failure when it is unintended and a style when it is declared. The test is whether the reader is invited to notice.

8.3 The On-the-Nose Exchange

Symptom. Text and subtext say the same thing throughout. I’m angry that you lied to me. I know. I’m afraid of losing you. Emotionally legible, dramatically inert.

Mechanism. A line that states a character’s feeling directly can foreclose the exchange instead of opening it. There may be nowhere for the reader to look, nothing further to infer, and no pressure left for the next line to apply. A cooperative utterance can still generate an implicature; the problem here is a passage that supplies its own interpretation before the reader has had to make one (§6.2). Reader investment is often strengthened by reader work.

There is a second and less obvious cost, developed at §6.5: on-the-nose dialogue everywhere destroys the gap-closing move, because a book with no gap has no climactic plainness available to it.

Persistence. It is what characters would say if they were being helpful, and writers instinctively make characters helpful. It is also encouraged by a misreading of the instruction to write clearly: clarity is a property of the prose, not of the characters’ self-knowledge.

Remedy. §6.4, applied to the three most direct turns. Substitution testing rather than deletion: the feeling stays, the statement of it goes.

The exception, in two forms.

First, the climactic declaration. The gap-closing move (§6.5) is an on-the-nose line, and it is the strongest single move in the repertoire. Darcy’s second proposal states his position without qualification; Janie answers Jody in public in terms nobody could mistake. These are not exceptions to the rule so much as the reason for it: the rule exists to keep the move in reserve.

Second, and more radically, Dostoevsky. The characters of The Brothers Karamazov and Crime and Punishment say outright what they think, at enormous length, including things about themselves that no one in life would articulate. Ivan states his theological position in full. Marmeladov delivers his own moral autopsy to a stranger in a tavern. Raskolnikov confesses. There is very little subtext in the ordinary sense, and the novels are among the most psychologically penetrating ever written.

The reason needs stating with care, because it is the section’s most useful discrimination. Dostoevsky’s characters state their positions fully and are still not transparent, because what they say is not the truth about them: it is their most powerful self-account, offered in bad faith, under pressure, to a hearer they are trying to affect. The gap in Dostoevsky is not between what is said and what is meant. It is between what is said and what is the case. That is a different two-channel structure, it is at least as productive as the Chekhovian one, and a pedagogy that recognises only the second will misread half the European novel.

8.4 The Static-Status Exchange

Symptom. Competently voiced, well paced, and going nowhere: the same person holds the upper hand at the last line as at the first, and nothing was contested.

Mechanism. The relational domain (§4.3) is untouched. Because status is what readers track most continuously and least consciously, an exchange in which it does not move produces a specific sensation — the reader feels that the passage was pleasant and cannot say what happened.

Persistence. Status is invisible to writers in a way it is not to actors and directors, who are trained to play it. Nothing in the sentence-level craft of dialogue draws attention to it, and an exchange can be excellent line by line while failing entirely at this level.

Remedy. Identify the currency (§7.1, test 3) and make one unit of it change hands. Usually the cheapest available move is physical: someone sits, someone stands, someone leaves the doorway.

The exception. Beckett built a body of work on exchanges in which status is structurally incapable of moving, and the impossibility is the subject. So, in a quieter register, does the whole tradition of the ritual exchange — the liturgical, the ceremonial, the formulaic greeting — where fixity is the point and any movement would be a scandal. There is also a common and legitimate local use: an exchange in which status is contested hard and holds, which passes the test’s second question and is frequently more interesting than a reversal. The failure is not stasis; it is stasis that nobody tried to disturb.

8.5 The Tennis Match

Symptom. Rapid, clever back-and-forth with real energy at the sentence level and no throughline. Entertaining in isolation, forgettable a page later, because no domain in §4 actually moved.

Mechanism. Multiplicity is present at the level of wit and absent at the level of function. Each turn is doing one thing — being good — and the exchange’s shape is a flat oscillation rather than a trajectory. The characteristic signature is that the turns are interchangeable in order: if an exchange can be reshuffled without loss, nothing is accumulating.

Persistence. It is the most enjoyable dialogue to write, it demonstrates fluency, and it is what writers produce when the scene is going well and they do not want to stop.

Remedy. The reshuffle test — move three turns and see whether anything breaks. Then cut to the two turns that could not be moved and rebuild around them.

The exception. Wilde, again, and the whole tradition of the comedy of manners: exchanges whose sole business is display, in books whose subject is a society organised around display. Austen’s Netherfield conversations run on the same principle with a difference that repays study — Elizabeth and Darcy’s sparring is a tennis match, and it is also the mechanism by which two people who cannot court each other openly conduct a courtship. The tennis match becomes structural the moment the reader can see what the players are actually playing for.

8.6 The Maid-and-Butler Opening

Symptom. A novel or scene opens with two minor characters — servants, colleagues, guards — discussing the principals for the reader’s benefit. Nobody’s seen her since the funeral, and the brother’s been in and out of that shop every day.

Mechanism. A specialised As-You-Know-Bob with an additional fault: the reader’s first exposure to the world is mediated by people who do not matter. Per the rules of notice discussed in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), characters introduced early are read as significant, so the device makes a promise about two people it intends to discard.

Persistence. It is a genuine inheritance from the drama, where the convention is ancient and where the practical problem it solves — how to orient an audience with no narrator available — is real. Prose fiction has a narrator and does not have the problem.

Remedy. Ask what the servants know that the principals do not. If the answer is nothing, give the material to the narration. If the answer is something, the exchange is not a maid-and-butler opening at all; it is a scene about servants, and should be written as one.

The exception. The Portrait of a Lady opens with three men on a lawn at Gardencourt discussing, among other things, the American cousin who is about to arrive. It is, structurally, a maid-and-butler opening. It succeeds because the three men are not minor: Ralph Touchett, his father and Lord Warburton are principals, their conversation establishes the terms — money, illness, marriage, English and American manners — on which the whole novel will run, and Isabel’s arrival into a room that has been discussing her is the novel’s governing situation in miniature. The device fails when the speakers are instruments and succeeds when they are stakeholders.

The wider exception is the servants’ hall in the nineteenth-century novel generally, where servants discussing employers is not an exposition device but a representation of the actual information economy of a household — and where, as §4.6 notes, the servant’s position as unratified participant is a structural fact rather than a convenience.

8.7 The Name in Every Line

Symptom. Characters address each other by name in nearly every turn. I don’t know, Elena. Then find out, Dov.

Mechanism. Vocatives in real conversation are rare and marked: people use a name to summon attention, to claim intimacy, to warn, to reprimand, or to establish a relationship for a third party’s benefit. Because they are marked, each one is read as doing something, and an exchange in which every line is marked is an exchange in which nothing is. The device also solves a problem the writer has — telling the reader who is speaking — by giving a line to a character who has no reason to say it, which is the As-You-Know-Bob mechanism in miniature.

Persistence. It is the easiest available substitute for attribution, and it feels warmer than a tag.

Remedy. Delete all of them, then restore the two that were doing work. Solve the attribution problem with action beats (§5.3) instead.

The exception. The Remains of the Day, where Stevens addresses Miss Kenton as “Miss Kenton” with a frequency that in any other novel would be a fault, and where the frequency is the characterisation. The name is a barrier: it is deployed at the moments when a first name would be possible, and its repetition across decades is the record of a refusal. The device also functions in institutional and military registers, where terms of address are obligatory and their omission is the marked case — in a novel about a hierarchy, counting the vocatives is a way of reading the hierarchy.

8.8 The Attribution Fault

Symptom. Speech tags that describe the line instead of letting it perform: he expostulated; she hissed; he said angrily; she laughed, attached to a sentence one cannot laugh.

Mechanism. Two distinct faults. Redundancy: the tag states what the line has already achieved, so the reader is told a thing they have just experienced, which retroactively converts an experience into an assertion. Compensation: the tag supplies a tone the line does not carry, which means the line has not been written yet, and the adverb is a note to self left in the manuscript.

Persistence. Nineteenth-century models are full of expressive attribution (§3.5), and a reader who has absorbed Dickens by osmosis will produce it. It also feels like variety, and repetition of said feels like poverty until one notices that said is invisible.

Remedy. Test 9 (§7.1). Delete tags to the minimum required for tracking; convert survivors to said or to action beats that carry a second function.

The exception. The whole of the nineteenth century, which is a large exception. Dickens’s expressive attribution is part of a narrating voice that is continuously present, evaluative and comic; a narrator who is supposed to be invisible cannot afford rejoined, and a narrator who is a performer can afford anything. The rule is therefore conditional on a narrative stance, not universal: transparent narration requires transparent attribution; a foregrounded narrator may attribute as expressively as it likes. Contemporary novels with strongly voiced narrators — comic, retrospective, oral — recover the licence at once.

8.9 Phonetic Dialect

Symptom. Non-standard speech rendered by respelling: apostrophes for dropped consonants, eye-dialect (wuz, sez, woz) that records no actual difference of pronunciation, and dense orthographic representation sustained over pages.

This pattern requires more care than the others, because the prohibition against it has a real history of being applied unevenly, and a student is owed the history along with the advice.

Mechanism, stated technically. Three distinct problems, which are usually conflated.

  1. Processing cost. Respelled speech is read more slowly, because the reader must convert orthography to sound to sense rather than orthography to sense. Sustained across pages this converts reading into decipherment, and a reader working that hard is not absorbed.
  2. Eye-dialect can mark a speaker without marking a distinctive sound. Sez represents an ordinary pronunciation of says; wuz can represent a common reduced pronunciation of was. Neither spelling, by itself, establishes a distinctive accent. Used for one group while another retains standard spelling, such forms can make the marked speakers look less educated on the page.
  3. Asymmetry of marking. In a novel where one group’s speech is respelled and another’s is not, the unmarked group is thereby positioned as speaking language itself, and the marked group as speaking a departure from it. Every reader registers this, and it is a claim about who is central.

The uneven history. The third problem is where the prohibition acquires its ethical weight, and where its enforcement has been least even. English novels have for two centuries rendered working-class, regional, Black, Irish, Scots and colonised speech phonetically while rendering the speech of the educated and metropolitan in standard orthography — including in books whose narrators are not otherwise unsympathetic. The received rule against phonetic dialect grew, in part, out of a correct perception that this practice was doing social work. But the rule has often been applied so as to fall hardest on writers representing their own communities, while the historical canon that established the practice is taught without comment. A student should be able to hold two facts together: that the technique has been used condescendingly for a very long time, and that a blanket prohibition disproportionately restricts the writers with the strongest claim to use it.

Persistence. It is the most obvious way to signal register, and it feels like fidelity.

Remedy. In the general case: carry dialect in syntax, idiom and lexicon rather than in spelling. Word order, tense and aspect systems, characteristic idioms, forms of address and what a speaker treats as sayable will place a speaker socially and regionally with complete precision and no orthographic cost. A single light orthographic marker, used sparingly, can then signal that a fuller representation is being withheld rather than that no difference exists.

The exceptions, which are major.

Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937). Hurston renders Black southern speech densely and phonetically, and the novel is one of the great achievements of American prose. Three features distinguish it from the condescending practice. The speech is celebrated rather than exhibited: the store-porch talk is the novel’s centre of value, its speakers are its wits and philosophers, and the reader is positioned inside the community rather than visiting it. The narration meets the speech halfway: Hurston’s free indirect passages move between a lyrical standard and the characters’ idiom, so the marked/unmarked asymmetry described above is dissolved rather than enforced. And Hurston was a trained anthropologist recording a language she spoke, which is not an aesthetic argument but is relevant to what the representation is for.

It is worth teaching that the novel was attacked on this ground at the time — Richard Wright’s hostile review in New Masses (1937) accused it of catering to a white audience’s appetite for minstrel pleasure — and that the judgement has been comprehensively reversed. The reversal is itself instructive: a technique’s meaning is not fixed by its surface, and the same orthography can be a caricature or a claim depending on who holds the centre of value in the book.

Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884). Twain prefixed an explanatory note claiming to distinguish several dialects, rendered by ear rather than by guesswork. The vernacular is the novel’s narrating voice, not merely its dialogue, which removes the asymmetry problem at a stroke: there is no standard register in the book against which Huck’s is a departure. Jim’s speech, however, is marked relative to Huck’s, and the novel’s handling of that difference remains contested among critics — a live disagreement rather than a settled one, and one worth setting as a seminar question (§12.2).

Wuthering Heights (1847). Joseph’s Yorkshire is nearly impenetrable, and editors have periodically softened it. The difficulty is functional (§4.5): Joseph exists to obstruct, and the reader’s struggle with him replicates every character’s. This is the narrowest of the three defences and the most easily abused.

The generalisable rule. Phonetic dialect fails when it marks a speaker as a deviation from a standard the book itself embodies. It succeeds when the book’s centre of value sits inside the marked speech, when the narration does not stand above it, and when the representation is doing something a lexical and syntactic rendering could not.

8.10 The Therapeutic Exchange

Symptom. Characters articulating their own psychology in terms the book has supplied. I think I keep pushing you away because of what happened with our father.

Mechanism. A double failure. The character has been given the author’s analysis, which they have no way of possessing; and the analysis, once stated, is closed to the reader, who now has a conclusion instead of evidence. It is on-the-nose dialogue with an additional layer of anachronism, since the vocabulary of self-diagnosis is a specific and recent register that most characters, in most settings, do not have access to.

Persistence. Contemporary readers and writers live inside a therapeutic vocabulary, and it is how many people now speak. That makes the register plausible; it does not make it dramatic.

Remedy. Give the analysis to a different character, as an accusation, which the first can then refuse. A diagnosis offered by an enemy is contestable; the same diagnosis offered by its subject is a verdict.

The exception. Dostoevsky again, and this time more sharply. Raskolnikov’s confession to Sonya, Ivan’s account of his own position, Marmeladov’s tavern monologue: all are characters articulating their own psychology at length and with precision. They work because — as §8.3 argued — the self-analysis is itself an act with a motive. Raskolnikov confesses at Sonya, to make her carry it. The therapeutic exchange fails not because characters explain themselves but because, in the failing version, the explanation is disinterested. Self-analysis is dramatic when it is a move; it is inert when it is a report.

The second exception is the novel that takes psychoanalysis as its explicit subject or its explicit setting, where the vocabulary is the world’s rather than the author’s.

8.11 The Speech That Answers a Question Nobody Asked

Symptom. A character delivers a paragraph, or a page, in response to a prompt that did not call for it: a short question, a passing remark, or nothing at all.

Mechanism. The exchange has stopped being an exchange. The second speaker has become an audience, and the passage is a monologue with an interlocutor attached for decency’s sake — usually signalled by that interlocutor’s contributions dwindling to Go on and I don’t understand. The reader detects the shift immediately, because the conditional relevance of adjacency pairs (§3.2) has been suspended: the second speaker is no longer a person with obligations in the conversation.

Persistence. The writer has something to deliver and has decided which character will deliver it. The prompting question is then reverse-engineered, which is why prompting questions in drafts are so often flat.

Remedy. Interrupt it. Any of the three standard interventions works: let the listener object, let the listener leave, or let the listener ask the wrong question — the last being the most productive, since it forces the speaker to restate and reveals what they think the point was.

The exception. The set speech has a long and distinguished life in the novel, and §9.3 analyses the greatest instance: Ivan’s Grand Inquisitor, in which one character speaks for many pages and the other is entirely silent. It works — and this is the whole of the discrimination — because the silence of the listener is the exchange’s content. Alyosha’s not answering is what the passage is about, and his eventual response is an act rather than a rebuttal. Where a listener’s silence is meaningful the set speech is an exchange by other means; where it is merely a convenience, it is not.

The other standing exception is the sermon, the trial speech, the public address and the deathbed testament — set-piece forms with their own conventions, which the novel absorbs whole and which readers accept as generically licensed.

8.12 The One-Engined Exchange

Symptom. One character wants something and the other does not want anything. The exchange proceeds by question and answer until the first character has what they came for.

Mechanism. The definition at §2.1 requires a want on both sides. Where only one exists, the exchange has no resistance and therefore no shape: it terminates when the questioner runs out of questions rather than when something is decided. This is the structural fault underneath a great deal of dialogue that readers describe as “flat” without being able to say why, and it is distinct from static status — status may even move, in an interview, without the exchange acquiring an engine.

Persistence. It is the natural shape of a scene in which a protagonist needs to find something out, which is a very common scene.

Remedy. Give the answerer a want. It need not relate to the questioner’s: I want this over; I want to know why you are asking; I want you to think better of me than you do. A single sentence of motive on the answering side converts an interview into an exchange without altering the information that passes.

The exception. The detective interview, which is one-engined by generic design and which readers accept because the pleasure on offer is inferential rather than dramatic — the reader is working alongside the detective, and the suspect’s flatness is data. The deposition, the interrogation and the frame-narrative enquiry (Lockwood questioning Nelly) all work the same way. In each, the genre has supplied the second engine outside the exchange: the reader’s own want.
