Chapter 3 · The Dialogue Exchange

5. Multiplicity: The Economy of the Exchange

Continue in Core

5.1 The principle, stated as a constraint

Multiplicity — the practice of making a single unit of text discharge several narrative functions at once — is introduced in Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) as a general virtue and is reused, in some form, by every chapter of this book. In dialogue it takes a particular shape, because dialogue’s constraint is unlike any other unit’s.

The constraint is this. A line of dialogue has only one channel of production and two channels of meaning. Everything a novelist wants an exchange to accomplish must be routed through sentences that a specific person, with a specific history, in a specific mood, would plausibly utter aloud to a specific hearer for a reason of their own. Narration can address the reader directly; description can dwell; interiority can explain. Speech can do none of these without breaking. It must serve the book while appearing to serve only the speaker.

This is why the classic failure of drafted dialogue is single-domain service. Two characters trade the facts the reader needs, and nothing else is riding on the trade. The exchange is legible, the information arrives, and the passage is inert — because the only thing happening in it is a thing that happens for the reader’s benefit rather than for the characters’.

The fix is never to remove the information. It is to make the information cost something.

Give the fact to a character who does not want to say it. Give the need to a character who already suspects part of it and is testing rather than receiving. Now the same facts arrive, but a relational value has moved (§4.3), a character has been revealed by what the disclosure cost them (§4.2), and the reader has watched two people negotiate instead of being briefed by two mouthpieces. Nothing about the plot information has changed; everything about what the exchange is doing has.

Note the difference from the account of multiplicity given in Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages), which is arithmetical — too few words, too many obligations. Dialogue’s multiplicity is not arithmetical but motivational. The constraint is not space. It is that every function must be discharged by a sentence someone had a reason to say.

5.2 Anatomy of an exchange, line by line

A much-taught exchange in twentieth-century fiction comes near the opening of Hemingway’s “Hills Like White Elephants” (1927; a short story, used here because it isolates the variable more cleanly than any novel can). A man and a young woman wait at a Spanish railway station, drinking, and later discuss an operation without naming its nature — an abortion. Early on, looking at a line of hills across the valley, she says they look like white elephants; he says he has never seen one; she says no, he wouldn’t have; he objects that her saying so does not prove anything.

Four turns. Nothing has happened. The exchange is one of the most efficient in the language.

Turn Text Functions discharged
1 They look like white elephants Character (she makes images; she is the one still trying to play) · Relational (an offer — a bid for shared attention) · Texture (the landscape enters through her, not through narration) · Theme (a white elephant can be an unwanted, burdensome possession — a possible resonance for the situation, without either speaker glossing it)
2 I’ve never seen one Character (a literal man answering a figurative remark) · Relational (the bid declined; he answers the sentence rather than the invitation) · Silence (an adjacency pair completed in form and refused in substance)
3 No, you wouldn’t have Relational (retaliation; the status move of the exchange) · Character (she is quicker than he is, and knows it) · Plot (the first evidence that the pressure is on and the courtesies are going)
4 I might have. Just because you say I wouldn’t have doesn’t prove anything Character (defensiveness; a man arguing a point nobody made) · Relational (the escalation he cannot afford, because he needs something from her) · Theme (proof, evidence and reasonableness are about to be his entire rhetorical repertoire on the real subject)

Several things are worth extracting from the table.

Every turn serves at least three domains, and none announces any of them. The reader experiences four short remarks about scenery.

The thematic content is carried by an idiom neither speaker glosses. A white elephant is conventionally an unwanted or burdensome possession, often costly to keep and difficult to dispose of. Neither character says this; the phrase is doing it. This is multiplicity’s cheapest and highest-yielding move — see §5.3.

The relational contest is conducted entirely in the second channel. Turn 2 is a perfectly cooperative answer to the literal question and a flat refusal of the offer that was actually made. Grice supplies the mechanism (§3.1): the man observes the maxim of relation with respect to the words and flouts it with respect to the act, and the reader computes the implicature.

Nothing changes, and the exchange is not static. No fact passes; no decision is made. What moves is permission: after turn 3 the two of them are no longer able to talk about the hills. That is a relational value shift, and it is the only shift the passage needs.

5.3 Where multiplicity actually lives

Novice revision looks for multiplicity in the content of what characters say, on the assumption that a line does more work when it means more. In practice, dialogue’s multiplying work is done in four much humbler places.

1. The choice of noun and idiom. Not the hills look strange but white elephants. A specific noun or a live idiom carries thematic and social freight that a general one would have to state. The best dialogue lines in fiction are usually the most concrete, and their concreteness is the mechanism.

2. The form of address. Whether a character is called by first name, surname, title, diminutive, nothing at all, or something they have not been called before. This single variable carries relational value on every line where it appears, at a cost of one word, and most drafts leave it constant across a whole novel.

3. What the answer does to the question. Whether the second pair-part is delivered, delayed, partially delivered, over-delivered or refused. This is the conversation-analytic machinery of §3.2 used as a compositional resource, and it is where the silence and relational domains are almost always available for free.

4. The action beat placed between turns. A gesture inserted in place of an attribution — turning a key over between two fingers, refilling a glass, not looking up — discharges texture, relation and character in a clause, while performing the tag’s practical function of telling the reader who is speaking. The action beat is the most under-used instrument in draft dialogue, and it is the one that most reliably converts a one-domain exchange into a three-domain one.

Note what is absent from that list. Elaborate figurative language in the mouths of characters is the highest-risk instrument in dialogue, because a metaphor that misses draws attention to the author rather than to the speaker, and because most people do not talk in extended figures. Hemingway’s white elephant works because it is a dead idiom being used literally, which is the kind of figure real speakers produce.

5.4 The ceiling, and the limit case

Multiplicity has an upper bound in dialogue as everywhere else, and its symptoms are specific.

Symptom What the reader experiences
Every line is quotable The exchange reads as a script for an audition; nobody is talking, everybody is performing
No line is casual Without unstressed turns there is no rhythm, and without rhythm no emphasis
Both speakers are equally articulate Wit distributed evenly signals a single author rather than two people
The subtext is uniformly present If every line is oblique, obliquity stops signifying

The remedy is counter-intuitive and best stated as a rule: an exchange needs turns that are only doing one thing. Ritual openings, a genuine agreement, a question answered plainly. These function as the neutral ground against which a deviation registers — the point §3.1 makes about implicature requiring a norm, restated as a compositional instruction.

The limit case in the other direction is instructive. The exchange with the lowest multiplicity in serious fiction is often the most powerful: a single flat sentence, arriving after a hundred pages of obliquity, that does one thing only. §6.5 is about that move and why it can only be made once or twice in a book.

5.5 Exercise: the domain audit

A procedure, and one of the more reliable teaching instruments in this chapter.

  1. Print one exchange from the draft, double-spaced, with the attributions removed.
  2. In the margin beside each turn, write the letters of every domain it serves: P, C, R, Th, Tx, S (plot, character, relational, theme, texture, silence).
  3. Count.

Interpretation:

Pattern Diagnosis
Most turns carry P alone The exchange is a briefing. Find who does not want to speak, and give them the facts.
Most turns carry C alone Voices are being displayed rather than used. Nothing is being contested; check test 3.
R never appears Status is fixed. Either the exchange is over before it begins, or a shift is available and unwritten.
S never appears Everybody is saying everything they think. Run test 5 and §6.4.
Most turns carry four or more Over-pressurised. Find a turn to let breathe.
One turn carries most letters That is the exchange’s spine. Consider whether the surrounding turns are earning their place beside it.

The instruction to strip the attributions before beginning is not incidental. It converts the audit into the transcript test (§7.1, test 7) at the same time, and it prevents the writer from reading a domain into a line that the tag rather than the speech supplied.
