Chapter 6 · The Chapter Break

15. Further Reading

Continue in Core

Annotated and grouped by what each source is good for. Starred items are the recommended first three.

15.1 On the chapter itself

  • ★ Dames, Nicholas. The Chapter: A Segmented History from Antiquity to the Twenty-First Century (2023). A major book-length study of the subject and a foundation for this chapter. Read it for the argument that segmentation is a technology with a history, and for its attention to what happens to a reader in the interval between units. Its literary chapters — on Fielding, Sterne, Austen, Dickens, Eliot, Tolstoy and their twentieth-century successors — are also the best available anthology of chapter endings analysed as chapter endings. Not a craft book, and better for it.
  • Dames, Nicholas. “The Chapter: A History” (The New Yorker, 2014). The argument in a few thousand words. The right thing to assign undergraduates before the book.
  • Genette, Gérard. Paratexts (1997). The chapter on intertitles is short and indispensable. Read it with a contents page of Vanity Fair open beside it.

15.2 On serial publication and the commercial shaping of form

  • ★ Hughes, Linda K., and Michael Lund. The Victorian Serial (1991). The standard account, and the most directly useful book on this list for anyone publishing in instalments. Its central move — treating the interval between parts as part of the work rather than as an interruption of it — is the one modern craft writing has failed to absorb.
  • Butt, John, and Kathleen Tillotson. Dickens at Work (1957). Old, unsurpassed, and built on the number plans. Read the chapters on Dombey and Son and Bleak House for what a working novelist’s structural planning actually looked like.
  • Stone, Harry, ed. Dickens’ Working Notes for His Novels (1987). The documents themselves. Worth an hour with a facsimile page simply to see how little of it is prose and how much is scheduling.
  • Patten, Robert L. Charles Dickens and His Publishers (1978). The commercial history. Useful as a corrective to any account that treats the serial format as an artistic choice.

15.3 On closure and the shape of endings

  • ★ Smith, Barbara Herrnstein. Poetic Closure (1968). About poems, and the best available account of how endings work at any scale. The concepts of closural allusion and terminal modification are directly usable on chapter endings and are used throughout §4.
  • Kermode, Frank. The Sense of an Ending (1967). For the tick-tock, and for the argument that readers impose shape on duration. Short and permanently in print for a reason.
  • Brooks, Peter. Reading for the Plot (1984). For narrative desire and the dilatory space. The chapters on plot’s dynamics explain what a break is doing to a reader’s want better than any craft manual.
  • Torgovnick, Marianna. Closure in the Novel (1981). Chapter 16’s principal source, and worth reading alongside this chapter for the typology of closural strategies, which applies at chapter scale with almost no adjustment.

15.4 On what readers do at a boundary

  • Zacks, Jeffrey M., et al. “Event Perception: A Mind-Brain Perspective” (2007). The clearest statement of event segmentation theory. Technical, but the first ten pages are readable by anyone and change how you think about where to cut.
  • Zwaan, Rolf A., and Gabriel A. Radvansky. “Situation Models in Language Comprehension and Memory” (1998). The dimensions readers track, and the costs of shifting them. Used throughout Part II of this textbook.
  • Emmott, Catherine. Narrative Comprehension (1997). Contextual frames; the best account of what a reader releases at a break and has to rebuild afterwards.
  • On the Zeigarnik effect, read Zeigarnik’s 1927 paper in translation alongside Van Bergen’s Task Interruption (1968). The pairing is a good lesson in how a psychological finding becomes a craft slogan.

15.5 On counting

  • Moretti, Franco. Graphs, Maps, Trees (2005). The case for quantitative literary history, and the licence for §5.4. Short, argumentative, and worth reading even if the conclusions do not persuade.
  • The Stanford Literary Lab pamphlets. Freely available, uneven, and occasionally revelatory. A good model for the kind of small empirical study a student can run on chapter lengths in an afternoon.

15.6 Craft manuals, used critically

  • Swain, Dwight V. Techniques of the Selling Writer (1965) and Bickham, Jack M. Scene & Structure (1993). The source of the six-beat model on which the placement taxonomy at §6.4 depends. Mechanistic and dated in idiom; structurally sound, and the only craft writing that treats the relation between scene shape and cut placement seriously.
  • Forster, E. M. Aspects of the Novel (1927). For pattern and rhythm — the closest the older criticism comes to what §5 calls proportion.
  • Yorke, John. Into the Woods (2013). For act structure and its relation to segmentation, from a television perspective; useful for §10.5.

15.7 Within this textbook

  • Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) — the value shift, and the principle of multiplicity applied to the final sentence.
  • Chapter 2 (Scene and Sequel) — the six beats that the placement taxonomy at §6.4 uses as coordinates. Read §6.4 with Chapter 2 open.
  • Chapter 5 (The Transition) — how the gap is crossed. This chapter’s test 8 hands over to it directly, and its §6.6 tabulates the typographic marks.
  • Chapter 13 (The Viewpoint Shift) — placement strategy 11 considered as a focalisation decision rather than as a cut.
  • Chapter 14 (The Subplot) — the alternation of threads across breaks, and the attention tax that alternation spends.
  • Chapter 9 (The Opening Pages) — the first chapter break is where the opening’s contracts get their first test.
  • Chapter 10 (The Prologue) — the prologue as a segmentation decision.
  • Chapter 16 (The Ending) — which inherits proposition and payoff, and for which every chapter ending in the book has been a rehearsal.
