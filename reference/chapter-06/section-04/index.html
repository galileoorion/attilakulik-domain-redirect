Chapter 6 · The Chapter Break

4. The Ending Types

Continue in Core

4.0 The model and its status

The taxonomy below names six recognisable ways of closing a chapter. The first five provide the baseline model used here; the sixth is proposed here.

Its value is selective. It converts the undifferentiated instruction end the chapter well into a choice among options with known costs, and it makes the commonest chapter fault — reaching for the same close every time — visible as a decision rather than invisible as a reflex.

Four cautions about its status.

  • The categories describe last sentences, not chapters. The same chapter can be given any of the six endings by changing its final paragraph, and one of the more useful exercises in §12.1 requires that.
  • They are not ranked. The cliffhanger is not more sophisticated than the soft landing. It is more expensive, which is a different property, and there are places in every novel where expense is the wrong choice.
  • They compound. A great many strong endings are two types at once — a pivot that is also an image, a question that is also a recasting close. §4.8 treats compounding.
  • They are misused as a checklist of things to include. The taxonomy answers which one, here, never how many across the book. The answer to the second question is a distribution, and §5 and §7 handle it.

Each type is set out with: what it is; why it matters; its textual carriers — the specific places in the prose where the effect actually lives; discussion; characteristic under-delivery and over-delivery; and illustrations from published fiction.

4.1 The Cliffhanger

What it is. An ending that leaves a danger, a crisis or a physical situation visibly unresolved, so that the reader stops — if they stop — holding an open threat.

Why it matters. It is the most expensive exit available, and one way to turn a chapter break into a page turn. It is also the type with the shortest half-life, the strongest historical baggage (§3.3) and the least tolerance for repetition.

Textual carriers

Carrier How it works Example of the move
The interrupted action The cut falls between a movement and its completion A hand reaching the handle; a shot fired and no one told whom it hit
The revealed threat A danger becomes visible in the final clause and is not addressed The figure at the end of the corridor, named and left standing there
The withheld object of a verb The sentence completes grammatically but not informationally She turned, and saw what was in the doorway.
The final short sentence after a long one Rhythm supplies the sense of a drop Herrnstein Smith’s terminal modification, used to create a gap rather than to close one
The unanswered address Someone speaks; the chapter ends before the reply Common in Dumas; the cheapest form and the most easily exhausted

Discussion

The cliffhanger is the type most damaged by being taught. Its mechanism is simple enough to imitate and its effect is immediate enough to feel like success, so drafts acquire them in bulk. Three things are worth knowing before using one.

It exploits investment rather than creating it. §3.6 sets out the evidence: an interrupted narrative stays more available in memory, but availability is not desire. A cliffhanger placed over a situation the reader has no stake in produces nothing at all — the reader notices the technique and puts the book down anyway. This is why cliffhangers are late-book devices in well-built novels and early-book devices in weak ones.

Delivery changes the interval. A monthly number ending could be followed by a month of waiting; a reader of a complete novel can continue immediately. That difference is worth considering when revising a cliffhanger, though it does not establish how emphatic a modern ending should be.

The interruption must be legible as interruption. A reader who cannot tell whether the chapter has ended in mid-event or has simply stopped will read the ending as a fault. The signal is usually rhythmic: the final sentence is short, the paragraph is short, and the preceding sentence has established a trajectory the reader can complete for themselves.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the manufactured cliffhanger. The danger is real for the space of the last sentence and disappears in the first paragraph of the next chapter, because it was never part of the story (§8.8). The reader learns, quickly, to discount all future endings.
  • Over: the interrupted sentence. Cutting so early that the reader does not know what the threat is. An unresolved danger requires a named danger; a chapter that ends on “and then she saw—” has offered nothing to be anxious about.

Illustrations

1. Dumas, The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46; trans. Robin Buss, 1996). The purest available case, because the format is visible in the prose. Dumas’s chapters are short and end, with great regularity, on a turn: an identity half-revealed, a door opened, a line of dialogue whose implication is left to detonate in the reader’s own head. Read as a novel, the effect is relentless and, past a few hundred pages, mechanical. Read as a newspaper serial, the work also had instalment breaks, sometimes inside chapters, and several long pauses in publication. The lesson is that chapter endings and newspaper instalment endings must be distinguished before making claims about their frequency or effect.

2. Dickens, Great Expectations (1860–61). Dickens’s weekly parts required a strong close roughly every seven or eight thousand words, and the endings around Magwitch’s return are the best available demonstration of a cliffhanger built on accumulated investment rather than on novelty. The threat at the end of Chapter 38 is not an event but a foreboding, and it works because two hundred pages have established what Pip has to lose.

3. Collins, The Woman in White (1859–60). Worth one paragraph as the period’s most self-conscious practitioner. Collins serialised weekly in All the Year Round alongside Dickens, and constructed the novel as a sequence of testimonies each of which breaks off at the limit of its witness’s knowledge — so that the cliffhanger is generated by the form rather than applied to it. The maxim usually attributed to Charles Reade, and sometimes to Collins — make them laugh, make them cry, make them wait — describes the commercial logic.

4.2 The Soft Landing

What it is. An ending on a settled, resolved beat: the chapter’s movement completes, the tension discharges, and stopping is free.

Why it matters. It is the type that makes the others work. Price is relative (§3.7); a book with no free exits has no expensive ones. It is also, and less obviously, the type that produces reader trust — a novel that regularly lets the reader out is a novel that is not anxious about being left, and readers detect the difference.

Textual carriers

Carrier How it works
Completion of the value shift The chapter’s own change finishes on the page; nothing is pending
Return to stillness The last image is static: a room after, a landscape, a person alone
Narratorial summary or comment The narrator lifts out of the scene and says something general — a formal signal of closure
Closural allusion (Herrnstein Smith) Terminal content: night, sleep, departure, a door closing, a season ending
Rhythmic settling A final sentence that falls rather than lifts; falling cadence; a full stop where a comma was possible

Discussion

The soft landing is under-taught because it is hard to make interesting in the abstract and easy to write badly. Two distinctions rescue it.

Resolved is not the same as inert. A soft landing discharges this chapter’s tension; it does not discharge the book’s. A chapter can close a domestic argument completely and leave the novel’s central question untouched and gaining. The reader is released from the immediate and retains the general, which is the state in which they pick the book up again tomorrow because they want to, rather than because they were left dangling.

The free exit is a signal. Where soft landings fall in a book tells the reader about its architecture. Placed at the end of a movement, a phase, or a Part, they say: a stage has completed. Placed at random they say nothing, and the book’s shape becomes illegible. This is one of the strongest arguments for treating chapter endings as a system rather than one at a time (§7.1, test 10).

It is the correct default. Most chapters in most good novels end softly, and a manuscript in which they do not is nearly always mis-tuned rather than exciting. The nineteenth-century novel, with its explicit narratorial closes, is the extreme case; but even a contemporary literary novel typically runs three or four settled endings for each unsettled one.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the anticlimactic soft landing (§8.4). The chapter should have held its tension and instead released it, discharging pressure the book needed. The characteristic symptom is a final paragraph that answers a question the reader wanted to keep.
  • Over: the valedictory close. Full closural apparatus — reflection, generalisation, cadence — applied to an ordinary chapter, so that every unit sounds like the end of the book (§3.5). Fourteen of these and the last page has nothing left to use.

Illustrations

1. Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813). Austen’s chapter endings are a masterclass in the cheap exit, and their characteristic move is to close on a settled social fact delivered with irony — a visit concluded, an opinion formed, a party dispersed. The reader is released completely at the level of event and retains the whole of the novel’s question, which is not an event but a judgement. Austen almost never ends a chapter on danger, and the novel is not less compelling for it.

2. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958). The chapters close, very often, on the completion of a communal movement: a ceremony finished, a season turned, a story told and its telling acknowledged. The effect is to give the novel the rhythm of a repeating year, which is the exact formal precondition for the third part’s rupture to register. The soft landing here is doing thematic work: a world in which things conclude properly is being established so that its ending can be felt as an ending.

3. Eliot, Middlemarch (1871–72). Eliot’s characteristic close lifts out of the scene into the narrator’s general reflection. It is an eighteenth-century inheritance, and it makes stopping not merely free but satisfying — the reader leaves each chapter holding an idea rather than a question. Note the trade: this is a novel almost nobody reads compulsively, and one a great many people finish.

4.3 The Pivot

What it is. An ending on a reveal or a turn — a fact placed at the chapter’s close that sends the reader forward with new urgency, or back to reconsider what they have just read.

Why it matters. It is the type that most efficiently uses the structural leverage of the final position. Rabinowitz’s rules of privileged position (Chapter 9 §3.2) apply at the chapter scale: material placed at a chapter’s end is read as significant automatically, without the writer spending a word to make it so. A reveal placed mid-chapter forfeits that free emphasis, which is the failure at §8.5.

Textual carriers

Carrier How it works
The withheld fact, released last Information available to the viewpoint character or narrator throughout, delivered in the final clause
The recontextualising detail A physical detail that changes the meaning of the scene: a name on a letter, a hand, a smell
The arrival of a person Someone enters in the last line whose presence rewrites the situation
The unmasking An identity resolved
The narratorial disclosure The narrator, not the character, supplies the fact — a device requiring an established narratorial presence

Discussion

The pivot is the type most often botched by placement rather than by execution. Writers discover the turn while drafting, write it where it occurs to them, and leave it there. A productive revision move is to ask, of every chapter containing a reveal: could this reveal be the last thing in the chapter? — and, if the answer is no, to ask what the chapter is doing with the pages that follow it.

Two refinements.

Forward pivots and backward pivots are different instruments. A pivot that sends the reader forward (“he was already in the house”) behaves like a cliffhanger with information in it. A pivot that sends them back (“the letter was in her own hand”) asks them to re-read the chapter mentally rather than to turn the page — and is, arguably, a different type altogether. That argument is the subject of §4.6.

The pivot needs a chapter behind it. A turn only turns if there was a direction. Chapters that pivot without having established a proposition (§6.3) produce a reveal the reader cannot place: the information arrives with nothing to revise.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the buried pivot (§8.5). The turn is present but not final, and the chapter’s leverage is spent on whatever follows it.
  • Over: the pivot per chapter. Every chapter ends on a reveal, so the novel’s information is delivered in a series of identical detonations and the reader stops trusting any of them. It also produces a specific structural problem: a book that reveals at every close has no room for accumulation, because nothing is ever allowed to stay unknown long enough to be wondered about.

Illustrations

1. Thackeray, Vanity Fair (1847–48), Chapter 32. The definitive case, and one of the great chapter endings in English. Thackeray declines to narrate the Battle of Waterloo. The chapter follows the civilians in Brussels, the panic, Jos Sedley’s flight, the sound of the guns — and closes with darkness coming down on the field and the city, and Amelia praying for George, who is lying on his face, dead, with a bullet through his heart. The pivot is delivered in a subordinate clause, at the end of a sentence, at the end of a chapter, at the end of a monthly number. Everything the chapter has withheld arrives in its final clause, and the battle the novel refused to describe is present entirely in its result. Note what makes it a pivot rather than a cliffhanger: nothing is left open. The reader is not made to wonder; they are made to reinterpret. Amelia’s prayer, in the same sentence, becomes futile retrospectively.

2. Dickens, Bleak House (1853), Chapter 59. Esther’s narrative closes on the discovery, at the burial-ground gate, of the woman she has been pursuing: And it was my mother, cold and dead. Eight words, seven of them monosyllables, after a chapter of increasingly frantic movement. The pivot is total — it resolves the pursuit, ends the mother, and reframes the whole of the search that preceded it — and the rhythmic contraction does the work that a page of reflection would have spoiled.

3. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958), Chapter 7. The chapter narrates the killing of Ikemefuna and gives Okonkwo’s motive immediately after the act: he fears being thought weak. It does not end there. Its final paragraphs turn to Nwoye, whose reaction recalls his distress on hearing abandoned twins in the forest. The motive is a turn within the chapter; the ending widens its consequence to the son. Chapter 1 of this textbook analyses the killing as a scene; the boundary here performs different work.

4.4 The Echo Close

What it is. An ending on a resonant image, line or gesture that carries emotional or thematic charge rather than plot information. The pull forward, where there is one, is curiosity or feeling rather than threat.

Why it matters. It is the type that lets a novel end a chapter without either resolving or teasing — the third option that most drafts do not know they have. It is also the principal instrument by which chapter breaks do thematic work, which is to say: by which a book’s argument becomes audible at its structural joints.

Textual carriers

Carrier How it works
The object held last A physical thing given the final position and no commentary
The repeated image A motif returning at a break, so the break becomes part of the motif’s rhythm
The gesture without gloss A character does something small and the narration declines to interpret it
The widened frame The narration pulls back from the scene to the weather, the street, the country
The line of dialogue left standing Speech given no reply, no tag and no response

Discussion

The echo close is where the principle of multiplicity from Chapter 1 (The Purposes of a Scene) does its most concentrated work, because the final position confers significance for free. A detail that would be ordinary in the middle of a chapter becomes a statement at its end. This is a resource and a hazard in equal measure: you cannot put an incidental image at a chapter’s close, because there are no incidental images at a chapter’s close.

The type’s characteristic difficulty is that its effect is not verifiable from inside the writing. A resonant image is resonant only if the reader has the material to resonate it against, and the writer always has that material. The stopped-reader test at §7.1 (test 4) exists for this reason.

A distinction worth holding: the echo close is not the same as the decorative close. The decorative close ends on an image because the writer wanted a nice sentence there. The echo close ends on an image because the image is the chapter’s argument in compressed form, and would be the right last line even if the prose were plain.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the mood ending. Atmosphere without a referent. The reader was following a plot movement and receives weather instead, and reads the chapter as having trailed off.
  • Over: the significant object. An image so evidently loaded that the reader can see the author placing it — the chapter-scale version of the precious match-cut described in Chapter 5 (The Transition) §8.4. The symptom is a final image that has no business in the scene except to be final.

Illustrations

1. Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), Chapter 1. The chapter ends with Nick watching Gatsby stretch his arms towards the water, and then finding, when he looks, nothing but a single green light, minute and far away. Nothing is explained; no question is posed in words; the plot does not advance. What the reader carries out of the chapter is an unexplained gesture and a light — and the novel spends two hundred pages making that image mean something. This is the echo close in its strongest form: an image planted at a break and redeemed at the end of the book.

2. Dickens, Bleak House (1853), Chapter 47. The death of Jo the crossing-sweeper closes with the narrator abandoning the scene entirely and addressing, in turn, the Queen, the lords and gentlemen, the Reverends of every order, and the reader — Dead… And dying thus around us every day. The chapter does not end on an image but on an accusation, and the reader is expelled from the fiction into their own society. It is an echo close whose echo is moral, and it demonstrates the type’s real range: what is carried across the white space need not be a feeling about a character.

3. Woolf, To the Lighthouse (1927). Woolf’s numbered sections routinely close on an image withdrawn from any character’s use — a lighthouse beam, a shawl, a wave — and the effect is to make the novel’s segmentation feel like breathing rather than like division. Chapter 5 (The Transition) reads “Time Passes” as an ellipsis; the observation here is about the close of the smaller units, which is what makes the larger break survivable.

4.5 The Question Close

What it is. An ending that raises a question — explicitly or by implication — without the physical danger a cliffhanger implies. The engine is what does this mean? rather than is she safe?

Why it matters. It is the cliffhanger’s quieter and more durable relative, and it is the correct instrument for most of the pull-forward work in literary, mystery and domestic fiction. It also fatigues far more slowly, because a question does not depend on escalation.

Sternberg’s distinction (Chapter 5 §3.4) is the theoretical basis: suspense is uncertainty about the future, curiosity is uncertainty about the past. The cliffhanger is a suspense instrument; the question close is usually a curiosity instrument, and curiosity has a longer shelf life because it accumulates rather than resetting.

Textual carriers

Carrier How it works
The anomaly A fact that does not fit is stated flatly and not pursued
The unanswered question in dialogue Someone asks; the chapter ends before the answer
The character’s own question, unvoiced Free indirect style ending on the character’s puzzlement
The withheld reaction A person receives news and the narration cuts before their response
The document, half-read A letter, a ledger, an inscription given in part

Discussion

The failure mode is specificity. A question close only works if the reader can state the question. Vague unease is not a question; a reader who finishes a chapter feeling that something is off, but unable to say what, has been given nothing to carry. The test is mechanical and is a form of test 4 in §7.1: write the question the chapter has left, in one sentence, as a reader would phrase it. If it takes two sentences or contains the word “somehow”, the close is not doing what it appears to be doing.

The second refinement concerns ownership. A question may belong to the reader, to the viewpoint character, or to both. The strongest question closes give the reader a question the character does not yet have — which is dramatic irony operating at the structural level — or give the character a question the reader has already answered, which produces the pleasurable impatience that drives most detective fiction.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the vague close. Atmosphere presented as enigma. The reader cannot formulate the question and therefore does not have one.
  • Over: the interrogative. The chapter ends on a literal question, italicised, addressed to no one: But who had put it there? The device is not forbidden — it is standard in a good deal of commercial fiction — but it converts an implicit engine into an explicit one, and readers who notice the machinery discount it.

Illustrations

1. Kafka, The Trial (1925; trans. Breon Mitchell, 1998). Kafka’s chapters characteristically close on a procedural question that the novel will not answer: an official’s remark whose implication cannot be pinned down, an assurance that resolves nothing. The type is being used against itself — the reader accumulates questions that never mature into answers — and the accumulation is the novel’s subject. A question close that is never paid off is a broken promise once and a formal principle thirty times.

2. Ishiguro, The Remains of the Day (1989). The novel’s day-sections end, repeatedly, on a question Stevens has declined to ask himself: a memory approached and set aside, a phrase whose implication he does not follow. The reader is left holding the question the narrator has just put down. This is the question close used for characterisation rather than for plot, and it is the type’s most sophisticated available application.

3. Dickens, Bleak House (1853). The Tulkinghorn and Dedlock chapters run on a single, statable question — what does Lady Dedlock’s face mean when she sees the handwriting? — and Dickens closes chapter after chapter without answering it while adding to what the question covers. The technique is worth studying because the question never changes and never goes stale: what changes is how much depends on it.

4.6 A Proposed Sixth: The Recasting Close

This section proposes an addition to the taxonomy; Chapter 16 (The Ending) adopts or rejects it at the scale of a book.

The five types share an assumption: that a chapter ending’s business is with what comes next. The cliffhanger, the pivot and the question close all point forward; the soft landing declines to point; the echo close points sideways, into theme. None of them describes an ending whose whole work is directed backwards.

But such endings exist, they are common in good novels, and they behave differently enough to deserve a name.

The recasting close. An ending whose final sentence changes the meaning of the chapter just concluded, so that the reader’s first movement is to look back rather than to turn on. What it alters is not the situation but the interpretation of what has already been read.

The distinction from the pivot

The pivot and the recasting close are easily confused and are separated by a single question: does the new information change what happens next, or what just happened?

Pivot Recasting close
Direction of force Forward Backward
What changes The situation The interpretation
Reader’s first movement Turn the page Re-read, or sit with it
Price of stopping High — something is pending Ambiguous — nothing is pending, but the reader is not finished
Effect on the following chapter It must resume the new situation It is free; the chapter that follows can go anywhere
Typical carrier A fact, an arrival, an identity A motive, a self-description, an admission, a re-naming

The last row of that table is the practical payoff, and it is why the distinction earns its keep. A recasting close leaves the writer free at the next break in a way a pivot does not. A pivot obliges the following chapter to engage the new situation; a recasting close obliges nothing, because nothing has moved. This makes the recasting close the correct instrument at the points where a novel needs to change thread, jump time, or hand over viewpoint — which is why it appears so often immediately before a Part boundary.

Textual carriers

Discussion

The recasting close’s characteristic pleasure is the small internal jolt of revision — the reader, having finished a chapter, discovers that they must revise the chapter rather than continue. Menakhem Perry’s account of the primacy effect (Chapter 9 §3.1) explains why the position matters: a frame once formed is resistant to revision, and the chapter boundary is the moment at which the frame has just been completed and is therefore most vulnerable. Placed one paragraph earlier, the same sentence would be absorbed into an ongoing scene and lose most of its force.

Its characteristic difficulty is that it is nearly invisible to the writer, for whom the reinterpretation was never necessary — the writer always knew the motive. This is the same asymmetry as §1.1, in a sharper form. The only reliable test is to read the chapter, cover the last sentence, ask what the chapter was about, then uncover it and ask again. If the two answers are the same, the sentence is not a recasting close.

Under- and over-delivery

  • Under: the confirmation. The final sentence states what the chapter already showed, so the reader is told rather than turned. The ending of Achebe’s Chapter 7 works because Nwoye’s final reaction links Ikemefuna’s killing to an earlier wound; a version that had already made that link would merely repeat it.
  • Over: the twist ending, chapter-sized. A reinterpretation so total that the chapter is retrospectively voided rather than deepened. If the reader concludes that the chapter they just read did not happen, or did not mean anything until the last line told them what it meant, they have been played with rather than shown something.

Illustrations

1. Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby (1925), Chapter 3. Nick has spent a chapter at Gatsby’s party, and closes it by asserting that he is one of the few honest people he has ever known. The line does not advance anything. It reaches back across the whole chapter — across the gossip, the borrowed judgements, the elisions about Jordan — and puts the reader’s own reading of it in question. Whether Nick is honest is the interpretative problem of the novel, and the recasting close is where it is installed.

2. Achebe, Things Fall Apart (1958), Chapter 7. The chapter ends with Nwoye remembering the inward shock he felt when he heard abandoned twins crying in the forest. That final connection recasts Ikemefuna’s death as part of Nwoye’s growing estrangement from his father and his community. It is an example of a recasting close produced by a delayed consequence, rather than by Okonkwo’s earlier statement of motive.

3. Ford, The Good Soldier (1915). Ford’s narrator repeatedly closes a movement with a sentence that revises the reliability of the movement itself — an admission that he did not understand, or was not present, or has told it out of order. The novel is a sustained demonstration of the type, and it shows the recasting close’s structural consequence clearly: because nothing is pending, Dowell is free to begin the next section anywhere in time at all.

The argument against a sixth type

Reasonable disagreement is possible, and students should be brought into it rather than told the answer. The case against admitting the recasting close runs as follows.

  1. It is a pivot facing backwards, and the pivot category already accommodates direction. The definition of the pivot in §4.3 includes sending the reader “back to reconsider what they just read”. Splitting the category makes the taxonomy less compact without adding a distinct remedy — and a taxonomy whose categories share a remedy is over-specified.
  2. Its effects are not distinguishable in practice. Readers do not, on the whole, report a categorical difference between a chapter that ends on a revelation about the future and one that ends on a revelation about the past. Both feel like turns.
  3. The distinguishing criterion is unstable. Achebe’s final turn to Nwoye both reinterprets the killing and prepares his later estrangement. Most strong endings do both, which suggests the axis is a continuum rather than a boundary.

The case in favour, restated against those objections: the categories in this taxonomy exist to make choices visible, and the recasting close names a choice that the five-type model cannot express — the decision to end a chapter with no forward obligation at all, so that the next chapter is free. That is a distinct craft decision with distinct consequences for the architecture of the following pages, and it is invisible under the pivot label.

This chapter adopts the sixth for diagnostic purposes while conceding that the five-type model captures most revision problems, and that the third objection is unanswered. Chapter 16 (The Ending) was to adopt or reject it at the scale of a book’s last line; its ruling (Chapter 16, §4.9) is that the category stops discriminating there, since nothing comes next and every close looks back, so the recasting close remains a type of chapter ending and the question how far a close may recast a whole book passes to Chapter 15 (The Reveal). Chapter 15 answers it with the teller rule: a close may reinterpret whatever the double reading reaches, and may falsify only passages that a teller in the book could have shaped, provided the reveal names the teller and the book gives a reason; a close that falsifies what the narration vouched for on its own authority voids the book as the over-delivered recasting close voids its chapter. Seminar question 2 (§12.2) puts the disagreement to the class.

4.8 Must a chapter ending be only one type?

No, and the model is misused when read that way. Four qualifications.

  1. Compounding is normal and usually good. Thackeray’s Chapter 32 is a pivot, an echo close and a recasting close simultaneously; Dickens’s Chapter 59 is a pivot with the rhythm of a soft landing. The types are named so that a writer can ask which of these am I doing? — and a good answer is often two of them, and here is which.
  2. Some compounds are contradictory. A soft landing plus a cliffhanger is not a rich ending; it is a chapter that resolves and then re-opens, and readers experience it as an author who could not decide. Where a chapter does need to close one thing and open another, the reliable structure is to close in the penultimate paragraph and open in the last, with a visible seam, rather than to blend them.
  3. The types describe the ending, not the chapter’s worth. A chapter with a weak ending may be the best chapter in the book, and the remedy is a new final paragraph rather than a rebuilt chapter. This needs saying because the diagnostic protocol in §7 can otherwise be read as an indictment of the material.
  4. The distribution matters more than any single instance. A novel’s ending types form a sequence, and the sequence is legible to readers even though almost none of them could describe it. §5 and §7.1 (test 7) treat the sequence as the object.
